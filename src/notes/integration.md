## Integrated treatment for co-occurring disorders (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Screening and Treatment of Co-Occurring Disorders](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/serious-mental-illness/co-occurring-disorders)
- Many adults have a mental illness and a substance use disorder (a co-occurring disorder); integrated care is recommended
- Integrating screening and treatment for mental and substance use disorders leads to better quality of care and health outcomes by treating the whole person
- People with co-occurring disorders are more likely to be hospitalized than people with a mental or substance use disorder alone
- **Integrated treatment:** Coordinates mental and substance use interventions by linking people to providers who deliver individualized services for the physical and emotional aspects of both disorders
- The three models for delivering care for co-occurring disorders: **coordinated, co-located, and fully integrated**
- Integrated treatment outcomes: reduced or discontinued substance use, improved psychiatric symptoms and functioning, better chance of recovery for both disorders, improved quality of life, decreased hospitalization, reduced medication interactions, increased housing stability, and fewer arrests
- **"No wrong door" policy (SAMHSA):** Effective systems must ensure a person needing treatment is identified, assessed, and treated, directly or through referral, no matter where they seek services
- Under "no wrong door," people seeking treatment for a mental disorder should be routinely screened for substance use disorders, and people seeking SUD treatment should be screened for mental disorders
- The **Center of Excellence for Integrated Health Solutions** advances evidence-based treatment for people with co-occurring physical and mental health conditions (including SUD) and supports integrating primary and behavioral health care
- **CONSTACS:** National peer-run centers that train organizations to integrate peers and recovery into behavioral health services for people with SMI

## Integrating SUD care into primary care (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Integrating SUD Services into Primary and Other Care Settings](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/integrating-sud-services)
- People living with SUD report a greater willingness to enter treatment in a primary care setting than in a specialty care setting
- People with SUD often use the health care system for other reasons: acute problems (chronic illness, injury, overdose) and chronic conditions such as HIV/AIDS, heart disease, or depression
- **Office-based opioid treatment (OBOT):** Effective for a substantial proportion of patients (50–80 percent), who are typically highly satisfied with the services
- 2024 NSDUH: about 1 in 6 Americans 12 and older had an SUD in the past year; an estimated 4.8 million people had an opioid use disorder
- Of the 4.8 million people with an OUD, only 17 percent (818,000) received medications for their OUD in the past year
- MOUD (methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone) is one component of an evidence-based care plan that also includes counseling, behavioral therapies, and medical and social services
- Buprenorphine and naltrexone can be prescribed in a variety of care settings, while methadone is available only through opioid treatment programs (OTPs); naltrexone is also approved to treat alcohol use disorder
- The 2023 elimination of the DATA Waiver (X-Waiver) lets practitioners, and pharmacists in some states, prescribe buprenorphine in many care settings
- Benefits of MOUD: reduced cravings, relapse risk, illicit opioid use, withdrawal symptoms, and overdose death risk; increased treatment retention; better outcomes; lower health care usage and costs
- **Low-barrier model of care:** SUD treatment that removes barriers, making services readily available and easily accessible in a nonjudgmental, welcoming, and accepting environment
- SAMHSA's 2024 **Core Curriculum Integration in Healthcare Profession Education** trains students early about SUD to reduce stigma and teaches that SUD is a treatable disease, similar to diabetes and heart disease
- **Practitioner Champion Initiative:** Highlights practitioners who have integrated MOUD into their practices to help others overcome barriers such as stigma
- Practitioners can call SAMHSA's Provider Support Team at 1-866-287-2728

## Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: CCBHCs](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/certified-community-behavioral-health-clinics) · [History and Background of CCBHCs](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/certified-community-behavioral-health-clinics/history-background)
- **Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic (CCBHC):** A clinic model designed to ensure access to coordinated comprehensive behavioral health care; CCBHCs must serve anyone who requests care for mental health or substance use, regardless of ability to pay, place of residence, or age
- The CCBHC model requires crisis services available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week; comprehensive services so people don't have to piece together care across multiple providers; and care coordination across behavioral health, physical health, social services, and other systems
- **The 9 required CCBHC services:** crisis services; outpatient mental health and substance use services; person- and family-centered treatment planning; community-based mental health care for veterans; peer family support and counselor services; targeted care management; outpatient primary care screening and monitoring; psychiatric rehabilitation services; and screening, diagnosis, and risk assessment
- CCBHCs are funded through the Section 223 CCBHC Medicaid Demonstration, SAMHSA's CCBHC Expansion (CCBHC-E) Grants, or independent state programs
- Federal partners: SAMHSA (planning grants, certification criteria, quality measures, expansion grants), the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS; the Prospective Payment System), and the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE; independent national evaluation and reports to Congress)

| Year | CCBHC milestone |
|---|---|
| 2014 | Congress passes the Protecting Access to Medicare Act (PAMA); Section 223 establishes the CCBHC Medicaid Demonstration |
| 2015 | SAMHSA and CMS release the CCBHC Certification Criteria; planning grants go to 24 states |
| 2016 | Eight demonstration states selected: Minnesota, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, Oklahoma, Oregon, and Pennsylvania |
| 2017 | The eight states launch their demonstrations; ASPE begins its evaluation |
| 2018 | $100 million creates the CCBHC Expansion Grant Program; the first 64 grants are awarded |
| 2019 | First federal evaluation; Missouri gets the first CCBHC Medicaid State Plan Amendment |
| 2020 | Demonstration expands to Michigan and Kentucky |
| 2022 | The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act adds up to 10 states to the demonstration every two years |
| 2023 | SAMHSA updates the CCBHC Certification Criteria |
| 2024 | Ten more states join; the Consolidated Appropriations Act creates a permanent state option for CCBHC services in Medicaid |

- Since 2017, CCBHCs have transformed care in hundreds of communities across the United States

## The Sequential Intercept Model (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: The Sequential Intercept Model](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/criminal-juvenile-justice/sequential-intercept-model)
- **Sequential Intercept Model (SIM):** Details how people with mental and substance use disorders come into contact with and move through the criminal justice system; helps communities find resources and gaps at each intercept and divert people into treatment
- SIM mapping workshops are available through SAMHSA's **GAINS Center**
- SAMHSA promotes early intervention and treatment as healthier alternatives to detaining people with behavioral health conditions in the justice system

| Intercept | Stage | Diversion opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Community services | Divert people into local crisis care without requiring a 911 call; connect them with treatment instead of arrest |
| 1 | Law enforcement | Police and emergency responders divert people to treatment instead of arrest or booking into jail |
| 2 | Initial court hearings / initial detention | Jail clinicians, social workers, or court officials divert people during intake, booking, or the initial hearing |
| 3 | Jails / courts | Diversion through jail or court programs after booking; services that keep illness from worsening during a stay |
| 4 | Reentry | Supported reentry after jail or prison through reentry coordinators, peer support staff, or community in-reach |
| 5 | Community corrections | Community-based supervision with added supports to prevent violations that lead to another jail or prison stay |

## Communities with access challenges (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Communities](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities) · [Homelessness Programs](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/homelessness-programs-resources) · [Rural Behavioral Health](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/rural-behavioral-health)
- For many populations with access challenges, coordinated behavioral health care plus social services, housing, education, and employment opportunities is needed to support wellness and recovery
- Stable housing is a critical component of recovery; about 30% of people experiencing chronic homelessness have a serious mental illness, and around two-thirds have a primary substance use disorder or other chronic health condition (Office of National Drug Control Policy)
- **PATH (Projects for Assistance in Transition from Homelessness):** A CMHS program that funds services for people with serious mental illness experiencing homelessness in all states and territories
- Other homelessness programs: Treatment for Individuals Experiencing Homelessness (TIEH), the Homeless and Housing Resource Center (HHRC), and CSAT's Grants for the Benefit of Homeless Individuals (GBHI)
- Rural barriers: fewer practitioners and hospitals, greater distances, limited broadband internet, and limited housing, education, employment, and transportation
- SAMHSA has funded SBIRT grants since 2003; most clinic sites were in rural communities of fewer than 2,000 people (the New Mexico SBIRT project screened over 50,000 adults)
- **Building Communities of Recovery (BCOR):** Programs administered and implemented by people with lived experience in recovery from SUDs that mobilize community resources for long-term recovery support
- **Technology Transfer Centers (TTC):** Three SAMHSA-funded networks: Addiction (ATTC), Mental Health (MHTTC), and Prevention (PTTC) Technology Transfer Centers
- Faith and community leaders are often the first point of contact when people face mental health problems or traumatic events; many turn to trusted leaders before a mental health professional

## Mental illness, justice and rural access (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Mental Health By the Numbers](https://www.nami.org/mental-health-by-the-numbers/)
- NAMI's message is that people with mental illness deserve help, not handcuffs, yet they are overrepresented in jails and prisons
- About 2 million times each year, people with serious mental illness are booked into jails
- About 2 in 5 incarcerated people have a history of mental illness (37% in state and federal prisons, 44% in local jails)
- Suicide is the leading cause of death for people held in local jails
- 70% of youth in the juvenile justice system have a diagnosable mental health condition
- About 3 in 5 people (63%) with a history of mental illness get no mental health treatment in state and federal prisons
- People with health coverage when released from incarceration are more likely to use services that reduce recidivism
- Rural Americans must travel twice as far to the nearest hospital and are twice as likely to lack broadband internet for telehealth; more than 25 million live in a Mental Health Professional Shortage Area
- During the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, 1 in 5 U.S. adults reported a significant negative impact on their mental health, and 26.3 million received virtual mental health services
