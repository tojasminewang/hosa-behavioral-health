## What recovery is (SAMHSA)
- **Recovery (SAMHSA definition):** A process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and strive to reach their full potential
- Recovery signals a dramatic shift in the expectation for positive outcomes for people with mental and substance use conditions or both
- SAMHSA's stated priority: build resilience and move "from chemical coping to human connection," from a pill culture toward a purpose culture
- 50.2 million American adults consider themselves to be in recovery from substance use and/or mental health problems: 2 in 3 adults who ever had a mental health problem and 7 in 10 adults who ever had a substance use problem
- Studies show most people with mental health problems get better, and many recover completely

## Guiding principles of recovery (SAMHSA)
- **Hope**, the belief that these challenges and conditions can be overcome, is the foundation of recovery
- Recovery is built on a person's strengths, talents, coping abilities, resources, and inherent values
- Recovery is holistic, addresses the whole person and their community, and is supported by peers, friends, and family members
- Recovery is highly personal and occurs via **many pathways**: clinical treatment, medications, faith-based approaches, peer support, family support, self-care, and other approaches
- Recovery is characterized by continual growth and improvement in health and wellness and managing setbacks; because setbacks are a natural part of life, **resilience** is a key component of recovery

## The four major dimensions of recovery (SAMHSA)
| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Health | Overcoming or managing one's disease(s) or symptoms (e.g., abstaining from alcohol, illicit drugs, and non-prescribed medication if one has an addiction) and making informed, healthy choices that support physical and emotional well-being |
| Home | Having a stable and safe place to live |
| Purpose | Conducting meaningful daily activities (a job, school, volunteerism, family caretaking, or creative endeavors) and having the independence, income, and resources to participate in society |
| Community | Having relationships and social networks that provide support, friendship, love, and hope |

## Peer support workers (SAMHSA)
- **Peer support workers:** People who have been successful in the recovery process who help others experiencing similar situations; also called people with lived experience
- Through shared understanding, respect, and mutual empowerment, peer support workers help people become and stay engaged in recovery and reduce the likelihood of relapse
- Peer support services extend the reach of treatment beyond the clinical setting into everyday life
- Peer support activities: advocating for people in recovery, sharing resources and building skills, building community and relationships, leading recovery groups, and mentoring and setting goals
- Peer roles can also include providing training, supervising other peers, developing resources, administering programs, and educating the public and policymakers
- The shared experience of recovery (or of being a family member) is the foundation of the peer recovery support relationship
- **Core competencies:** The capacity to easily perform a role; clusters of knowledge, skills, and attitudes; SAMHSA's Core Competencies for Peer Workers in Behavioral Health Services guide training, certification standards, and job descriptions (not barriers to entering the workforce)

![Peer support: people with lived experience of recovery helping others](peer-support)

| Principle of peer core competencies | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Recovery-oriented | Peers hold out hope, build on strengths, and recognize multiple pathways to recovery |
| Person-centered | Services are always directed by the person and aligned with their hopes, goals, and preferences |
| Voluntary | Peers are partners or consultants; participation is always the person's choice |
| Relationship-focused | The respectful, trusting, empathetic, collaborative, mutual relationship is the foundation |
| Trauma-informed | A strength-based framework emphasizing physical, psychological, and emotional safety and rebuilding control and empowerment |

## Supporting recovery (SAMHSA)
- **RecoverMe campaign:** Connects people in recovery with empowering, practical resources (Spanish: Voy a Recuperarme)
- Recovery support services include recovery housing, clubhouses, Oxford House, peer support, and supportive employment
- A written **recovery plan** identifies wellness goals, what you can do to reach them, daily and long-term activities, tracking of your condition, and triggers and how to manage them; it can be developed with family and supporters
- Managing life with a serious mental illness: with early and consistent treatment, people with SMI can manage their conditions, overcome challenges, and lead meaningful, productive lives
- Treatment and recovery plans often include support groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, NAMI groups, and SMART Recovery
- **National Recovery Month** (September) celebrates individuals and families in recovery

## NAMI programs and support groups
@ [NAMI: Programs](https://www.nami.org/programs/) · [Support Groups](https://www.nami.org/support-groups/) · [Supporting Recovery](https://www.nami.org/family-members-and-caregivers/supporting-recovery/)
- NAMI programs are peer-led by trained volunteers with lived experience and offer free education, skills training and support
- **NAMI Family-to-Family:** A class for families, significant others and friends of people with mental health conditions; designated an evidence-based program by SAMHSA
- **NAMI Peer-to-Peer:** A class for adults with mental health conditions that encourages growth, healing and recovery
- **NAMI Basics OnDemand:** An online class for parents, caregivers and other family to learn about mental health conditions
- **NAMI Homefront (class):** A class for families, caregivers and friends of military service members and veterans with mental health conditions
- **NAMI Provider:** A class for mental health professionals that builds compassion for individuals and families and promotes a collaborative model of care
- **NAMI Ending the Silence:** A presentation for middle and high school students, school staff and parents about the signs of mental health conditions and recognizing early warning signs
- **NAMI In Our Own Voice:** A presentation for the general public that promotes awareness of mental health conditions and recovery
- **NAMI Family & Friends:** A 4-hour seminar for people with loved ones who have a mental health condition
- **NAMI Sharing Your Story with Law Enforcement:** Trains peers and families to share their stories during law enforcement trainings such as CIT training
- NAMI Sharing Hope (for Black/African ancestry communities) and NAMI Compartiendo Esperanza (for Hispanic/Latin American communities) are free mental wellness programs
- **NAMI FaithNet:** An interfaith network that encourages faith communities to welcome and support people and families living with mental illness
- **NAMI On Campus:** Student-led, student-run mental health clubs for colleges and high schools
- NAMI Smarts for Advocacy trains people to turn lived experience into grassroots advocacy, and FLARE by NAMI is a mental health literacy curriculum for middle school students
- **NAMI Connection:** A peer-led support group for people with mental health conditions (in Spanish, NAMI Conexión)
- **NAMI Family Support Group:** A peer-led support group for family members, significant others and friends of people with mental health conditions
- NAMI support groups meet weekly, every other week or monthly, and many are virtual and open to everyone
- Supporting a family member's recovery: support is not control (accept their right to make treatment decisions), remember the illness influences behavior, maintain your own mental health, listen for the emotion behind hurtful words, resume normal routines, don't push too hard, prepare a crisis plan, and don't give up
- Providers cannot talk to family members without the patient's permission, so ask your family member to arrange it
