## Symptoms of schizophrenia (textbook) {pp. 561–562}
- **Schizophrenia:** A disorder characterized by hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thought and speech, disorders of movement, restricted affect, and avolition or asociality
- Schizophrenia affects approximately 1% of the human population worldwide; it distorts perception, cognition, movement, and emotion
- DSM-5 places it in the group "schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders"; delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, and movement disorders together indicate **psychosis**
- **Negative symptoms:** Behaviors seen in healthy people but missing in patients, including "diminished emotional expression and avolition"
- **Avolition:** A lack of volition, or goal-oriented behavior ("a" means "lack of")
- **Delusion:** A false, illogical (unrealistic) belief; types include persecution (paranoia), unrealistic power or importance (grandiosity), and beliefs that others control one's behavior (control)
- **Hallucination:** A false perception; 64% to 80% of hallucinations in schizophrenia are auditory (hearing voices, often accusatory or unpleasant)
- Auditory hallucinations are real sensations correlated with increased activity in the primary auditory cortex of the temporal lobe, different from simply imagining sounds
- Disorganized speech: jumping from topic to topic; difficulty inhibiting secondary word meanings ("jam" as food or music)
- **Loosening of associations:** Odd connections among ideas, possibly due to reduced latent inhibition
- **Catatonia:** Maintaining awkward or unusual body positions for hours at a time
- John Nash, diagnosed with schizophrenia, shared the 1994 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (the film A Beautiful Mind)

## Causes of schizophrenia (textbook) {pp. 562–566}
- No single factor alone is sufficient to produce schizophrenia
- Genetics: if one identical twin has schizophrenia, the other has nearly a 50% lifetime risk; genes overlap with those involved in bipolar disorder
- Dysfunctional eye movements, also found in healthy relatives, are a possible genetic marker
- **Enlarged ventricles** are a reliable correlate, suggesting loss of neural tissue (neural degeneration)
- Patients often show lower frontal lobe activity, at rest and during tasks, possibly explaining emotional disturbances and social withdrawal
- Teens with schizophrenia experience gray matter loss "like a forest fire"; cortical thinning is characteristic at all ages
- **Dopamine hypothesis:** Abnormal dopamine activity may be the major biochemical culprit; amphetamine and L-dopa (which boost dopamine) can cause hallucinations and paranoid delusions, while dopamine-blocking medications reduce them
- Limits of the dopamine hypothesis: about one-fourth of patients do not respond to dopamine-reducing drugs, negative symptoms are not improved, other neurotransmitters matter, and PCP ("angel dust") causes a schizophrenia-like syndrome by acting on glutamate
- Stress: schizophrenia is nearly five times more frequent in lower socioeconomic groups (stress of poverty vs. "drift" into lower-paying jobs)
- African immigrants in London neighborhoods with the fewest minorities were twice as likely to be diagnosed, suggesting the stress of minority status and isolation
- Prenatal exposure to viral illness is an environmental risk
- Cannabis: a 25-year study found cannabis use nearly doubled the risk; daily use tripled the risk of psychosis, and daily high-potency use raised it about five times; heavy adolescent use reduces frontal white matter
- Adolescent nicotine use doubles the risk of developing schizophrenia; tobacco use is very common among people with schizophrenia
- People with schizophrenia show less latent inhibition, are more likely to be left-handed or have ambiguous handedness, and have trouble distinguishing facial expressions

## Treating schizophrenia (textbook) {pp. 623–624}
- Before the 1950s, treatments included restraint in lukewarm baths, insulin shock therapy (daily comas), and frontal lobotomy
- French surgeon Henri Laborit discovered the calming effects of phenothiazines; chlorpromazine (Thorazine) began a new era of treatment
- U.S. institutionalized populations dropped by about 50% between 1955 and 2000, largely due to antipsychotic medication
- Phenothiazines ("typical" or first-generation antipsychotics) are dopamine antagonists that block dopamine receptors; high doses cause Parkinson-like tremors
- Nearly one-quarter of patients do not respond to phenothiazines, and they work poorly on negative symptoms
- "Atypical" or second-generation antipsychotics such as clozapine (Clozaril) affect dopamine and other neurotransmitters such as serotonin and work better on negative symptoms, but can cause diabetes, weight gain, low white blood cell counts, and seizures
- **Tardive dyskinesia:** A movement syndrome from antipsychotic medication, with tremors and involuntary movements of the face and tongue, often permanent
- **Psychosocial rehabilitation:** Meaningful work, social skills training, education about schizophrenia and medication, affordable housing linked to services, and symptom management; WHO found better recovery when patients stayed home and did meaningful work

## Schizophrenia (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Schizophrenia](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/schizophrenia)
- Schizophrenia is a serious brain disorder that causes people to interpret reality abnormally; they don't know which sights, sounds, and experiences are real
- It usually involves delusions (false beliefs), hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that don't exist), unusual physical behavior, and disorganized thinking and speech; paranoid thoughts and hearing voices are common
- No one is sure what causes schizophrenia, but genetic makeup and brain chemistry likely play a role
- Schizophrenia usually starts between ages 16 and 30: in men symptoms usually start in the late teens and early 20s; in women in the mid-20s to early 30s
- People may not seek treatment because they deny being sick, are ashamed of being labeled with a serious mental illness, or do not realize they have symptoms
- People with schizophrenia require lifelong treatment; the earlier treatment begins, the better the chances for recovery
- Medication: antipsychotics help control symptoms; it can take several weeks to notice improvement; ECT may be an option for people who do not respond to medication
- Therapy: individual talk therapy helps normalize thought patterns, notice early warning signs of relapse, and handle stress; family therapy supports families
- Daily habits: stay focused on goals, stick to the treatment plan (medication calendar or pillbox), know warning signs, take care of physical health, use relaxation (meditation, tai chi), join a support group, educate yourself, and ask about social services (housing, jobs, transportation)

## Schizophrenia (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Schizophrenia](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/schizophrenia/)
- NAMI describes schizophrenia as a serious, often long-term illness that interferes with thinking clearly, managing emotions, making decisions and relating to others
- Older estimates for schizophrenia and related psychosis ranged from 0.25% to 0.64% of U.S. adults; a newer estimate for schizophrenia spectrum conditions is 1.2% of U.S. adults
- Average age of onset is the late teens to early 20s for men and the late 20s to early 30s for women; it is uncommon to be diagnosed younger than 12 or older than 40
- Having a close relative (parent or sibling) with schizophrenia makes it more than 6 times more likely, but most people with an affected first-degree relative never develop it
- Environmental risks include exposure to viruses or malnutrition before birth, especially in the first and second trimesters
- **Epigenetics:** The process by which environmental stressors activate genetic risks
- Problems with the neurotransmitters dopamine and glutamate may contribute to schizophrenia
- Marijuana raises the risk of psychotic episodes; the younger a person is and the more often they use it, the greater the risk
- **Prodromal period:** The early stage of schizophrenia in young people, with nonspecific signs such as a change of friends, a drop in grades, sleep problems, irritability, isolation and unusual thoughts
- **Positive symptoms:** Experiences added that are generally absent, such as hallucinations and delusions
- **Negative symptoms:** Reductions in typical feelings and behaviors, such as being emotionally flat, speaking in a dull way and having trouble starting activities; they can be confused with clinical depression
- **Delusions (NAMI):** False beliefs that do not change even when the person is presented with new ideas or facts
- Cognitive symptoms include trouble remembering, organizing thoughts and completing tasks, and many people also have anosognosia (lack of insight into their illness)
- Schizophrenia diagnosis: 2 or more core symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech, disorganized or catatonic behavior, negative symptoms) for a significant part of a 1-month period, with reduced functioning lasting at least 6 months
- Brain tumors, thyroid problems and drug use must be ruled out because they can cause schizophrenia-like symptoms; there is no single lab test
- A correct diagnosis comes from the "movie" of a person's life rather than a "snapshot" or single interview
- Black and Latino individuals are more likely to be misdiagnosed with schizoaffective or other psychotic disorders, partly because of provider bias and limited culturally responsive care
- **AMP-SCZ (Accelerated Medicines Partnership for Schizophrenia):** An international research effort, which NAMI joined with the Foundation for the NIH, to find biomarkers that identify people at risk of psychosis
- There is no cure for schizophrenia, but it is treated with antipsychotic medications, psychotherapy and psychosocial interventions, and self-management and education
- First-generation (typical) antipsychotics such as haloperidol (Haldol) and chlorpromazine (Thorazine) can cause movement side effects like muscle stiffness or tardive dyskinesia
- Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics such as risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole and quetiapine are less likely to cause movement problems but may increase weight gain, metabolic changes and diabetes
- **Long-acting injectables (LAIs):** Antipsychotic shots given every few weeks or months that keep medication levels steady and lower the risk of relapse
- **Clozapine (Clozaril):** The only FDA-approved medication for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and the only one approved to reduce suicidality in schizophrenia; it needs blood monitoring and is underused
  - Treatment-resistant schizophrenia means no response to at least 2 different antipsychotics
  - In 2025, after NAMI advocacy, the FDA removed the REMS paperwork process that had been a barrier to clozapine
- **CBTp (CBT for psychosis):** Therapy that helps people reframe distressing beliefs and cope with voices and suspicious thoughts, especially symptoms that persist despite medication
- **Cognitive enhancement therapy (CET):** Computer-based exercises to improve attention, memory and social cognition in schizophrenia
- **Assertive Community Treatment (ACT):** A model that provides highly individualized, comprehensive services directly to people with serious mental illness, prevents crises and ensures medications are taken
- **Individual Placement and Support (IPS):** An evidence-based supported employment approach that helps people with mental illness find jobs that match their strengths and gives ongoing support at work
- People with schizophrenia have higher rates of diabetes, heart problems, lung disease and smoking; NAMI's Hearts+Minds helps reduce the risk of early heart disease, and GLP-1 medications may help with antipsychotic weight gain
- **Motivational interviewing:** A research-backed approach, first developed for substance use disorder, that helps someone who is struggling to accept help; family members can use its skills too
- Supporting a loved one with schizophrenia: respond calmly (hallucinations seem real to them, so explain that you see things differently), help them take medications as prescribed, and help them avoid drugs and alcohol
- **Catatonia:** A neuropsychiatric condition with abnormal movements, behaviors and speech that can be associated with psychosis

## Psychosis (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Psychosis](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/psychosis/)
- **Psychosis (NAMI):** Disruptions to thoughts and perceptions that make it hard to tell what is real and what is not; often described as a break with reality
- Psychosis is a symptom, not a specific illness; about 15–100 people out of 100,000 develop psychosis each year
- **First-episode psychosis (FEP):** When a person first shows signs of losing contact with reality; "first episode" does not mean more episodes will follow, but it does mean acting quickly
- Teens and young adults are at increased risk of psychosis, but it can occur at any age
- Contributors to psychosis: genetics, trauma (such as a death, war or sexual assault), substance use (marijuana, LSD, amphetamines), physical illness or injury (brain injury, tumors, strokes, HIV, Parkinson's, Alzheimer's), and mental health conditions
- Psychosis rarely appears suddenly; early warning signs include a drop in grades or job performance, trouble thinking clearly, suspiciousness, declining self-care, spending much more time alone, and strong inappropriate emotions or no feelings at all
- Families are often the first to notice early signs of psychosis
- **Hallucinations (NAMI):** Seeing, hearing or feeling things that are not there, such as hearing voices (auditory hallucinations)
- Delusions in psychosis include believing outside forces control one's thoughts, that trivial events have special personal meaning, or that one has special powers or a special mission
- Diagnosing psychosis includes medical and family history, a physical exam, and possibly lab tests or brain imaging to rule out infection, epilepsy, brain tumors or head injury; substance use can mimic schizophrenia
- **Coordinated Specialty Care (CSC):** A team-based treatment for early psychosis with case management, family support and education, psychotherapy, medication management, supported education and employment, and peer support; almost 400 U.S. programs exist
- SAMHSA's Early Serious Mental Illness (ESMI) Treatment Locator and the EPINET clinic map help families find CSC programs
- Early treatment gives the best hope of recovery by slowing, stopping or reversing the effects of psychosis

## Schizoaffective disorder (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Schizoaffective Disorder](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/schizoaffective-disorder/)
- **Schizoaffective disorder:** Symptoms of schizophrenia (such as hallucinations or delusions) plus symptoms of a mood disorder (mania or depression)
- Many people with schizoaffective disorder are first misdiagnosed with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia; it is less studied, so many of its treatments were developed for those conditions
- Schizoaffective disorder has a lifetime prevalence of 0.3%; men and women are affected at the same rate, but men often develop it earlier
- The two major types of schizoaffective disorder are the depressive type and the bipolar type
- Diagnosis: a major mood episode at the same time as schizophrenia symptoms, plus delusions or hallucinations for 2 or more weeks without a mood episode, with mood symptoms present for most of the illness
- There are no biomarkers to confirm schizoaffective disorder, and symptoms cannot be due to substance use or another medical condition
- Causes: genetics, brain chemistry and structure, stress (a death, divorce or job loss) and drug use, including hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin), regular marijuana use in adolescence, and stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine)
- **Paliperidone (Invega):** The only FDA-approved medication specifically for schizoaffective disorder
- Antipsychotics, antidepressants (for the depressive type) and mood stabilizers (for the bipolar type) are also used for schizoaffective disorder
- CBT with added cognitive therapy for psychosis (CBTp) helps with persistent symptoms that do not respond to medicine; studies show CBT changes brain activity
- ECT (done under anesthesia, using small electric currents to trigger a brief, controlled seizure) may be considered when medication and therapy do not work
