## Managing stress {pp. 656–658}
- We adapt to stress over time, and people report less stress as they get older even though stressors become more complex
- After identifying a stressor, a second set of appraisals guides coping: What harm has occurred? What threats remain? What resources are needed?
- Start by asking whether a stressor can be eliminated (reduce workload, better time management, visit the financial aid office); the worst approach is to withdraw and avoid stressors
- A sense of control is a major predictor of stress response; nursing home residents given more choice over daily activities live longer
- People with an **internal locus of control** (believing outcomes come from their own efforts) are less affected by stressors
- Patients who educate themselves about their condition and participate in treatment decisions experience less stress
- **Tragic optimism (Viktor Frankl):** Instead of asking why disaster happened, ask "How can I face this disaster with courage and responsibility?"
- Keeping healthy (eating, exercise, sleep) helps; regular aerobic exercise reduced stress, and none of the high-stress cardiac patients with the greatest fitness gains died in follow-up (vs. 19% with low gains)
- **Mindfulness:** The trained awareness of current thoughts, emotions, and actions; an effective way to reduce stress; meditation reduces psychological stress
- **Social support** is one of the most powerful antidotes to stress; after 9/11 nearly 100% shared thoughts and feelings with others, over 90% prayed, and nearly 40% donated money or blood
- Socially disconnected neighborhoods (more common as income drops) increase the risk of MDD
- Pets help: stockbrokers with high blood pressure who got a shelter cat or dog had smaller blood pressure increases under stress; robotic pets (PARO seal) reduced anxiety in people with dementia
- Religious belief helps people withstand unemployment, low income, and widowhood; service attendance was associated with lower inflammation, and belief in God predicts higher life satisfaction and lower suicide rates across nations

## Three types of coping {pp. 658–659}
| Type of coping | Goal | Positive example | Negative example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem-focused coping | Address the problem head-on by finding solutions | Meet the professor, hire a tutor, join a study group after flunking an exam | Escape and avoidance (skipping class) |
| Emotion-focused coping | Deal with the negative emotions caused by the stressor | Talk with friends or family, take a break for a movie or jog, visit the counseling center | Overeating or using alcohol to dull stress |
| Relationship-focused coping | Maintain and protect social relationships during stress | Empathy, support, compromise | Confronting, ignoring, blaming, withdrawal |

- **Problem-focused coping:** A response to stress designed to address specific problems by finding solutions; used more for work-related stressors (including schoolwork)
- **Emotion-focused coping:** A response to stress that targets the negative emotions arising from the situation
- **Relationship-focused coping:** A response to stress designed to maintain and protect social relationships
- People high in neuroticism experience more distress, escape work stressors, and confront in interpersonal stress; they show more empathy with distant relationships than close ones

## Resilience and hardiness {pp. 659–660}
- **Resilience:** The ability to adapt to life's challenges in positive ways; resilient people still feel sadness but stay optimistic and confident they can cope
- People low in resilience feel overwhelmed, helpless, and victimized and are more vulnerable to negative coping such as alcoholism and drug abuse
- **Hardiness:** A personality trait that contributes to resilience, combining commitment (seeking involvement), control (belief in influencing events), and challenge (seeing change as an opportunity for growth)
- Hardiness was not related to college grades but was linked to fewer health complaints and less academic stress
- Other protective factors: cognitive skills, social skills, flexibility, and generally positive mood
- **Logotherapy:** Viktor Frankl's approach (from logos, "meaning"), developed from his experiences in the Auschwitz concentration camp
- Resilience is the most common response to trauma: about two-thirds of those affected show little disruption; at least half of SARS survivors in Hong Kong were resilient
- Ways to promote resilience during a pandemic: build social support, stay informed without overdoing media, use distraction (fun and laughter), and reduce isolation with online communication
- Family resilience: cohesion, adaptability, communication, and financial management; resilient communities have higher social cohesion, lower crime, and less disaster exposure

## Positive psychology {pp. 672–676}
- **Positive psychology:** An approach to psychology that emphasizes normal behavior and human strengths, using scientific methods to understand positive experiences
- William James foreshadowed it in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902) with "healthy mindedness"; Abraham Maslow kept a "Good Human Being" notebook of people he admired
- Martin Seligman, building on learned helplessness, proposed that optimism can be learned
- **Optimism:** Thinking positively about the future
- **PERMA model:** Five elements of well-being: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment
- Happiness is linked to longevity, advancement at work, and staying married
- **Hedonic approach:** Happiness as obtaining pleasure and avoiding pain; **eudaimonic approach:** happiness as meaningfulness and self-realization
- Happiness is relative: we adapt to circumstances, and twin research suggests a genetically influenced happiness "set point"
- Close relationships are the most-cited source of happiness, and happiness spreads through social networks
- Marriage: 40% of married adults vs. 22% of never-married adults report being "very happy"; benefits double for couples who consider their spouse a best friend; 70% of very unhappy couples were happy five years later
- Wealth: being poor is linked to unhappiness, but once basic needs are met more money adds little; the 100 richest Americans were only slightly happier than average; spending on experiences beats material goods
- Happiness predicts later income gains more than income predicts happiness
- World Happiness Report: Finland ranked first; the U.S. ranked 18th; Afghanistan ranked last

## Increasing happiness {pp. 677–682}
| Type of happiness | What it is | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| The pleasant life (hedonic) | Moment-to-moment pleasures | Savor experiences without multitasking |
| The good life (eudaimonic) | Using your strengths in work and hobbies; "flow" | Incorporate your key strengths into activities; choose work you truly enjoy |
| The meaningful life (eudaimonic) | Serving others or something larger than yourself | Volunteer for a cause; acts of kindness; spend money on others |

- People are happiest overall when their good life or meaningful life score is high; pleasant life alone does not raise happiness much (it is highly heritable and habituates quickly)
- **Flow:** A state of complete absorption in a current activity, such as work, problem solving, or creativity, in which time seems to stand still (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi); video games meet many flow criteria
- Steps to improve happiness: time management, regular exercise, good sleep, interesting work, nurturing close relationships, and following a chosen faith
- **Hope:** An expectation that your goals will be met in the future; hopeful people withstand more pain and stress, solve problems better, and have strong social networks
- **Posttraumatic growth:** Seeing oneself as a survivor rather than a victim, more self-confidence, closer bonds, and a greater sense of what is meaningful after trauma
- Martin Seligman's resilience program for the U.S. Army lowered PTSD, anxiety, and depression among deployed soldiers and cut substance abuse in half
- **Value:** "An enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct or end state of existence is personally or socially preferable"; higher value ratings correlate with self-esteem and less violence, addiction, anxiety, and depression
- Positive institutions share a purpose, fair rules, reciprocal caring, safety, and respect
- Work orientations: a job (pays the bills), a career (status and achievement), and a calling (personal achievement plus contributing to the community)
- Gratitude for a partner's investments (especially emotional ones) increases commitment to the relationship
- Criticisms: positive psychology may imply that sick people just need a positive attitude, and positive strengths must be studied alongside negative experiences

## Everyday coping steps (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: How to Cope](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-support/how-to-cope)
- Accept where you are now: healing isn't a straight line, and there will be ups and downs; speak to yourself with kindness, as you would to a friend
- Self-care is different for everyone; taking care of yourself is healing yourself
- Take care of your body: get enough sleep on a regular schedule, spend time outside if safe, and exercise (even stretching or walking)
- Take care of your mind: write or draw your feelings, take deep breaths to calm your mind and slow your heart rate, take breaks from news or social media, explore hobbies, follow a regular schedule with breaks, and celebrate large and small successes
- Connect with people: ask for help when ready, join a support group or safe online community, talk to people who won't judge you, get involved in a team, school activity, or religious organization, and volunteer
- Taking care of your mental health takes courage and is a sign of strength

## Coping supports for specific groups (SAMHSA)
- **Teens and young adults:** Many people are introduced to drugs and alcohol in these years, and serious mental health issues commonly begin; reach out to a trusted adult, doctor, school nurse, or school-based health center; get help for bullying; make a plan to quit vaping
- **Workplace:** Long or irregular hours and emotionally or physically tiring jobs raise risk; many employers offer workplace wellness programs or an **employee assistance program (EAP)** with free or low-cost counseling
- **Older adults:** Talk to a doctor about mental health just as you would physical health; the Eldercare Locator helps with transportation and legal help; adult protective services and the Long-Term Care Ombudsman help with abuse
- **No Wrong Door System:** A coordinated way to access community services so you only have to tell your story once
- **Veterans:** Veterans Crisis Line (dial 988 then press 1, text 838255); the VA helps regardless of discharge status; **Vet Centers** offer no-cost community counseling (1-877-927-8387); Make the Connection and VA Peer Support (veterans who are trained counselors)
- **Active service members:** Military OneSource (1-800-342-9647, 24/7); commanders cannot access mental health records except in certain situations such as serious risk of harm; Real Warriors Campaign; TRICARE is the health program for service members, retirees, and families
- **People with disabilities:** Mental health and substance issues are as important as the main disability; Centers for Independent Living are run by people with disabilities; the Disability Information & Access Line is 1-888-677-1199
- **Caregivers:** Take care of yourself too: set times to talk, state what you are and are not willing to do, help the person find others to count on, and make time for yourself each day

## Coping with anger after a disaster (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA DTAC: Coping with Anger](https://www.samhsa.gov/technical-assistance/dtac/disaster-survivors/coping)
- Anger can make the heart beat faster, raise blood pressure, tighten muscles, release adrenaline, and disrupt breathing, digestion, and sleep
- Anger has benefits (identifying problems and giving energy to react to threats) but is linked to heart disease, high blood pressure, sleep and digestion problems, headaches, depression, anxiety, and risky substance use
- Calming strategies: **self-management** (notice cues and take a break or count to 10), **assertive communication** (use "I" statements and avoid "always," "never," and "should"), a **problem-solving approach**, and **forgiveness**
- Relaxation techniques: deep (abdominal) breathing, visualization of a peaceful place, progressive muscle relaxation, and gentle stretching, yoga, or tai chi
- Seek professional support if anger seems out of control, causes regret or harm, damages relationships or property, or lasts longer than one month (more anger is common in the first 2 to 4 weeks after a disaster)
- **Shield of Resilience:** SAMHSA's free 1-hour online course helping law enforcement officers recognize stress, depression, PTSD, and suicidal ideation and build peer support and resilience
