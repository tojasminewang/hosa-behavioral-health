## Eating disorders in the textbook {pp. 260–261}
- Eating disorders involve unusual patterns of eating and distortions of body image; people with distorted body image can believe they are obese when in danger of starvation
- Lifetime incidence of eating disorders: about 0.17% in males and about 3% in females; rates are about six times higher in homosexual and bisexual males than heterosexual males
- **Anorexia nervosa:** An eating disorder characterized by the maintenance of unusually low body weight, an intense fear of gaining weight, and a distorted view of the body as obese ("anorexia" means "loss of appetite")
  - Two patterns: the restricting type (eating little food) and the bingeing-purging type (restricted eating plus bulimia-like behaviors)
  - Affects about 0.4% of females and perhaps 0.04% of males
  - One of the few psychological disorders that can kill: up to 5% of people with anorexia die per decade, including from elevated suicide risk
  - Other symptoms: interrupted menstruation, very dry and yellow skin, fine downy hair (lanugo), sensitivity to cold, and cardiovascular and gastrointestinal problems
- **Bulimia nervosa:** An eating disorder characterized by cycles of binge eating and purging (vomiting or laxatives), with feelings of depression, disgust, and lost control; more common than anorexia (1.5% of females and 0.15% to 0.5% of males)
- **Binge-eating disorder:** Eating abnormally large amounts of food at one sitting and feeling that eating is out of control, without compensatory behaviors such as vomiting or laxatives
  - People eat rapidly, until uncomfortably full, when not hungry, and often alone out of embarrassment
  - Smaller gender difference than other eating disorders: about 1.6% of adult females and 0.8% of adult males
- Culture: after American television arrived in Fiji in 1995, 74% of adolescent girls in Anne Becker's study reported feeling "too big or too fat," and dieting rose to U.S. levels (correlational)
- Runway models average a BMI of only 16.5; some European countries now require models to have a BMI of at least 18.5
- Heritability: personality characteristics that raise risk may be inherited rather than the disorders themselves; twin studies are mixed (48% to 88% heritability in some reports)
- Bingeing has been discussed as a food addiction; nearly all individuals with bulimia score as food-addicted on the Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0
- Treating anorexia: the first priority is keeping the person alive (often hospitalization and monitored eating); cognitive behavioral therapy addresses body image; no known medications are effective; about 50% fully recover and nearly 20% fail to respond
- Treating bulimia and binge-eating disorder is more encouraging: antidepressants, especially SSRIs, are usually quite effective, especially combined with cognitive behavioral therapy

## Eating disorders (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Eating Disorders](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/eating-disorders)
- **Eating disorders (SAMHSA):** Mental disorders that involve extreme mental preoccupation and disturbing emotions, attitudes, and behaviors involving weight and food; can be a serious mental illness when they meet DSM criteria and cause significant functional impairment
- Common eating disorders: anorexia nervosa, binge eating disorder, bulimia, and avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)
- Misconception: people with eating disorders have a particular body type; eating disorders can affect anyone regardless of body size, race/ethnicity, gender, age, or socioeconomic status
- Contributing factors: genes, hormones, co-morbid mental disorders (depression, anxiety), social attitudes promoting very thin bodies, and diet culture
- **Anorexia nervosa (SAMHSA):** An eating disorder in which people avoid or severely restrict food, with an intense fear of weight gain; they may diet, exercise excessively, or use laxatives or diuretics
- Anorexia risk factors: overconcern with weight and shape, anxiety or depression, negative self-image, early childhood eating problems, internalizing cultural ideals of beauty, perfectionism
- Anorexia signs: cutting food into small pieces or moving it around the plate, exercising even when hurt, going to the bathroom right after meals, refusing to eat around others, diet pills or laxatives; also slow thinking, depression, dry mouth, loss of bone strength, muscle, and fat, and fatigue
- **Binge-eating disorder (SAMHSA):** Eating much larger amounts of food in a shorter time than normal with a feeling of loss of control, often secretly, using food to cope, and feeling shame or guilt
- **Bulimia nervosa (SAMHSA):** Binge eating with loss of control followed by methods to prevent weight gain (vomiting, excessive exercise, laxatives, diuretics, diet pills); people are usually aware the pattern is abnormal
- Bulimia signs: chronically inflamed sore throat, sensitive and decaying teeth (from stomach acid), severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalances that can lead to heart attack or stroke, food disappearing quickly, bathroom visits right after meals
- **Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID):** Limiting the volume, type, or variety of foods eaten, most often starting in infancy or early childhood (sometimes after an event like choking); unlike other eating disorders, there is no distress about body image or weight
- ARFID signs: extremely picky eating, avoiding meals or eating with others, pretending to eat, distress at mealtime; symptoms include weight loss, delayed growth or puberty, nutritional deficiencies, and social impairment
- Eating disorders can be fatal due to medical complications and a high risk of suicide; treatment can include psychotherapy, medical care, nutrition counseling, or medications; full recovery is possible

## Eating disorders (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Eating Disorders](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/eating-disorders/)
- An early sign of an eating disorder is becoming so preoccupied with food and weight that it is hard to focus on other parts of life; untreated, eating disorders can cause serious, potentially fatal medical complications
- Eating disorders can affect any age or gender, but rates are higher among women, and symptoms commonly appear in adolescence and young adulthood
- **Anorexia nervosa (NAMI):** Self-starvation driven by an obsession with weight loss; the person denies hunger, refuses food, may binge and purge, or exercises to exhaustion
  - Emotional signs: irritability, social withdrawal, not understanding how serious the situation is, fear of eating in public, and food rituals
  - Physical toll: loss of menstruation, constipation, irregular heart rhythms, low blood pressure, dehydration and trouble sleeping as the body slows down to conserve energy
- **Bulimia nervosa (NAMI):** Feeling out of control while binging on large amounts of food in a short time, then trying to get rid of the calories by forced vomiting, laxatives or excessive exercise
  - People with bulimia are usually normal weight or a bit overweight
  - Vomiting damages teeth and causes acid reflux; dehydration upsets the body's electrolytes and can lead to cardiac arrhythmias, heart failure and death
- **Binge eating disorder (BED):** Losing control and eating a very large amount of food in a short time, often when not hungry, followed by embarrassment or guilt but without purging or excessive exercise
- Most experts now believe eating disorders come from trying to cope with overwhelming feelings and painful emotions by controlling food
- Contributing factors: genetics (first-degree relatives; evidence that serotonin is involved), environment (cultural pressure toward thinness for women or muscularity for men), peer pressure (teasing, bullying), and emotional health (perfectionism, impulsivity)
- Risk factors: being in the teens or early 20s, being female (men may be under-diagnosed), family history, dieting taken too far, times of change (college, a new job, divorce), and activities such as gymnastics, running, wrestling and dance
- Early diagnosis gives the best recovery outcome; a doctor does a physical exam, interview and lab tests, and a mental health professional does a psychological evaluation using DSM criteria
- There is no medication for eating disorders themselves, but antidepressants and anti-anxiety drugs can help with co-occurring depression or anxiety
- NAMI eating disorder treatment: psychotherapy, medical monitoring and nutritional counseling (weight restoration)
- **Family-based treatment:** An eating disorder treatment that enlists the family to support healthy eating in children and adolescents
- CBT developed for bulimia is very effective at changing binge-purge behaviors and eating attitudes
- Eating disorder treatment settings range from outpatient care to residential or partial hospital programs; hospitalization may be needed for serious nutrition problems or very low weight
- Related conditions: depression, anxiety disorders, BPD, OCD and substance use disorders
- Self-help: avoid frequent mirror checks and weighing, don't diet or skip meals, plan for triggers, and accept that your healthy weight is your ideal weight; weight-loss supplements, diuretics and laxatives are often unregulated and misused
- Supporting someone: talk in a loving, non-confrontational way, suggest a professional, avoid blame or simple solutions, and be a good role model (the National Eating Disorders Association is a resource)
