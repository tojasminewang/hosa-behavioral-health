## Stress and the stress response
- **Stress:** unpleasant emotional state from perceiving danger
- **Stressor:** the source of stress; highly individual
- Selye (1975): stress can push good performance and long-term health, but can also hurt performance and well-being, and in extremes contribute to depression, schizophrenia, PTSD
- Cold, lack of oxygen, and emotional experiences can trigger fight or flight: heart rate, blood pressure, breathing rise; nonessential functions are held back; tuned by evolution for emergencies
- Hans Selye studied stronger, longer stressors in rats by timing how long they swam before giving up; cold water, restraint, shock, surgery, and whisker cutting all shortened it
- **General adaptation syndrome (GAS):** Selye's consistent response pattern to any stressor
- GAS has three stages, in order: alarm, resistance, exhaustion
- Resistance to stress dips at first (alarm), climbs above normal (resistance), then drops below normal (exhaustion)

| GAS stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Alarm | Resources mobilized for fight or flight |
| Resistance | Adapting and coping while stress continues |
| Exhaustion | Resources run out |

## Sources of stress
- Stressors are in the eye of the beholder
- **Cognitive appraisal models:** predict what stresses a person from quick judgements of a stressor as irrelevant or harmless, positive or negative; because people judge from their own point of view, the same event can stress people differently

![Cognitive appraisal: why the same event can stress people differently](hc-appraisal)

- Common stressors: large-scale disasters (terrorist attacks, Haiti and Japan earthquakes, Oklahoma tornadoes), life changes, hassles
- **Hassles:** relatively minor everyday stressors
- The amygdala's "fear circuit" quickly flags danger and signals the hypothalamus, which commands the autonomic (sympathetic, fight-or-flight) system
- Fear-conditioning picture: in a rat, a tone (CS) travels through the auditory thalamus and auditory cortex, and a shock (UCS) through the somatosensory thalamus and cortex; both reach the amygdala, and the rat freezes
- Two stress pathways: the faster sympathetic adrenal-medullary (SAM) system (sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine, the adrenal glands release norepinephrine and epinephrine, or adrenaline; boosts the immune system) and the slower hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (hypothalamus → pituitary gland → adrenal glands → cortisol; suppresses the immune system)

![The fast fear circuit](hc-stress-path)

- **Tend and befriend:** stress response more common in women, tied to oxytocin, a social-bonding hormone
- Oxytocin relieves stress in animals only when in a social group, not isolated
- Lower socioeconomic status brings more noise, toxins, carcinogens, violence, drug and alcohol abuse, anger; fewer resources; less health care, exercise, control, trust (Adler, 2013): all linked to high stress

## Stress and health
- **Immune system:** defends against infection and cancer
- A stressful memory task improved immune response (saliva markers), but chronic stress weakens it, raising vulnerability to infections like colds
- **Lymphocytes:** white blood cells protecting against invaders; stress hormones directly suppress them
- **Type A personality:** Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman's (1974) competitive workaholics, proposed to be more prone to heart disease than laid-back Type B people
- Competitiveness itself mattered little; Type A people who developed heart problems showed interpersonal hostility
- Stress harms the cardiovascular system by impairing blood vessels' ability to expand
- Chronic stress can cascade into mood, sleep, and appetite changes; stress-related sleep loss is especially harmful
- **Cortisol:** stress hormone that also maintains the sleep–wake cycle; high early in the morning (wakefulness), falling through the day; high cortisol at bedtime hurts sleep quality

![Cortisol through the day (a sketch)](hc-cortisol)

- Stress and depression both disturb sleep, especially with frequent night waking
- Stress hormones make fat cells grow in size and number
- Integrated view: stress and health sit inside nested social layers: micro (family, peer groups, school, work), meso (links among those), exo (social networks, neighbourhood, communications, mass media, transportation, goods and services, government agencies), macro (political, legal and economic systems, social norms, education, culture, race and ethnicity)

## Coping
| Type of coping | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Problem-focused coping | Addresses the problem itself |
| Emotion-focused coping | Handles negative emotions from a stressor |
| Relationship-focused coping | Maintains and protects relationships under stress |

- **Biopsychosocial model:** health results from biological, psychological, and social factors
- The slide's diagram: environmental stressors, major life events and trauma or abuse → perceived stress (threat, helplessness, vigilance) → behavioural responses (fight or flight; habits such as diet, smoking, drinking, exercise) and physical responses → adaptation → health outcomes, all shaped by individual differences (genes, development, experience)
