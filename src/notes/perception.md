## Perception, attention, and processing {pp. 150–153}
- **Perception:** The process of interpreting sensory information; it allows us to organize, recognize, and use the information provided by the senses
- We construct models of reality from the information obtained through our senses ("The Dress" was seen as blue/black by 57% and white/gold by 30% of people)
- **Attention:** A narrow focus of consciousness; an important gateway to perception
- Unfamiliar, changing, or high-intensity stimuli grab our attention (memorable ads use novelty, change, and intensity)
- **Divided attention:** Attempting to process multiple sources of sensory information at once; these abilities are limited
- **Selective attention:** The ability to focus on a subset of available information and exclude the rest; it may be disrupted in ADHD
- Expert radiologists focused on scans for cancer: 20 out of 24 completely missed a gorilla superimposed on the scans
- **Bottom-up processing:** Perception based on building simple input into more complex perceptions
- **Top-down processing:** A perceptual process in which memory and other cognitive processes are required for interpreting incoming sensory information (e.g., reading jumbled words)
- We always use bottom-up processing; as stimuli become more complicated or familiar (reading, recognizing a friend), we add top-down processing

## Color vision {pp. 160–161}
- The primary colors of light are red, green, and blue; mixing them produces white light
- **Trichromatic theory:** A theory of color vision based on three types of cones that detect short (blue), medium (green), and long (red) wavelengths; color comes from comparing the responses of all three cone types
- **Opponent process theory:** A theory of color vision suggesting a red–green channel and a blue–yellow channel in which activating one color in each pair inhibits the other (black and white also share a channel)
- Opponent process theory explains color afterimages: staring at a green, black, and yellow flag fatigues those neurons, so the afterimage looks red, white, and blue
- Both theories are correct at different levels: trichromatic theory fits the cones of the retina; opponent process theory fits higher levels of visual analysis
- **Color vision deficiency:** Having fewer than the typical three types of cones; the term "colorblind" is no longer used because it is not accurate
- Between 7% and 10% of males and about 0.4% of females have red–green color vision deficiency; it is sex-linked because the red and green pigment genes are on the X chromosome
- Complete lack of color vision is rare (about 1 person in 30,000); the Ishihara Color Test (1917) is a standard method for detecting color vision deficiency
- Accessible design: color should never be the only basis for extracting meaning (traffic lights also vary in location)

## Recognizing objects {pp. 162–163}
- A bottom-up hierarchical model would require a hypothetical "grandmother cell" that combines all earlier processing; it cannot account for top-down processing
- The visual system might instead analyze patterns of lines (gratings), which vary in frequency (number of bars, detail) and contrast (difference in intensity between bars)
- Compared with humans, cats see less detail but see large, low-contrast objects (like shadows) better

## Gestalt principles {pp. 163–164}
- The Gestalt psychologists (German researchers) objected to Wundt's structuralist effort to reduce experience to elements; Kurt Koffka: "the whole is something else than the sum of its parts"
- They believed we are born with built-in tendencies to organize incoming sensory information

| Gestalt principle | What it means |
|---|---|
| Figure–ground | We spontaneously divide a scene into a main figure and a background (ground); ambiguous images like the vase/faces switch roles |
| Proximity | Objects that are close together tend to be grouped together |
| Similarity | Similar stimuli are grouped together |
| Continuity | Points that form smooth lines when connected probably belong together |
| Closure | We see a complete, unbroken image even when there are gaps in the lines (e.g., the World Wildlife Fund logo) |
| Simplicity | We use the simplest solution to a perceptual problem |

## Depth perception {pp. 164–167}
- **Depth perception:** The ability to use the two-dimensional image projected on the retina to perceive three dimensions
- **Monocular cue:** A depth cue that requires the use of only one eye
- **Binocular cue:** A depth cue that requires the use of both eyes
- **Linear perspective:** The apparent convergence of parallel lines at the horizon (used by Italian artists in the 15th century)
- **Texture gradients:** We see more texture in close objects; distant textures look blurry
- **Shading:** Shading and highlights suggest curved surfaces
- **Occlusion:** The blocking of images of distant objects by closer objects; among the most powerful monocular cues
- **Relative size:** Judging distance from the retinal image size of objects whose real size we know
- **Motion parallax:** When moving, closer objects appear to move faster (in the opposite direction) than distant objects
- **Retinal disparity:** The difference between the images projected onto each eye; a binocular cue that tells the relative distance between two objects (disparity increases as the distance between objects increases)
- Retinal disparity helps predators spot camouflaged prey and has been used to detect camouflaged military equipment and counterfeit currency; 3D cameras use two lenses about 2 inches apart
- **Ames room:** A room built by Adelbert Ames that confuses distance judgments so people seem larger or smaller than normal
- **Moon illusion:** The moon looks larger near the horizon because we judge it against familiar objects (relative size)
- **Müller–Lyer illusion:** Lines of equal length look different because of outward- or inward-pointing arrowheads
- **Ponzo illusion:** Parallel lines signal depth (linear perspective), so the upper bar looks farther away and longer
- Roger Shepard's "Turning the Tables" illusion: two identical tabletops look different because a visual system built for a 3D world is applied to a 2D picture
- Infant development: binocular disparity and adult-like color responses by 4 months; occlusion understood at 2 months; relative size used between 5 and 7 months; 2-day-old newborns gaze longer at their mothers' faces

## Auditory perception {pp. 175–178}
- **Place theory:** The frequency of a sound is correlated with the part of the basilar membrane showing a peak response; high tones peak near the narrow, stiff base and low tones near the wide, floppy apex; works well above 4,000 Hz
- **Temporal theory:** Below 4,000 Hz, the pattern of neural firing matches the frequency of the sound
- Humans hear sounds varying in intensity by more than 10 billion times; a sound 10 times more intense is perceived as only twice as loud
- Humans are most sensitive to sounds in the range of speech, between 80 and 10,000 Hz
- Sound localization: the pinna localizes sound vertically; horizontally we compare arrival times at each ear (0 milliseconds for sounds straight ahead or behind, up to 0.6 milliseconds for sounds perpendicular to the head) and intensity differences (the head casts a sound "shadow")
- Sounds from one location, or that start and stop together, are grouped as having the same source
- **McGurk effect:** An auditory illusion combining vision and hearing: hearing "ba-ba" while seeing lips say "ga-ga" is perceived as "da-da"
- **Sine wave speech:** Speech altered into sine waves sounds like tweeting birds unless people are told it is speech (top-down influence)
- **Absolute pitch:** The ability to name a musical tone you hear; linked to larger left-hemisphere areas and to higher levels of autistic traits
- Deafness can be viewed as a culture with its own attitudes, language, and norms; American Sign Language (ASL) is distinct from signed English

## Perceiving pain {pp. 181–183}
- **Gate theory:** Input from touch fibers competes with input from pain receptors, possibly preventing pain messages from reaching the brain (why rubbing a bumped elbow helps); chronic stress opens the gate wider
- Higher brain centers influence pain through the periaqueductal gray, which is rich in endorphin receptors; electrical stimulation there significantly reduces pain
- **Placebo effect:** Pain reduction after an ineffective substance or treatment, such as a sugar pill; it can occur even when people know they are receiving a placebo
- No other sense is as dramatically affected by culture, context, and experience as pain (e.g., the hook-swinging ritual in India)
- Women who took prepared childbirth classes report less pain; athletes in contact sports tolerate more pain than nonathletes; patients who self-administer morphine need less medication (sense of control)
- Holding a husband's hand reduced pain-related brain activity in women expecting a shock, especially in happy marriages

## Perceiving smells, tastes, and flavor {pp. 185–188}
- Humans can distinguish among at least 1 trillion odors
- Labels change perception: an onion odor labeled "pizza" is rated more pleasant than the same odor labeled "body odor"
- **Flavor:** The combined perception of smell and taste; pathways converge in the orbitofrontal cortex (with your nose held you cannot tell apple from raw potato)
- Combat veterans with PTSD experienced marked anxiety and amygdala changes when exposed to the smell of diesel
- Culture shapes smell and taste: wintergreen (candy in Canada, medicine in France) was rated differently; infants whose mothers drank carrot juice during pregnancy preferred carrot flavor

## Culture, autism, and loneliness in perception {pp. 168–189}
- East Asian cultures tend to process information holistically (context and relationships); Western cultures process analytically (salient objects and categories); Western children begin to ignore context around age 5
- Infants at high risk for autism spectrum disorder who were superior at a visual search task at 9 months showed more ASD symptoms at 15 months and 2 years
- People who are lonely become more sensitive to social threats (such as social rejection) but not to non-social threats (such as snakes); this can lead to self-preservation behavior that perpetuates loneliness
