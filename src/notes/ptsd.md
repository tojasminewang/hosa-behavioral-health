## Posttraumatic stress disorder (textbook) {pp. 582–583}
- **Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD):** A disorder caused by the experience of trauma, which leads to flashbacks, dreams, hypervigilance, and avoidance of stimuli associated with the traumatic event
- PTSD was formerly known as shell shock or battle fatigue
- PTSD is unique in the DSM-5 because it is the only disorder with a specified cause: you must have experienced trauma
- Causes include combat (especially hand-to-hand), automobile accidents, assaults, abuse, and natural disasters
- Symptoms: hypervigilance, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, emotional numbing, and reexperiencing through intrusive thoughts, flashbacks, and dreams
- Children may reenact the trauma in play; play that reworks the event (revenge or a happy ending) is less disturbing than repetitive play without resolution
- PTSD affects 3% to 4% of U.S. adults each year; after large-scale trauma about 10% of the population is expected to develop PTSD (more than 12% of lower Manhattan residents after 9/11)
- Children are more vulnerable: 25% of injured children developed PTSD after car accidents compared with 15% of their parents
- 8.5% to 14% of combat soldiers from Iraq and Afghanistan had severe PTSD impairment, and up to 31% had some impairment

## Causes of PTSD (textbook) {pp. 583–584}
- PTSD is heritable, and its genetic profile overlaps significantly with schizophrenia
- PTSD is consistently correlated with smaller hippocampal volume; current data suggest a smaller hippocampus is more likely a risk factor than an outcome
- Disturbed GABA/benzodiazepine systems may explain hypervigilance and the tendency to self-medicate with alcohol
- Classical conditioning: people with PTSD show conditioned responses to trauma-related cues (combat veterans react to burning rubber and diesel smells) and increased generalization over time
- Abnormal norepinephrine activity is implicated in PTSD
- Social support: people with low social support before 9/11 were twice as likely to develop PTSD; strong social support protects against PTSD and speeds recovery
- After 9/11, PTSD affected 13.4% of Hispanic, 9.3% of African American, 6.5% of White, and 3.2% of Asian American New York City residents
- **Secondary traumatization:** Families of soldiers are also affected by combat experiences; veterans with PTSD have a higher risk of intimate aggression and engage in little self-disclosure
- EMDR (eye movement desensitization and reprocessing) is considered an evidence-based treatment for trauma; MDMA-assisted treatment for PTSD awaits FDA approval

## Treating PTSD (textbook) {pp. 630–631}
- The American Psychological Association and the U.S. Veterans Administration strongly recommend three psychotherapies for PTSD: prolonged exposure therapy, cognitive processing therapy, and trauma-focused CBT
- **Prolonged exposure therapy:** Assumes that confronting the trauma rather than avoiding it produces benefits
- **Cognitive processing therapy:** Addresses inaccurate attributions and self-blame about the traumatic events
- **Trauma-focused CBT:** Combines exposure with correcting guilt and other flawed thinking
- Virtual reality ("Virtual Iraq") exposure can add sounds, weather, and diesel and gunpowder smells; it appeals to veterans unwilling to try traditional treatment
- Joseph Wolpe popularized combining relaxation with controlled exposure in the 1950s

## PTSD (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: PTSD](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/ptsd)
- PTSD is a real disorder that develops when a person has experienced or witnessed a scary, shocking, terrifying, or dangerous event, usually one where life was threatened or severe injury occurred
- Causes: war, natural disaster, sexual assault, physical abuse, or a bad accident; PTSD makes you feel stressed and afraid after the danger is over
- PTSD may start soon after the event or months or even years later; it is related to the seriousness of the trauma, whether it was repeated, proximity to it, and the relationship with the victim or perpetrator
- To be considered PTSD, signs and symptoms must last more than a month and be severe enough to interfere with school, work, or relationships; PTSD can happen to anyone, even children
- Symptoms: flashbacks, trouble sleeping or nightmares, feeling detached, losing interest, angry outbursts, guilt, frightening thoughts, trouble concentrating, physical pain, avoidance of reminders, memory problems, negative beliefs, irritability, feeling very vigilant, and startling easily
- Symptoms of anxiety, depression, and substance use are also seen with PTSD
- A psychiatrist or psychologist experienced with mental illness can diagnose PTSD

## PTSD (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Posttraumatic Stress Disorder](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/posttraumatic-stress-disorder/)
- **Trauma (NAMI):** A deeply distressing experience that overwhelms a person's ability to cope, including abuse, accidents, disasters, violence and loss, as well as systemic experiences such as racism and discrimination
- About 4.1% of U.S. adults (over 8 million people) experience PTSD, and women are more likely than men to experience it
- Trauma's effects were described as early as Homer's The Iliad; earlier terms for war trauma were "shell shock" (World War I) and "battle fatigue" (World War II)
- PTSD became a formal diagnosis in the DSM-III in 1980, largely because of Vietnam veterans' symptoms, and advocacy from rape victim groups showed it was more than a war injury
- The newest DSM moved PTSD out of the anxiety disorders into its own category, Trauma and Stressor Related Disorders, because anger, depression and dissociation are also trauma responses
- Only a relatively small portion of people exposed to trauma develop PTSD; risk factors include prior trauma, limited social or family support, other stressors, and a personal or family history of mental illness or substance use
- PTSD can be seen as the body's inability to return to "normal" in the months after its fight-or-flight response to a traumatic event
- PTSD symptoms typically emerge within three months of the trauma, but sometimes not until years later
- The four PTSD symptom categories (National Center for PTSD): re-experiencing or intrusion (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance of reminders, negative changes in mood and cognition (numbness, detachment), and hyperarousal (irritability, trouble sleeping, heightened startle)
- PTSD is diagnosed only when all four types of symptoms last at least one month and cause significant distress or disruption
- Young children's behavior (such as clinging to parents) shows how they are doing better than their words, and they may slip back to earlier behaviors, such as toileting accidents after potty training
- PTSD screening tools: the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS, a structured interview), the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5, a self-report questionnaire), the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) and the Trauma Screening Questionnaire (TSQ)
- Most people find that psychotherapy plus medication works best for PTSD; no one should be forced to talk about a traumatic event before they are ready
- **Cognitive processing therapy:** A type of CBT that targets the negative thinking and self-blame that can come with PTSD
- EMDR was specifically designed to treat trauma, using measured exposure to traumatic memories with alternating stimuli such as eye movements
- **Exposure therapy:** Therapy that helps people safely face what traumatized them so they can learn to cope; it can use virtual reality programs
- Group therapy with people who have similar experiences lessens shame, isolation and helplessness
- Some SSRIs are FDA approved for PTSD in adults and are often the first-line medication, but no single medication treats all PTSD symptoms
- No randomized controlled trial has tested marijuana for PTSD, and MDMA is being studied to boost therapy but is illegal in the U.S. and not FDA approved
- Complementary approaches for PTSD: yoga, aqua therapy, acupuncture, and mindfulness and meditation
- Service dogs stay by a person's side 24 hours a day and can act as a reality-grounding tool, a social buffer and a de-escalation tool
- Successfully treating PTSD almost always improves related conditions (anxiety, OCD, BPD, MDD, substance use), and treating those usually improves PTSD
- NAMI HelpLine: call 800-950-6264 or text "NAMI" to 62640, Monday through Friday, 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. ET; the 988 Veterans Crisis Line is reached by dialing 988 then pressing 1, or texting 838255
- **NAMI Homefront:** NAMI's resources and program for active-duty service members, veterans and military families
- **NAMI TraumaInSight:** A free, self-paced course that helps teachers and others who work with youth become more trauma-aware
