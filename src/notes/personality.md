## Personality disorders overview (textbook) {p. 586}
- **Personality disorder:** A disorder characterized by impairments in identity, in personality traits, and in establishment of empathy or intimacy
- Personality disorders cannot be diagnosed in people under the age of 18
- The DSM-5 has ten types of personality disorders; three are covered in detail: antisocial, borderline, and narcissistic
- ICD-11 groups all DSM-5 personality disorders into a single category
- About 18% of young adults surveyed met criteria for a personality disorder in the previous year

## Antisocial personality disorder (textbook) {p. 586}
- **Antisocial personality disorder (ASPD):** A disorder characterized by an unusual lack of remorse, empathy, or regard for normal social rules and conventions; a "pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others"
- Behavior is risky and irresponsible, with shallow, fleeting relationships
- ASPD is similar to the term **psychopath**, which the DSM has not used since DSM-III (1980); ASPD criteria focus on behavior (especially criminal behavior), while "psychopath" implies flawed character traits
- 0.2% to 3.3% of the population experiences ASPD in a given year; Robert Hare estimated about 1% of Americans are psychopaths
- Most people with ASPD are not criminals, and not all criminals have ASPD
- Heritability is about 50%; fearlessness may characterize both heroes and antisocial people (David Lykken)
- People with ASPD show reduced prefrontal volume and activity (about 11% less prefrontal volume) and orbitofrontal abnormalities; they fail to anticipate consequences and cannot delay gratification
- People with ASPD show little emotional brain activation to words like "torture" or "rape" and little amygdala activity; children with antisocial tendencies have trouble identifying sadness and fear
- A genetic predisposition interacts with child maltreatment, especially physical abuse

## Borderline personality disorder (textbook) {p. 587}
- **Borderline personality disorder (BPD):** A disorder characterized by instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, and emotion
- The name came from the idea that its characteristics fell on the "border" between anxiety and psychosis
- People with BPD are extremely sensitive to perceived slights, make frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, and may act in impulsive, self-destructive ways; suicide and self-mutilation are more common
- Relationships start intense and idealized but can quickly turn to anger after a perceived slight
- BPD affects about 2% of people; women outnumber men about 3 to 1
- BPD is five times more common among close relatives of people with the disorder; as many as 70% have histories of abandonment, neglect, or abuse
- People with BPD are vulnerable to bipolar disorder, substance abuse, eating disorders, and other personality disorders; their anterior insula responds abnormally in trust games

## Narcissistic personality disorder (textbook) {pp. 587–591}
- **Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD):** A disorder characterized by grandiosity, need for admiration, and low empathy
- Two possible subtypes: the grandiose narcissist (inflated self-worth, aggressiveness, dominance, extroverted) and the vulnerable narcissist (inflated self-worth plus insecurity, defensiveness, shy and neurotic)
- Younger people show more narcissism than older adults; selfies and social media are an outlet for narcissistic tendencies
- Narcissistic traits are somewhat heritable; parental overvaluation or cold, high-expectation parenting may contribute
- **Dark Triad:** Psychopathy, narcissism, and Machiavellianism (coldness, dishonesty, calculated manipulation); narcissism and Machiavellianism are linked to career success, but psychopathy is not

## Treating personality disorders (textbook) {pp. 632–633}
- No known medications specifically reduce antisocial behavior; people with ASPD tend to see themselves as "OK" and not needing treatment
- CBT and learning models emphasizing anger control, social skills, and moral reasoning are modestly effective in youth but not for true psychopathy; many nations rely on incarceration
- No medications are approved specifically for borderline personality disorder, though antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, antianxiety drugs, and anticonvulsants are prescribed; brief hospitalization may be needed for safety
- **Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT):** A therapy designed for borderline personality disorder (Marsha Linehan) that addresses symptoms in order of importance: reducing suicidal thoughts, then behaviors that interfere with therapy, then behaviors that interfere with quality of life; outcomes are superior to conventional CBT
- NPD is treated with CBT that carefully builds an alliance with the client; traits like defensiveness make people with NPD less likely to seek treatment

## Personality disorders (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Borderline Personality Disorder](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/borderline-personality-disorder) · [Antisocial Personality Disorder](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/antisocial-personality-disorder)
- **BPD (SAMHSA):** A mental health condition with long-term patterns of unstable or explosive emotions, leading to impulsive actions, self-image issues, and chaotic relationships
- BPD causes are unknown; genetic, family, and social factors play roles; risk factors include abandonment in childhood or adolescence, disrupted family life, poor family communication, and sexual, physical, or emotional abuse
- BPD occurs more often in women and among hospitalized psychiatric patients
- People with BPD are uncertain about their identity, see things in extremes ("all good or all bad"), and their views of others change quickly
- BPD symptoms: intense fear of abandonment, cannot tolerate being alone, frequent emptiness, inappropriate anger, impulsive and risky behavior (substance misuse, gambling, binge eating, sex), repeated crises and self-harm, and a distorted sense of self
- BPD can lead to depression, substance use, relationship problems, suicide attempts, self-harm, and death by suicide
- Individual talk therapy can successfully treat BPD; group therapy and medications for co-occurring conditions can help; with long-term talk therapy the person often gradually improves
- **Antisocial personality disorder (SAMHSA):** A long-term pattern of manipulating, exploiting, or violating the rights of others
- Its cause is unknown; genetics and environment (such as child abuse) contribute; people with an antisocial or alcoholic parent are at increased risk; more men than women are affected
- Intentional fire-setting and cruelty to animals during childhood are linked to developing antisocial personality
- Signs: witty and charming, good at flattery and manipulation, repeatedly breaks the law, disregards safety, substance misuse, lies, steals, fights, shows no guilt or remorse, often angry or arrogant
- ASPD is difficult to treat because people rarely seek treatment; behavioral treatments that reward appropriate behavior and give consequences for negative behavior may work, talk therapy may help, and treating co-morbid ADHD, depression, and PTSD helps

## Borderline personality disorder (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Borderline Personality Disorder](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/borderline-personality-disorder/)
- NAMI describes BPD as a condition of difficulty regulating emotion: emotions are felt intensely and for long periods, and it is harder to return to a stable baseline after a triggering event
- This difficulty can lead to impulsivity, poor self-image, stormy relationships, intense reactions to stress, and self-harm such as cutting
- About 1.4% of U.S. adults experience BPD; nearly 75% of people diagnosed are women, but men may be equally affected and are often misdiagnosed with PTSD or depression
- Key BPD signs: frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, unstable relationships, a distorted and unstable self-image, dangerous impulsive behaviors, self-harm, intense moods lasting hours to days, chronic emptiness, intense anger followed by shame, and dissociation or stress-related paranoia
- **Splitting:** In BPD, relationships that swing between idealization ("I'm so in love!") and devaluation ("I hate her")
- Causes of BPD: genetics (a close family member with BPD raises risk), environment (childhood abuse, neglect or separation from parents), and brain function (the brain areas for emotion and for judgment may not communicate well)
- There is no medical test for BPD; a mental health professional diagnoses it after a comprehensive clinical interview that may include past clinicians, records, and family or friends
- Psychotherapy is the first-line treatment and the cornerstone of BPD care
- **Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT):** Individual and group therapy that teaches coping and mindfulness skills to regulate emotions, resist harmful urges and improve relationships; it reduces suicidal behavior, hospitalization, treatment dropout, substance use and anger
- **Mentalization-based therapy (MBT):** Therapy that helps people become aware of their own internal states and develop empathy for other people's experiences
- No medication is made specifically for the core symptoms of BPD; medications are used off-label for symptoms (mood stabilizers and antidepressants for mood swings, low-dose antipsychotics for disorganized thinking)
- Short-term hospitalization may be needed during extreme stress or suicidal behavior, but most people with BPD are treated as outpatients in the community
- Conditions that often occur with BPD: anxiety disorders, PTSD, bipolar disorder, depression, eating disorders (notably bulimia nervosa) and substance use disorders
- People with BPD who follow a comprehensive treatment plan have good long-term outcomes
- Family members can watch for warning signs (shopping sprees, sexual or substance binges, fighting), encourage treatment, learn the language of DBT, speak honestly and kindly, and react calmly
