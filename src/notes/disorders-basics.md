## Defining a psychological disorder {pp. 550–553}
- **Psychological disorder:** "A syndrome characterized by clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning" (APA, 2013)
- **Abnormal psychology:** The study of psychological disorders
- "Abnormal" literally means "away from the rule" (Latin ab = away from, norma = the rule), which implies a statistical definition of abnormality
- Statistical approach: clear (e.g., an IQ of 70 or below is the typical cutoff for intellectual disability, along with difficulties in adaptive skills), but it is culturally specific and does not capture distress
- "Deviation from the ideal" approach captures distress but runs into cultural differences (hearing voices may be seen as religious giftedness in some cultures)
- The textbook's combined definition: **Abnormal behavior is distressing and harmful to self or others**
- **Comorbid:** Two or more disorders in the same individual
- About 26% of people experience a psychological disorder in a given year; in one study the cumulative chance of at least one disorder from age 19–20 to 50 was 73.9%
- Disorders can be viewed categorically (distinct characteristics) and along a continuum from normal behavior to severe disorder
- "First-year medical student's disease": the sense that you have all the disorders you are studying
- Nearly half of young adults under 24 met criteria for at least one disorder in the past year (18% personality disorder, 12% anxiety disorder, 11% major depressive or bipolar disorder); college students have the same rates as non-students except for higher alcohol use disorders
- Serious psychological distress, major depression, suicidal thoughts, and suicide attempts have increased dramatically among adolescents and young adults in the last 10 years

## Diagnosing disorders {pp. 553–554}
- Unlike medical illnesses, psychological disorders are diagnosed purely on observable behaviors; there are no blood tests or brain scans that diagnose them
- **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM):** A system for classification of psychological disorders published by the American Psychiatric Association (APA); first published in 1952; the current edition is DSM-5
- **ICD-11:** The International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 11th edition, published by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2018)
- ICD-11 (but not DSM-5) has a category for gaming disorder; ICD-11 groups all personality disorders into a single category
- Allen Frances (chaired the DSM-IV task force) warned of three "false epidemics": ADHD, autism, and childhood bipolar disorder
- Over its history the DSM has become less Freudian and more sensitive to cross-cultural issues
- The first U.S. effort to gather data on psychological disorders was a single category, "idiocy/insanity," in the 1840 census
- **Philippe Pinel (1745–1826):** "The father of modern psychiatry"; following former patient Jean-Baptiste Pussin's example, he removed patients' iron shackles and conversed with them instead of using bleeding, purging, and blistering

## Perspectives on disorders {pp. 554–555}
- Biological, clinical, developmental, cognitive, and social/personality perspectives each explain some disorders better than others; an integrated approach leads to more effective treatments
- Relationships are reciprocal: depression is linked with low serotonin, but being in a leadership position also boosts serotonin
- **Diathesis–stress model:** "Diathesis" means predisposition; biological predispositions interact with a person's experience of stress to produce a disorder
- **Neurodevelopmental disorders:** DSM-5 term for disorders typically diagnosed in childhood that often continue across the life span (e.g., autism spectrum disorder and ADHD)
- We say "a person with autism spectrum disorder" rather than "an autistic person" because people are not their disorders

## Mental health and mental illness (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Mental Health](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health) · [What Is Mental Health?](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health) · [Mental Health Conditions](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions)
- **Mental health (SAMHSA):** Includes our emotional, psychological, and social well-being; it affects how we think, feel, and act, and helps determine how we handle stress, relate to others, and make choices
- Mental health is important at every stage of life, from childhood and adolescence through adulthood
- **Mental illnesses (SAMHSA):** Disorders, ranging from mild to severe, that affect a person's thinking, mood, and/or behavior; according to NIMH, nearly one in five adults live with a mental illness
- About 22% of U.S. adults are affected by mental illness, including depression and anxiety
- **Serious mental illness (SMI):** A level of mental illness that substantially interferes with major life activities (e.g., bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and schizophrenia); more than 5% of adults experience SMI
- Children can have **serious emotional disturbances (SED)**, the term SAMHSA uses alongside SMI for adults
- Factors contributing to mental health conditions: biological factors (genes, physical illness, injury, brain chemistry), life experiences (trauma, a history of abuse), and family history of mental health problems
- SAMHSA's list of mental health topics: antisocial personality disorder, anxiety disorders (generalized anxiety, panic, OCD, phobias, social anxiety), ADHD, bipolar disorder, borderline personality disorder, co-occurring disorders, depression, eating disorders, PTSD, schizophrenia, seasonal affective disorder, and self-harm

## Early warning signs of a mental health problem (SAMHSA)
- Eating or sleeping too much or too little
- Pulling away from people and usual activities
- Having low or no energy; feeling numb or like nothing matters
- Having unexplained aches and pains
- Feeling helpless or hopeless
- Smoking, drinking, or using drugs more than usual
- Feeling unusually confused, forgetful, on edge, angry, upset, worried, or scared
- Yelling or fighting with family and friends; severe mood swings that cause problems in relationships
- Persistent thoughts and memories you can't get out of your head
- Hearing voices or believing things that are not true
- Thinking of harming yourself or others
- Inability to perform daily tasks like taking care of your kids or getting to work or school

## Mental health myths vs. facts (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Get the Facts about Mental Health](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/facts)
- Fact: Mental health issues can affect anyone; in 2020 about 1 in 5 American adults had a mental health condition in a given year, 1 in 6 young people had a major depressive episode, and 1 in 20 Americans lived with a serious mental illness
- Suicide was the second leading cause of death for people ages 10–24 and took more than 45,979 American lives in 2020, nearly double the number lost to homicide
- Fact: Even very young children may show early warning signs; half of all mental health disorders show first signs before age 14, and three-quarters begin before age 24
- Only half of children and adolescents with diagnosable mental health conditions receive the treatment they need
- Fact: Most people with mental health conditions are no more likely to be violent than anyone else; only 3%–5% of violent acts can be attributed to people living with a serious mental illness
- People with severe mental illnesses are over 10 times more likely to be victims of a violent crime than the general population
- Fact: People with mental health conditions can be just as productive as other employees, with attendance, punctuality, motivation, and job tenure on par with or greater than others
- Fact: Mental health conditions have nothing to do with being lazy or weak
- Fact: People with mental health conditions get better; **recovery** refers to the process in which people can live, work, learn, and participate fully in their communities
- Fact: Treatment varies by individual and could include medication, therapy, or both
- In 2020, only 20% of adults received any mental health treatment in the past year, including 10% who received counseling or therapy from a professional
- Friends and family can help by reaching out, helping them access services, helping them learn self-care, sharing facts, treating them with respect, and refusing to define them by their diagnosis or use labels such as "crazy" (use **person-first language** instead)
- Fact: Prevention of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders focuses on addressing known risk factors, such as exposure to trauma
- Promoting social-emotional well-being leads to higher productivity, better educational outcomes, lower crime rates, stronger economies, improved quality of life, increased lifespan, and improved family life

## Tips for living well with a mental health condition (SAMHSA)
- With early and consistent treatment, often a combination of medication and psychotherapy, people can manage conditions and lead meaningful, productive lives
- Stick to a treatment plan: even if you feel better, don't stop therapy or medication without a doctor's guidance
- Keep your primary care physician updated; primary care physicians are an important part of long-term management, even if you also see a psychiatrist
- Learn about the condition; practice good self-care (meditation or tai chi, healthy eating, exercise); reach out to family and friends; develop coping skills
- Get enough sleep: consistently poor sleep is associated with anxiety, depression, and other mental health conditions

## Co-occurring disorders (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Co-Occurring Disorders](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/co-occurring-disorders)
- **Co-occurring disorders:** Mental health problems and substance use disorders occurring together
- Why they co-occur: some substances cause symptoms of a mental health problem; people with mental health problems may misuse substances to **self-medicate**; and both share underlying causes (changes in brain composition, genetic vulnerabilities, and early exposure to stress or trauma)
- More than one in four adults living with serious mental health problems also has a substance use problem
- Substance use problems occur more often with depression, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia, and personality disorders
- Someone with a mental health problem and a substance use disorder must treat both issues (rehabilitation, medications, support groups, and talk therapy)

## SMI and SED definitions (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: SMI and SED](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/serious-mental-illness/about)
- **Serious mental illness (SMI):** Someone over 18 having (within the past year) a diagnosable mental, behavioral, or emotional disorder that substantially interferes with a person's life and ability to function (e.g., bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, schizophrenia)
- **Serious emotional disturbance (SED):** Someone under 18 having (within the past year) a diagnosable mental, behavioral, or emotional disorder that results in functional impairment that substantially interferes with or limits the child's role or functioning in family, school, or community activities
- 2023 NSDUH: 14.6 million adults, or 5.7%, reported having SMI in the past year; an estimated 4.6 million children (5% to 13%) were diagnosed with SED
- **National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH):** SAMHSA's survey that reports national data on mental illness and substance use
- With early and consistent treatment, people with SMI and SED can manage their conditions and lead meaningful, productive lives
- **Interdepartmental Serious Mental Illness Coordinating Committee (ISMICC):** Established in 2017; reports to Congress and federal agencies on meeting the needs of people with SMI and SED and their families

## Understanding mental illness (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Types of Conditions](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/) · [Understanding Your Diagnosis](https://www.nami.org/living-with-a-mental-health-condition/understanding-your-diagnosis/)
- **Mental illness (NAMI):** A condition that affects a person's thinking, feeling, behavior or mood, deeply affecting day-to-day living and possibly the ability to relate to others
- NAMI uses "mental health conditions" and "mental illnesses" interchangeably
- A mental health condition is not the result of one event; genetics, environment and lifestyle all play a role, along with stressful jobs or home lives, traumatic events, biochemical processes and brain structure
- Mental illness is no one's fault, and for many people recovery (including meaningful roles in social life, school and work) is possible, especially with early treatment
- No medical test can diagnose mental illness the way tests diagnose diabetes or cancer; after ruling out medical causes, a mental health professional uses the DSM-5, published by the American Psychiatric Association
- A diagnosis guides treatment, tells insurance companies that care is needed, and is required for Social Security disability support or job protection under the Americans with Disabilities Act
- NAMI calls a diagnosis a starting point for good treatment, not a life sentence; if you disagree with it, ask whether you can work with the doctor and "agree to disagree" while trying their recommendations
- A diagnosis should be reevaluated over time as the professional gathers new information

## Mental health by the numbers (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Mental Health By the Numbers](https://www.nami.org/mental-health-by-the-numbers/)
- NAMI's statistics come from SAMHSA, the CDC, NIMH and other sources, and are used for awareness, fighting stigma and advocacy
- More than 1 in 5 U.S. adults experience mental illness each year (23.4% in 2024, or 61.5 million people)
- More than 1 in 20 U.S. adults experience serious mental illness each year (5.6% in 2024, or 14.6 million people)
- More than 1 in 7 U.S. youth ages 6–17 experience a mental health disorder each year (16.5% in 2016, or 7.7 million)
- 50% of all lifetime mental illness begins by age 14, and 75% by age 24
- Suicide is the 2nd leading cause of death among people ages 10–24

- Annual mental illness rates are 20.0% for males and 26.7% for females (sex assigned at birth), and 53.2% for lesbian, gay or bisexual adults
- In 2024, 52.1% of U.S. adults with mental illness and 70.8% of adults with serious mental illness received treatment; 50.6% of youth 6–17 with a disorder received treatment (2016)
- The average delay between the start of mental illness symptoms and treatment is 11 years
- More than 120 million people live in a designated Mental Health Professional Shortage Area
- Risk of cardiometabolic disease is up to 2 times higher in people with mental illness, and people with depression have a 40% higher risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes and obesity
- 34.5% of U.S. adults with mental illness also have a substance use disorder
- High school students with recent depression symptoms are more than 2 times as likely to drop out, and students 6–17 with mental, emotional or behavioral problems are more than 2 times as likely to repeat a grade
- At least 8.4 million people in the U.S. care for an adult with a mental or emotional health issue, giving an average of 32 hours of unpaid care per week
- In 2024, 18.1% of people experiencing homelessness had a serious mental illness, and 12.3% of adult emergency department visits are related to mental health
- 17.6% of U.S. veterans experienced a mental illness in 2023 (3.5 million people)
- Serious mental illness causes $193.2 billion in lost earnings each year in the U.S., and depression and anxiety cost the global economy $1 trillion in lost productivity each year
- Globally, mental health disorders are the leading cause of years lived with disability
