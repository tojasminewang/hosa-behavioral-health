## Psychology and its roots
- **Psychology:** scientific study of behaviour and mental processes (of mind)
- **Behaviour:** observable action
- **Mental processes:** thoughts and feelings, studied through introspection
- **Hub science:** psychology is one of 7 major hub sciences, linked to the medical sciences, social sciences, and education
- The discipline dates to the 1870s, with 2 major roots: philosophy and the natural sciences
- Shared questions: the self, biology versus environment, mind–body connection, humans and other species
- René Descartes (1596–1650): ideas and emotions are innate; Aristotle (384–322 BCE): all knowledge comes from sensory experience
- **Empiricism:** 17th-century view of the mind as a blank slate at birth, filled by observation
- 17th–18th centuries: new technologies brought discoveries about body and mind
- Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894): speed of nerve signalling showed the mind has a physical basis
- Philosophy and natural science gradually merged into something like today's psychology

| Who | What to remember |
|---|---|
| Ancient Greek philosophers | Explained what they saw with natural, not supernatural, causes |
| British empiricists | Knowledge comes from experience |
| Ancient physicians | The brain is the source of the mind |
| 17th- and 18th-century natural scientists | Findings on sensation and movement showed the mind is physical |
| Hermann von Helmholtz | Reaction-time studies backed the idea of a physical mind |

## Early schools
- **Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920):** credited as the first psychologist; former research assistant to von Helmholtz; ran the first documented experiment; saw mental experience as a hierarchy; linked with structuralism (behaviour can be broken into parts)
- **Structuralism:** Edward Titchener (1867–1923), Wundt's student: the mind breaks down into the smallest elements of mental experience
- Gestalt psychologists (early 20th-century Germans) challenged structuralism; e.g., Max Wertheimer (1880–1943): breaking behaviour into parts loses its meaning
- **Functionalism:** William James (1842–1910): behaviour is purposeful because it led to survival
- 17th–19th centuries: supernatural explanations of disorders gave way to two scientific approaches
- **Medical model:** physical causes and medical treatment of abnormal behaviour
- **Psychological model:** abnormal behaviour from life experiences causing fear, anxiety, other emotions
- **Sigmund Freud (1856–1939):** psychodynamic theory dominated the first half of the 20th century; founded the study of personality; relied on introspection rather than science; popularized psychological explanations of everyday behaviour; his ideas about the unconscious mind, about experience causing abnormal behaviour, and his new therapy methods laid the groundwork for later work on personality and therapy
- **Humanistic psychology:** by 1960, people seen as innately good, self-improving, bad only when corrupted by society
- **Abraham Maslow (1908–1970):** asked what makes a person "good"; major theory of motivation
- **Client-centered therapy:** Carl Rogers (1902–1987): clients have equal standing with the therapist and take an active part
- **Behaviourism:** early 20th century; only observable, measurable behaviour, mostly in animals, generalized to humans
- **Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849–1936):** digestion in dogs; pioneered classical (Pavlovian) conditioning; for behaviourism, experience is the main source of behaviour
- **John B. Watson (1878–1958):** rat experiments; echoed the empiricists' blank slate
- **Edward Thorndike (1874–1949):** proposed the law of effect (outcomes of a behaviour shape whether it recurs)
- **B. F. Skinner:** effects of reward and punishment on future behaviour
- **Ulric Neisser (1928–2012):** led the cognitive revolution: private mental processing can be studied scientifically

## Six perspectives
| Perspective | Focus |
|---|---|
| Biological psychology | Mind and behaviour and their biology (genetics, biochemistry, anatomy, physiology); also called behavioural neuroscience |
| Evolutionary psychology | How structure and behaviour were shaped by survival and reproduction |
| Cognitive psychology | Information processing, thinking, reasoning, problem solving |
| Social psychology | Effects of the social environment on individuals; how other people's presence changes behaviour |
| Developmental psychology | Normal behaviour changes across the lifespan |
| Clinical psychology | Explaining, defining, treating abnormal behaviour; also promotes general well-being |

- No single perspective fully explains a phenomenon; combining them is more holistic; zoomed-in, specialized views greatly increased understanding, and 21st-century psychology works to integrate cross-cutting perspectives, all resting on scientific research methods
- The summary slide pairs biological with evolutionary psychology and adds an individual (personality) perspective: behaviour varies around averages, and individual differences often interact with environments
