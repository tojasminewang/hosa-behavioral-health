## Sensation basics {pp. 150–152}
- **Sensation:** The process of detecting environmental stimuli or stimuli arising from the body
- **Perception:** The process of interpreting sensory information
- **Stimulus:** Anything that elicits a reaction from our sensory systems
- **Transduction:** The process of translating a stimulus into a neural signal the nervous system can understand
- **Sensory adaptation:** A reduced response to an unchanging stimulus (e.g., ignoring the heater's running noise; not seeing the blood vessels in your own eye)
- Differences in sensation from person to person are relatively subtle; individual differences become more evident in perception
- Sensory systems were shaped by natural selection: dogs hear high-pitched sounds better than humans, insects see ultraviolet light, and some snakes (vipers, boas, pythons) sense infrared energy to find prey

## Measuring sensation (psychophysics) {pp. 153–155}
- **Psychophysics:** The study of relationships between the physical qualities of stimuli and the subjective responses they produce; developed by Gustav Fechner (1801–1887)
- **Absolute threshold:** The smallest amount of stimulus that can be detected (at least 50% of the time)
- **Difference threshold:** The smallest detectable difference between two stimuli (at least 50% of the time); as stimuli get larger, differences must also get larger to be noticed
- Absolute thresholds under ideal conditions: vision = a candle flame 30 miles away on a dark, clear night; hearing = a mosquito about 10 feet away; touch = a fly's wing falling on your cheek from 0.4 inches; smell = a drop of perfume in a six-room apartment; sweetness = one teaspoon of sugar in two gallons of water
- **Signal detection:** Correctly identifying when a faint stimulus is or is not present; a two-step process involving (a) the actual intensity of the stimulus and (b) the observer's own criteria for deciding whether it occurred

| Participant response | Stimulus present | Stimulus absent |
|---|---|---|
| "Yes" | Hit | False alarm |
| "No" | Miss | Correct rejection |

- A radiologist afraid of missing a tumor will have a high hit rate but a high false alarm rate; a radiologist who needs more certainty will have fewer false alarms but a higher miss rate
- Jury example: convicting an innocent person is a false alarm; letting a real criminal go is a miss

## The visual stimulus {p. 156}
- **Vision:** The sense that allows us to process reflected light
- About 50% of the cerebral cortex processes visual information, compared with only 3% for hearing and 11% for touch and pain
- Visible light is the part of the electromagnetic spectrum our eyes respond to; gamma rays, x-rays, ultraviolet, infrared, microwaves, and radio waves are outside human vision
- **Wavelength:** The distance between successive peaks of a light wave; decoded as color (or shades of gray)
- **Amplitude:** The height of a light wave; decoded as brightness (large amplitude = bright, low amplitude = dim)

## Structures of the eye {pp. 157–159}
- **Cornea:** The clear surface at the front of the eye that begins the process of bending (directing) light to the retina
- **Pupil:** An opening formed by the iris
- **Iris:** The colored circular muscle surrounding the pupil ("rainbow" in Greek); adjusts the pupil to the amount of light and to signals from the autonomic nervous system (arousal = dilated pupils, relaxation = constricted pupils)
- **Lens:** The clear structure behind the pupil that bends light toward the retina; muscles change its shape to accommodate (focus on near or distant objects)
- **Retina:** Layers of visual processing cells in the back of the eye
- **Optic disk:** Where blood vessels enter and the optic nerve exits the eye; it has no rods or cones, so each eye has a blind spot (perception fills in the missing details)
- **Fovea:** An area of the retina specialized for highly detailed (central) vision
- The image projected on the retina is upside down and reversed; the brain interprets it in the correct orientation

## Photoreceptors {pp. 158–159}
- The retina has three types of photoreceptors: rods, cones, and a small group of special ganglion cells that respond to brightness
- Each eye contains over 110 million rods, about 6 million cones, and only about 50,000 of the special ganglion cells
- **Rod:** A photoreceptor specialized to detect dim light; more sensitive to light than cones but gives no color and no sharp images; rods are more common toward the periphery of the retina
- **Cone:** A photoreceptor in the retina that processes color and fine detail; works best in bright light
- Peripheral vision sees dim light better than central vision, so soldiers on night patrol were trained to look to the side of a target
- Under starlight, normal vision is about 20/200 instead of daylight 20/20
- The special ganglion cells help set our biological clocks, maintain pupil reflexes, and assess brightness

## Visual pathways {p. 159}
- **Optic nerve:** The nerve exiting the retina of the eye, formed by the axons of ganglion cells
- **Optic chiasm:** The X-shaped point where the optic nerves cross the midline; axons closest to the nose cross to the other hemisphere
- **Optic tracts:** Nerve pathways traveling from the optic chiasm to the thalamus, hypothalamus, and midbrain
- Looking straight ahead, everything left of center is processed by the right hemisphere, and everything right of center by the left hemisphere
- About 90% of optic tract axons synapse in the thalamus, which sends visual information to the amygdala (quick emotional judgments) and the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe
- Other optic tract fibers go to the hypothalamus (light information for sleep–wake cycles) and the superior colliculi of the midbrain (visually guided reflexes such as pupil size)
- The primary visual cortex responds to object shape, location, movement, and color; a parietal pathway processes movement and a temporal pathway processes shape and color for recognizing objects and faces

## The auditory stimulus {pp. 172–173}
- **Audition:** The sense of hearing; Helen Keller said blindness separated her from things, while deafness separated her from people
- Sound is waves of vibration made by collisions between molecules in air, liquid, or solids, so sound cannot occur in the vacuum of space
- Sound wave amplitude is perceived as loudness (measured in decibels, dB); frequency is perceived as pitch (measured in hertz, Hz)
- High-frequency waves sound high pitched; low-frequency waves sound low pitched
- **Ultrasound:** Frequencies above the range of human hearing, beginning around 20,000 Hz (used to clean jewelry and teeth and for medical images)
- **Infrasound:** Frequencies below the range of human hearing (less than 20 Hz); used by elephants and marine mammals to communicate; can make people feel chills, fear, or revulsion

## Structures of the ear {pp. 172–175}
- The ear has three parts: the outer ear, the middle ear, and the inner ear
- **Pinna:** The visible outer structure of the ear; collects and focuses sound like a funnel and helps localize sounds above or below the head
- **Tympanic membrane:** The eardrum, at the end of the auditory canal, at the boundary between the outer and middle ear
- **Oval window:** The membrane forming the boundary between the middle and inner ear
- Tiny bones of the middle ear bridge the gap between the eardrum and the oval window, transferring sound energy from air to the fluid of the inner ear
- **Cochlea:** The fluid-filled, snail-shaped ("cochlea" = snail in Greek) structure of the inner ear, about the size of a pea, that contains the auditory receptor cells
- **Basilar membrane:** Membrane in the cochlea on which the organ of Corti is located
- **Organ of Corti:** A structure on the basilar membrane that contains auditory receptors (rows of hair cells; about 15,500 per ear) that transduce sound energy into neural signals
- **Auditory nerve:** Nerve carrying sound information from the cochlea to the brain
- Auditory pathway: hair cells → auditory nerve → medulla → midbrain (reflexive turning toward sounds, sound localization) → thalamus → primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe
- Secondary auditory cortex surrounds the primary auditory cortex and responds to complex sounds (clicks, noise, patterned sounds)
- The fetus can hear outside sounds, such as the mother's voice, during the final trimester; fetal hearing is functional at 24 weeks of gestation

## Body senses (somatosensation) {pp. 178–182}
- **Somatosensation:** The senses of body position and movement, touch, skin temperature, and pain ("soma" = body)
- People who cannot feel pain often die prematurely because they cannot respond to injury
- **Vestibular system:** Structures in the inner ear that provide information about body position and movement (head position, linear acceleration, rotation of the head)
  - Its hair cells bend in fluid when the head moves; signals travel in the auditory nerve to the medulla and cerebellum
  - Rotating the head causes a reflexive eye movement in the opposite direction to keep a steady view
- Skin is the largest and heaviest organ in the human body; its receptors respond to pressure, vibration, stretch, pain, and temperature
- Touch pathway: skin → spinal cord → thalamus → primary somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe
- **Sensory homunculus:** A map ("little man") of how body areas are represented in the somatosensory cortex according to their sensitivity rather than their size (e.g., large areas for lips and hands)
- The brain itself has neither touch nor pain receptors, so neurosurgeons can operate on alert patients using local anesthesia
- **Phantom limb:** Sensations (often pain) from a missing body part, caused by reorganization of the somatosensory cortex; the term was first used by a Civil War physician
- The somatosensory cortex is plastic: areas for trained fingertips expand (Braille readers, string players)
- Many individuals with autism spectrum disorder are oversensitive to touch and may reject hugs and cuddling
- Pain receptors (free nerve endings and glial networks) respond to mechanical damage, temperature, or chemicals such as capsaicin (in hot peppers)
- Fast, myelinated axons carry the sharp "ouch"; slower, unmyelinated axons carry dull, aching pain
- Pain pathway: spinal cord → thalamus → anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and amygdala (emotional qualities of pain) and the somatosensory cortex (location and intensity)

## Chemical senses: smell and taste {pp. 183–186}
- **Olfaction:** The sense of smell (Immanuel Kant called it the "most dispensable" sense, yet people who lose it often experience profound depression)
- **Gustation:** The sense of taste
- Olfactory receptors in the nasal cavity regularly die and are replaced in cycles of 4 to 6 weeks
- **Olfactory nerve:** A nerve carrying olfactory information from the olfactory receptors to the olfactory bulbs
- **Olfactory bulb:** One of two structures below the frontal lobes that receive input from the olfactory receptors in the nose
- Unlike most other senses, smell does not go to the thalamus before reaching the cortex; olfactory bulb fibers go to the olfactory cortex and the amygdala, which may explain strong emotional reactions to odors
- The five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami ("savory" or "meaty"); the mouth also has receptors for carbohydrates and capsaicin
- **Papillae:** Small bumps on the tongue that contain taste buds (1 to 100 taste buds each)
- **Taste bud:** A structure in the papillae containing 50 to 150 taste receptor cells; taste buds are replaced after a limited life
- Receptors for all tastes are spread equally across the tongue; the "taste map" of the tongue is a myth
- Taste pathway: mouth and tongue → medulla → thalamus → insula, lower somatosensory cortex, and orbitofrontal cortex (pleasantness of taste)
- The average person has about 6,000 taste buds
- **Supertasters:** About 25% of people are extrasensitive to taste (more papillae, so more taste buds); 25% are nontasters and 50% are in between; supertasters tend to dislike bitter foods and were often picky eaters

## Developmental and individual differences in sensation {pp. 166–188}
- Infants see everything adults see but with less detail and need more contrast (they prefer large, high-contrast objects)
- With age, lens accommodation slows (beginning in middle adulthood), pupils stay smaller, and the lens yellows
- **Nearsightedness:** Elongated eyeballs interfere with seeing distant objects
- **Farsightedness:** Shortened eyeballs interfere with seeing close-up objects
- **Astigmatism:** An uneven surface of the cornea
- Age-related hearing loss occurs first at higher frequencies: after 30 most people cannot hear above 15,000 Hz, after 50 above 12,000 Hz, and after 70 they have difficulty above 6,000 Hz
- Newborns respond preferentially to their native language at 2 days old; by 1 year children should reliably turn when their name is called
- Newborns can tell sweet, bitter, and sour tastes apart but seem relatively oblivious to salty tastes; taste buds decrease with age, and smell sensitivity also decreases
- Females are generally more sensitive to smell than males
