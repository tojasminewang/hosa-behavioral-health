## What is stress? {pp. 639–641}
- **Stress:** An unpleasant emotional state that results from the perception of danger
- **Stressor:** A stimulus that serves as a source of stress
- The key word is perception: stressors are highly individual (one person's phobia is another person's pet)
- Brief stressors are short-term challenges (an exam, a job interview, a close call on the highway); chronic stressors are ongoing and long-lasting and often require rethinking our identities and roles; stressors can also arrive in sequence
- Stress can be positive: it mobilizes resources, motivates good performance (exercise, working under time pressure), and makes people more prosocial and empathic with people they know well
- Stress becomes harmful when we cannot cope or adapt; the diathesis–stress model links stress to MDD, schizophrenia, and PTSD
- **Stress mindset:** Whether you believe the effects of stress are enhancing or debilitating (college students averaged on the "stress is debilitating" side)

## The stress response and the GAS {pp. 640–643}
- **Fight-or-flight response:** Walter Cannon showed extreme cold, lack of oxygen, and emotional experiences activate the sympathetic nervous system: heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration increase; nonessential functions like digestion are inhibited; stored energy is released and blood is shunted to the muscles
- **Hans Selye:** Studied strong, long-lasting stressors in rats (cold water, restraint, shock, surgery, cut whiskers) and measured how long they would swim; regardless of the stressor, rats showed a consistent pattern
- **General adaptation syndrome (GAS):** Hans Selye's three-stage model for an organism's response to stressors: alarm, resistance, and exhaustion
- **Alarm reaction:** The first stage of the GAS, characterized by sympathetic arousal and mental clarity (essentially Cannon's fight-or-flight response)
- **Resistance:** The second stage of the GAS, characterized by coping with ongoing stress; arousal and rest functions run at the same time, so neither works smoothly and judgment may suffer
- **Exhaustion:** The third and last stage of the GAS, characterized by depletion of physical and psychological resources; resembles MDD and can even lead to death
- Caged lower-status baboons in Kenya, unable to escape higher-status baboons, died from stress-related conditions such as cardiovascular disease

## Sources of stress {pp. 643–645}
- **Cognitive appraisal (Richard Lazarus):** We make rapid initial assessments of potential stressors (irrelevant, harmless, positive, or negative), so the same event stresses people differently
- Disasters: 90% of Americans felt unusually stressed in the 3 to 5 days after 9/11; one-quarter to one-third of the population reported higher stress during COVID-19
- Stress can cross generations: infants of pregnant women who developed PTSD after witnessing 9/11 showed signs of chronic stress (cortisol crosses the placenta)
- After the 2004 tsunami, 14% to 39% of Sri Lankan children had PTSD (about 10% of the general population is expected after most disasters)
- **Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS):** Holmes and Rahe (1967) listed life events, good and bad, by the amount of adjustment required (e.g., getting married ranks as more stressful than being fired); suggests spreading controllable changes out over time
- Critics argue that negative items drive most SRRS stress; good events may be stressful mainly when they fail to meet expectations
- **Hassles:** Relatively insignificant sources of stress (long lines, lost keys, parking tickets) that add up and predict psychological symptoms even apart from major events
- About 38% of living U.S. veterans have been exposed to combat; younger combat veterans had higher rates of lifetime PTSD (30.6%), current PTSD (19.2%), and suicidal ideation (18.6%)
- Loneliness may be an early warning sign of mental health problems in the military; soldiers who died by suicide had reported being lonelier and more depressed

## The biology of stress {pp. 645–649}
- The amygdala provides a rapid "fear circuit" assessment of danger; sensory input can reach it separately from the cortex, so we may feel frightened before understanding why
- The amygdala signals the hypothalamus, which commands the sympathetic nervous system
- **Sympathetic adrenal–medullary (SAM) system:** A circuit that responds to perceived stressors by initiating the release of epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine into the bloodstream from the adrenal glands (fast, short-lived effects such as a pounding heart)
- **Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis:** A circuit that responds to perceived stressors by initiating the release of cortisol into the bloodstream (hypothalamus → pituitary → adrenal glands); slower and longer-lasting, linked to the effects of chronic stress
- **Cortisol:** A hormone released into the bloodstream from the adrenal glands; boosts available energy; levels are naturally high in the morning
- Prolonged high cortisol can kill neurons and inhibit neurogenesis; Cushing's disease (high cortisol) causes reduced hippocampus volume, memory problems, abnormal sleep, and depression
- The hippocampus acts like a "rev limiter": it detects high stress hormones and signals the hypothalamus to reduce cortisol; overwhelming this feedback loop may bridge stress and MDD
- **Epigenetics of stress:** Well-nurtured rats and children show more resilience to later stress; severe early stress increases HPA responses, norepinephrine hyperactivity, smaller hippocampus volume, and stronger amygdala responses to threat
- The first 1,000 days after conception (until about age 2) strongly influence vulnerability to chronic disease and life expectancy
- **Tend-and-befriend:** An alternative to fight-or-flight, characterized by soothing and building social connections, possibly more typical of females; linked to oxytocin (Shelley Taylor)
- Socioeconomic status: lower SES is linked to worse health (more noise, toxins, violence, less health care, less control, and less trust, all of which raise stress); subjective social status also predicts disease

## How stress affects health {pp. 650–655}
- Chronic stress keeps us in Selye's resistance phase and accelerates aging, including shortening of telomeres (the "caps" on chromosomes)
- **Immune system:** The body system that defends against infection and cancer
- **Lymphocyte:** White blood cells that are products of the immune system; stress hormones directly suppress their activity
- Short bursts of stress can enhance immune responses and wound healing; chronic stress reduces immune responses, increasing colds, flu, herpes flare-ups, mononucleosis, and progression of HIV to AIDS
- Chronic stressors affecting social roles (death of a loved one, divorce) cause the greatest immune suppression, especially when they are uncontrollable and hopeless
- **Type A personality:** A competitive, workaholic, and in some cases hostile personality type (Friedman and Rosenman, 1974)
- **Type B personality:** A mellow, laid-back personality type
- Competitiveness itself had little to do with heart disease; the risk came from hostility (suspicion, anger, and resentment of others)
- Frequent conflict with one's social network raised the risk of death 200% to 300% in middle-aged adults
- Mental stress reduced blood vessels' ability to expand by 50% for 45 minutes; blood pressure reactivity to stress predicted later hypertension
- Monkeys raised by peers or surrogates had immune genes shifted toward inflammation and away from antiviral defense, helping explain poor adult health after adverse childhood experiences
- Stress disturbs mood, sleep, and appetite together; cortisol late in the evening makes sleep difficult; stress hormones make fat cells grow in size and number
- Sleep-deprived dieters lost only half as much fat as well-rested dieters, and three-quarters of their loss was bone and muscle
- A one-time "belongingness" intervention (framing worries about fitting in as common and temporary) raised African American Stanford students' GPAs and happiness and reduced doctor visits over three years

## Managing stress (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Managing Stress](https://www.nami.org/living-with-a-mental-health-condition/managing-stress/)
- Stress can help you focus, but frequent, intense stress strains the body and can make it impossible to function
- Common signs of stress: headaches, trouble sleeping, jaw pain, appetite changes, mood swings, difficulty concentrating and feeling overwhelmed
- Long-term stress exposes the brain to more cortisol, which weakens the immune system and makes it easier to get sick
- Stress can worsen mental illness: in schizophrenia it can encourage hallucinations and delusions, and in bipolar disorder it can trigger mania and depression
- People are most vulnerable to stress when they lack sleep, lack a support network, face a major life change (moving, a death, a new job, a child, marriage), have poor physical health or don't eat well
- Everyone has their own stress threshold; some people are stressed by crowds and noise, others by silence and free time
- NAMI ways to reduce stress: accept your needs (know your triggers), manage your time, practice relaxation, exercise daily, set aside time for yourself, eat well, get enough sleep, avoid alcohol and drugs, and talk to someone
- Daily exercise naturally produces stress-relieving hormones, and too little sleep can trigger symptoms such as mania in bipolar disorder
- Alcohol and drugs do not actually reduce stress and often make it worse

- HOSA Canada notes that wellness, stress and coping make up 15% of the written test
- Stress hormones directly suppress the activity of lymphocytes (white blood cells that protect us from invading organisms), which is one reason chronic stress makes people more vulnerable to infections such as colds
- A short stressful memory task actually improved immune responses (measured by markers in saliva), but the immune system works less well under long-term, chronic stress
- Stress puts the cardiovascular system at risk by affecting the ability of blood vessels to expand when needed
- People of lower socioeconomic status face more noise, toxins, carcinogens and violence, fewer resources, less health care, more substance abuse, less exercise, more anger, less control and less trust, all of which are linked to high stress (Adler, 2013)
