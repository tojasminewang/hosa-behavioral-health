## Key ideas about psychoactive drugs (textbook) {pp. 215–216}
- **Psychoactive drug:** Any drug with the capability of altering a person's state of consciousness; psychoactive drugs penetrate the blood–brain barrier with ease
- Humans may be unique among animals in voluntarily seeking altered consciousness with psychoactive substances; most psychoactive substances are poisonous and bitter
- **Tolerance:** The need to administer greater quantities of a drug to achieve the same subjective effect; the nervous system compensates (biologically and through learning) to maintain homeostasis
- **Withdrawal:** Physical responses to the removal of some habitually administered drugs; the effects are the opposite of the drug's effects
  - Withdrawal from alcohol (which slows the nervous system) can cause life-threatening seizures; withdrawal from caffeine makes people sluggish
  - Heroin and nicotine produce powerful withdrawal effects, but cocaine usually does not
- **Addiction:** A compulsive physical or psychological dependence on a substance or activity that continues in spite of negative consequences; people choose short-term outcomes (getting high) over long-term outcomes (family, finances, job)
- Addiction involves three neural systems: an impulsive system (dopamine pathways connecting the midbrain and basal ganglia, including the nucleus accumbens), a reflective system in the frontal lobes (weighs pros and cons, controls impulses), and a craving system involving the insula
- Strokes that damage the insula eliminate the urge to use some addictive drugs
- Nearly all Vietnam veterans who used heroin while serving quit after returning home, a reminder that addiction has multiple causes

## Hallucinogens: cannabis, LSD, and others (textbook) {pp. 216–219}
- **Hallucinogen:** A drug that stimulates the experience of false perceptions
- Cannabis (from the Cannabis sativa plant) appears in a pharmacy written by Chinese emperor Shen Neng nearly 5,000 years ago; marijuana is the most commonly used federally illegal substance in the U.S.
- Cannabis distorts time and space and produces hallucinations in some users, so it is formally classified as a hallucinogen
- **Cannabinoids:** The more than 50 psychoactive compounds in cannabis; the most important is tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)
- Cannabinoid receptors are numerous in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex and are involved with pain, appetite, learning, reasoning, and movement
- **Anandamide:** Our main endogenous cannabinoid; removes unnecessary memories and slows movement; it increases dopamine, and because THC shares this ability, cannabis can produce dependency
- **LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide):** Discovered by Albert Hoffman (1938); chemically similar to serotonin and acts on serotonin receptors; can cause flashbacks (intrusive, involuntary hallucinations after the drug is stopped)
- Most hallucinogens (including DMT in ayahuasca and MDMA) interact with serotonin systems
- Researchers are studying LSD, ketamine, psilocybin (from mushrooms), DMT, and MDMA to treat major depressive disorder, anxiety, PTSD, and substance use disorders; challenges include small samples, no believable placebo, and the risk of psychosis in people at risk for schizophrenia
- **Entheogens:** Hallucinogens used in religious contexts ("generating the divine within"); in 2006 the U.S. Supreme Court allowed ayahuasca churches to use the tea in ceremonies

## Stimulants (textbook) {pp. 218–222}
- **Stimulant:** Any drug that increases the activity of the nervous system
- **Caffeine:** Used by 85% of U.S. adults; interferes with the inhibition normally produced by adenosine, increasing excitation and alertness
  - Withdrawal causes headaches and fatigue; caffeine crosses the placenta and enters breast milk
  - Caffeine use is correlated with lower risk of Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and other dementias
- **Nicotine:** The most commonly used stimulant after caffeine; mimics acetylcholine, increasing heart rate and blood pressure, reducing fatigue, improving cognition, and relaxing muscles (a unique combination of alertness and relaxation)
  - Use usually begins in adolescence, and adolescents become dependent faster than adults; e-cigarette use is growing because of an inaccurate belief in their "safety"
  - Adolescent nicotine use doubles a person's risk of developing schizophrenia (a correlation)
  - Smoking raises blood nicotine faster than chewing tobacco, but chewing produces higher sustained levels
- **Cocaine and amphetamines:** At moderate doses produce alertness, elevated mood, confidence, and well-being; at higher doses they produce schizophrenia-like symptoms (hallucinations such as bugs on the skin, paranoid delusions, teeth grinding)
  - Cocaine blocks dopamine transporters (reuptake); methamphetamine makes the transporters work in reverse, trapping dopamine in the synaptic gap
  - Methamphetamine is especially likely to cause psychosis and "meth mouth" dental decay; these drugs are among the most addictive substances
  - Freud originally believed cocaine was an effective antidepressant
- **Methylphenidate (Ritalin) and Adderall:** Stimulant medications for ADHD that boost dopamine and norepinephrine; at clinical doses they do not seem to produce dependence; everyone (not only people with ADHD) responds with increased concentration
- **MDMA (Ecstasy):** A relative of amphetamines and mescaline; increases heart rate, blood pressure, and body temperature and increases sociability by stimulating serotonin and oxytocin
  - **Serotonin syndrome:** Excess serotonin activity (from MDMA, antidepressant overdose, or drug combinations) causing dehydration, exhaustion, hyperthermia, convulsions, and death
  - MDMA has been used experimentally for alcohol use disorder and autism-related anxiety; its use for PTSD awaits FDA approval

## Depressants and opioids (textbook) {pp. 222–223}
- **Alcohol:** One of the earliest psychoactive drugs; low doses dilate blood vessels (warm, flushed feeling) and reduce anxiety; higher doses lead to aggression, risky behavior, and poor coordination; very high doses can cause coma and death
- Alcohol boosts GABA (the main inhibitory neurochemical), blocks receptors for glutamate (the main excitatory neurotransmitter), and stimulates dopamine reward pathways (its addictive potential)
- **Opioids:** Natural or synthetic substances that interact with endorphin receptors
- **Opiates:** Opioids derived from the opium poppy (morphine, codeine); heroin is synthesized from morphine
- Opioids cause large dopamine release (well-being and pain relief); medical uses include pain control, cough suppression, and control of diarrhea
- High doses produce a strong, rapid "rush" of euphoria; even higher doses strongly depress breathing and can cause death
- The U.S. "opioid epidemic" (since the 1990s) killed 700,000 people between 1999 and 2017 (about 130 per day) in three waves: prescription opioids, then heroin, then synthetic opioids such as fentanyl

## Substance use and addiction (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Substance Use](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use) · [What Is Substance Use Disorder?](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/what-is-sud)
- In 2023, more than 1 in 10 U.S. adults perceived that they ever had a substance use problem; more than 7 in 10 of them considered themselves to be in recovery
- Many people mistakenly believe addiction results from a lack of willpower or moral principles; in reality, **addiction is a complex disease that alters the brain**, making it hard to quit even for those who want to stop
- **Drug addiction (SAMHSA):** A chronic disease in which people compulsively seek and use drugs despite harmful consequences; repeated use changes the brain, making it hard to resist intense cravings
- Addiction is considered a **"relapsing" disease**: people may return to drug use even after long periods of sobriety; relapse is common but does not mean treatment failed, and treatment must be ongoing and adapted like other chronic illnesses
- Drugs target the brain's **reward system**, flooding it with dopamine; they "hijack" a system that normally motivates healthy behaviors like eating or spending time with loved ones
- Over time the brain reduces its response to dopamine, leading to **tolerance** (needing more of the drug for the same high) and less ability to enjoy other activities
- Drug use also disrupts brain areas for learning, judgment, decision-making, stress management, memory, and behavior
- Three key addiction risk factors: **biology** (genetics account for about half of a person's risk; gender, ethnicity, and mental health also matter), **environment** (family, friends, economic conditions, peer pressure, abuse, early exposure to drugs), and **development** (drug use during adolescence raises risk because teen brains are still developing decision-making and self-control areas)
- Addiction can be effectively managed: combining medications with behavioral therapy is the most effective approach, tailored to the individual
- Drug use can often be prevented: education and outreach involving families, schools, and communities are effective; parents, teachers, and health care providers play a critical role

## Alcohol (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Alcohol](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/alcohol)
- There is no known safe amount of alcohol use during pregnancy or while trying to get pregnant
- Signs of drinking too much: drinking more or longer than intended, being unable to cut down, needing more for the same effect, continuing to drink despite depression or anxiety, comments from loved ones, spending a lot of time drinking, drinking interfering with daily life, legal problems, and withdrawal symptoms (shakiness, sweating, tremors, headaches, anxiety, irritability, insomnia)
- Check your alcohol use: set daily and weekly limits, write down and record your drinking, avoid triggers, ask a friend to help, and talk with a doctor
- Alcohol treatment can include counseling, medications, and/or mutual-support groups
- Policies with the strongest evidence for reducing alcohol misuse: regulating alcohol outlet density, minimum legal purchase age, limiting days or hours of sales, increasing alcohol taxes, minimum pricing, limiting advertising and marketing, and **dram shop (commercial host) liability laws**

## Marijuana and CBD (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Marijuana and CBD](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/marijuana-cbd) · [Marijuana Risks](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/marijuana/risks)
- **Marijuana:** Products from the cannabis plant (Cannabis sativa or Cannabis indica) that contain THC (tetrahydrocannabinol), the chemical that causes the "high"; marijuana contains close to 500 chemicals
- **Cannabidiol (CBD):** Another cannabis compound that does not produce a "high"; CBD products are not regulated by federal standards for content, purity, or potency
- Marijuana is the most commonly used psychoactive substance (and most commonly used illegal substance) in the U.S.; in 2024 over 64 million people aged 12+ used it in the past year, and 20.6 million had a past-year cannabis use disorder
- Today's marijuana is much more potent: it has about three times the THC concentration of 25 years ago; concentrates, vape cartridges, oils, and edibles often contain much more THC than flower
- Marijuana use is rising among all adult age groups, both sexes, and pregnant women, while perceived harm is declining; people ages 18–25 have the highest rate of use
- Marijuana and THC remain illegal at the federal level even though many states have legalized them
- About **1 in 10** people who use marijuana will become addicted; for those who start before age 18, the rate rises to **1 in 6**
- Starting young can cause permanent IQ loss of as much as 8 points that does not return after quitting
- Marijuana use is linked to depression, anxiety, suicide planning, and psychotic episodes (cause not known); it impairs timing, coordination, and driving (slower reactions, lane weaving)
- Use during pregnancy may cause fetal growth restriction, premature birth, stillbirth, and brain development problems; THC passes through breast milk
- Higher-risk groups: children and adolescents, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, older adults, and people with certain mental health conditions
- Edibles can look like ordinary food; if a child eats a marijuana product, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 (911 for severe symptoms)

## Hallucinogens (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Hallucinogens](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/hallucinogens)
- **Hallucinogens (SAMHSA):** Mind-altering substances that can change a person's perceptions, thoughts, mood, and sense of reality
- In 2024, 73.6 million people aged 12+ used an illicit drug in the past year, including 10.4 million who used hallucinogens; hallucinogen use has been rising

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Classic psychedelics | LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), psilocybin (magic mushrooms), mescaline, peyote |
| Dissociative drugs | PCP (phencyclidine), ketamine |
| Other substances with hallucinogenic effects | MDMA (ecstasy or molly) |

- Short-term effects: altered perception, mood, and sense of time; confusion; anxiety or panic; dilated pupils; increased heart rate, blood pressure, and body temperature; nausea; tremors
- **Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD):** A rare condition causing recurring visual disturbances (flashes of light, halos) after the drug wears off
- About 433,000 people had a past-year hallucinogen use disorder in 2024
- Call 911 if someone who used a hallucinogen has trouble breathing, cannot be awakened, has a seizure, severe agitation, very high body temperature, or delirium

## Prescription drugs and opioids (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Prescriptions and Opioids](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/prescriptions-opioids)
- **Prescription drug misuse:** Taking a medication the wrong way: too much, too often, or too long; for the feeling it gives; taking someone else's medication; taking it other than directed (such as crushing pills); or mixing it with alcohol or drugs
- Commonly misused prescriptions: pain relievers (opioids), stimulants (ADHD medication), and sedatives and tranquilizers (for anxiety or sleep)
- Questions to ask a doctor about pain treatment: Is it an opioid? What non-opioid options exist? How do I stop safely? What should I expect?
- Opioid overdose is life-threatening and can happen even with a prescription opioid; **naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose**
- **Fake pills:** Criminal networks mass-produce counterfeit pills that look like oxycodone, hydrocodone, alprazolam (Xanax), or Adderall, often sold on social media; they often contain fentanyl
- DEA tests found 6 out of every 10 fentanyl pills contain a potentially lethal dose; a lethal dose of fentanyl (2 milligrams) is about the size of 10–15 grains of table salt
- Animal tranquilizers, even more potent than fentanyl, have been found in fake pills
- Safe medications come from a licensed medical professional, are prescribed to you, and are dispensed by a licensed pharmacist

## Stimulants and other drug risks (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Stimulants](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/stimulants) · [Know the Risks](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/risks)
- **Methamphetamine (meth):** A synthetic, addictive stimulant that can be smoked, snorted, injected, or taken orally; it rapidly releases high levels of dopamine into reward areas
- Meth speeds up the body to dangerous levels (blood pressure, heart and breathing rates); about 2 million people aged 12+ use meth each year and about 500 people try it for the first time each day
- Short-term meth effects: higher blood pressure and body temperature, fast breathing, irregular heartbeat, loss of appetite, disturbed sleep, erratic or violent behavior
- Long-term meth effects: permanent heart and brain damage, heart attacks and strokes, liver, kidney, and lung damage, psychotic symptoms that can last months or years, skin sores from itching, premature osteoporosis, and severe dental problems
- There are no FDA-approved medications for meth addiction; effective behavioral therapies include CBT and **motivational incentives** (vouchers or rewards earned for not using)
- Young adults 18–25 have the highest rates of drug use, but use among adults 26–49 is rising
- Cocaine is involved in nearly one in five overdose deaths; prescription and illicit opioids are the top cause of overdose deaths
- 81,230 U.S. drug overdose deaths occurred in the 12 months ending May 2020, the highest single year ever reported at that time

## Tobacco, e-cigarettes, and vaping (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Tobacco, E-cigarettes, and Vaping](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/learn/tobacco-vaping)
- E-cigarette aerosol can contain addictive nicotine, cancer-causing chemicals, heavy metals (nickel, tin, lead), flavorings such as diacetyl (linked to serious lung disease), volatile organic compounds, and ultrafine particles
- Commercial tobacco smoke contains more than 7,000 chemicals, hundreds toxic and about 70 that can cause cancer
- There is no safe level of exposure to secondhand smoke; it can cause heart disease, stroke, and lung cancer in nonsmokers, and children are especially vulnerable
- Smoking is the leading cause of preventable death in the U.S. and harms nearly every organ in the body
- When you quit: in minutes heart rate drops; in 24 hours blood nicotine drops to zero; in several days carbon monoxide drops to nonsmoker levels; over time risks of heart disease, stroke, and cancer decrease
- Quit resources: smokefree.gov, text QUIT to 47848, or call 800-QUIT-NOW (800-784-8669) for your state's quitline; medications include the patch, gum, lozenge, inhaler, and nasal spray

## Smoking and mental illness (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Smoking](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/smoking/)
- About 18 million people with mental illness use tobacco, and adults with mental illness smoke cigarettes at higher rates than those without
- People with mental illness or a substance use disorder are 25% of U.S. adults but consume 40% of cigarettes sold
- Nicotine can alter mood in a way that covers up symptoms, which reinforces tobacco use in people with mental illness
- People with mental illness who smoke die up to 15 years earlier than people without mental illness who do not smoke; heart disease, cancer and lung disease are their leading causes of death
- Smoking can decrease the effectiveness of some psychiatric medications, so a higher dose may be needed
- After quitting smoking: coughing and shortness of breath drop in the first month, heart attack risk drops after one year, stroke risk after five years, and lung cancer risk is dramatically reduced after 10 years
- Vaping is not safe: e-liquids contain nicotine (which can cause dependence) and chemicals such as propylene glycol and glycerol that are not proven safe to inhale, and e-cigarettes are not FDA-approved quitting devices
- In 2020, 3.6 million middle and high school students used e-cigarettes; youth ages 12–17 who had a major depressive episode are 2.5 times more likely to smoke
- As many as 70–85% of people with schizophrenia and 50–70% of people with bipolar disorder smoke
- About 70% of people with mental illness who smoke want to quit; cessation treatments are safe and do not increase symptoms in people with serious mental illness, and treating the mental illness at the same time works best
- **Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT):** Gum, patches, lozenges, nasal sprays or inhalers that deliver measured nicotine to ease withdrawal; it raises the chance of quitting by about 50%, and using more than one NRT at once is safe
- Two FDA-approved quit-smoking medications: varenicline (Chantix), which makes people 3 times more likely to quit, and bupropion (Zyban, Wellbutrin), which makes them 2 times more likely; bupropion can interact with MAOIs
- Only 62% of psychiatrists ask about tobacco use or advise patients to stop smoking
- Free quit resources: 1-800-QUIT-NOW, Smokefree.gov, and texting QUIT to 47848 (SmokefreeTXT)
