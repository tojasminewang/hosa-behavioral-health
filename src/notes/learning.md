## Reflexes, instincts, and learning {pp. 278–279}
- Behaviors fall into three broad categories: reflexes, instincts, and learned behaviors
- **Reflex:** An inevitable, involuntary response to a stimulus (e.g., the knee-jerk reflex); most human reflexes are controlled by circuits in the spinal cord and brainstem
- Reflexes are fast and reliable but inflexible; goosebumps (piloerection) are a leftover reflex from when humans had more body hair
- A batter's "great reflexes" are actually learned behaviors made fast and automatic through practice
- **Instinct:** An inborn pattern of behavior elicited by environmental stimuli; also known as a fixed action pattern; once started, it runs to completion (e.g., a peacock's tail display, human yawning)
- **Learning:** A relatively permanent change in behavior or the capacity for behavior due to experience (changes from maturation, brain damage, moods, or illness are not learning)
- **Imprinting:** A young organism bonds with the adults it first sees; baby swans follow the first thing they see on hatching (human children show it closer to 1 year of age)

## Three main types of learning {pp. 280–281}
- **Associative learning:** The formation of associations, or connections, among stimuli and behaviors; it helps us predict the future based on past experience
- **Classical conditioning:** A type of learning in which associations are formed between two stimuli that occur sequentially in time (e.g., seeing a bee → getting stung)
- **Operant conditioning:** A type of learning in which associations are formed between behaviors and their outcomes (e.g., study hard → good grades)
- **Nonassociative learning:** Learning that involves changes in the magnitude of responses to a single stimulus
- **Habituation:** A simple form of learning in which reactions to repeated stimuli that are unchanging and harmless decrease (e.g., sleeping better the second night in a noisy hotel)
- **Sensitization:** An increased reaction to many stimuli following exposure to one strong stimulus (e.g., jumpiness after an earthquake)
- Habituation occurs with milder stimuli and saves resources; sensitization occurs with stronger stimuli and is useful in dangerous situations
- There is concern that children's emotional responses to violent media will habituate, increasing tolerance for violence
- **Observational learning:** Learning that occurs when one organism watches the actions of another; also known as social learning or modeling

## Classical conditioning basics {pp. 281–283}
- Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849–1936) noticed his dogs salivated before food arrived; classical conditioning is also called Pavlovian conditioning
- Pavlov used a ticking metronome, not a bell (the bell is a myth)
- "Conditioned" means learned; "unconditioned" means reflexive or occurring without learning
- **Unconditioned stimulus (UCS):** A stimulus that elicits a response without prior experience (e.g., food, electric shock)
- **Unconditioned response (UCR):** A response to an unconditioned stimulus that requires no previous experience (e.g., salivating to food)
- **Conditioned stimulus (CS):** An environmental event whose significance is learned through classical conditioning (e.g., the metronome)
- **Conditioned response (CR):** A response learned through classical conditioning (e.g., salivating to the metronome); its appearance tells us learning has occurred
- Classical conditioning explains many learned emotional responses and is used in prepared childbirth methods and the treatment of addiction and unrealistic fears

## Classical conditioning phenomena {pp. 283–288}
- **Acquisition:** The development of a learned response
- **Contiguity:** Closeness in time between the CS and UCS; a CS that comes at the same time as or after the UCS is not a useful signal
- **Contingency:** A correlation between the CS and UCS; Robert Rescorla (1968) showed learning is fastest when the signal reliably predicts the shock 100% of the time
- **Extinction:** The reduction of a learned response; in classical conditioning it occurs when the UCS no longer follows the CS
- Pavlov believed extinction is not forgetting but new learning that overrides old learning
- **Spontaneous recovery:** During extinction training, the reappearance of conditioned responses after periods of rest
- **Inhibition:** A feature of classical conditioning in which a CS predicts the nonoccurrence of a UCS (e.g., zebras learn that a lion calmly drinking is safe)
- **Generalization:** The tendency to respond to stimuli that are similar to an original CS (e.g., a child stung by a bee fears wasps; a combat veteran fears a car backfiring)
- **Discrimination:** A learned ability to distinguish between stimuli (e.g., salivating to a high tone paired with food but not a low tone)
- **Higher-order conditioning:** Learning in which stimuli associated with a CS also elicit CRs (e.g., fearing a dog's doghouse or bark after a dog bite)
- **Latent inhibition:** The slower learning that occurs when a CS is already familiar compared to when it is unfamiliar (you are less likely to blame familiar pizza than unfamiliar food for getting sick)
- **Rescorla–Wagner model (1972):** Learning occurs as a function of how surprising the CS–UCS association is; we learn little about a new signal that adds no new information

## Taste aversion and preparedness {pp. 288–289}
- **Taste aversion:** A classically conditioned dislike of a food (CS) that was paired with illness (UCS)
- **Garcia and Koelling (1966):** Rats quickly associated "tasty" (saccharin) water with nausea from lithium chloride, but associated "bright-noisy" water with shock; some stimulus combinations are learned faster than others
- This challenged Pavlov's view that stimuli are interchangeable and renewed interest in biological preparedness to learn certain things
- Rats (poor vision) easily link taste to illness; birds (excellent vision) easily link visual stimuli to illness (birds avoid monarch butterflies)
- Applied in conservation: coyotes and wolves given sheep meat laced with lithium chloride killed fewer sheep (30% to 60% fewer)

## Applying classical conditioning {pp. 289–292}
- **Little Albert (Watson & Rayner, 1920):** A 9-month-old was conditioned to fear a white rat (CS) by pairing it with a loud noise (UCS); his fear generalized to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat, and a Santa Claus mask
- **Mary Cover Jones:** Reduced 3-year-old Peter's phobia of rabbits by pairing the rabbit with food; called the "Mother of Behavior Therapy"
- **Phobia:** An intense, unrealistic fear
- **Exposure therapy:** Treating phobias by exposing people to the feared stimulus safely until they no longer respond (extinction); it can be full strength (flooding) or gradual
- **Counterconditioning:** The substitution of one CR for another, opposite response
- **Aversion therapy:** An application of counterconditioning in which a CS formerly paired with a pleasurable UCS is instead paired with an unpleasant UCS (e.g., a silver compound that makes cigarettes taste terrible)
- **Systematic desensitization:** A type of counterconditioning in which people relax (often with progressive muscle relaxation) while being gradually exposed to stimuli that elicit fear (in person, through guided imagery, or virtual reality)
- Addiction: drug-related cues become CSs that trigger cravings; abstaining alone does not weaken these associations, so exposure to old cues (people, places, a favorite bar) often leads to relapse; extinction can help addicts continue to abstain
- Opioid overdose and context: the body learns to compensate for a drug in familiar settings; rats given a large heroin dose in a familiar place were less likely to die than rats given it in a new place, so using a drug in an unfamiliar place is a significant overdose risk
- Advertisers pair products with valued stimuli (celebrities, influencers) to improve attitudes; latent inhibition can contribute to prejudice
- Creative people and people with schizophrenia show less latent inhibition (linked to "loosening of associations" in schizophrenia)

## Operant conditioning {pp. 295–300}
- **Law of effect (Edward Thorndike):** Behaviors are "stamped in" depending on their consequences (cats escaping puzzle boxes)
- B. F. Skinner extended Thorndike's work using the Skinner box, a cage with levers or buttons that animals press or peck
- Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli and works best with involuntary behaviors; operant conditioning pairs a behavior with its consequence and involves voluntary behaviors

| | Add a stimulus | Remove a stimulus |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior increases | Positive reinforcement (e.g., food for pressing a bar) | Negative reinforcement (e.g., pressing a bar turns off a shock) |
| Behavior decreases | Positive punishment (e.g., a shock for pressing a bar) | Negative punishment (e.g., losing food or privileges) |

- A reinforcer or punisher is defined by its effect on behavior, not by any quality of the consequence (some children find a spanking reinforcing because of the attention)
- **Positive reinforcement:** Increases a behavior by adding a desired stimulus; a child with autism learned to say "ba" for chopped carrots, not M&Ms
- **Premack principle:** The behavior an organism spends the most time doing is likely to be important to it, so a higher-priority activity can reward a lower-priority one (eat broccoli to get ice cream)
- **Primary reinforcers:** Effective because of their natural roles in survival, such as food
- **Conditioned reinforcer:** A reinforcer that gains value from being associated with other things that are valued (money, grades, trophies); also known as a secondary reinforcer
- **Negative reinforcement:** A method for increasing behaviors that allow an organism to escape or avoid an unpleasant consequence (buckling up to stop the beeping, taking aspirin for a headache); not the same as punishment
- A heroin user is positively reinforced (euphoria) and negatively reinforced (relief from withdrawal) at the same time
- Superstitious behaviors (lucky socks) can be learned because a behavior only has to be followed by a positive outcome
- **Punishment:** A consequence that eliminates or reduces the frequency of a behavior
- **Positive punishment:** Reduces a behavior by applying an aversive stimulus
- **Negative punishment:** Reduces a behavior by removing something desirable whenever the target behavior occurs
- Thorndike and Skinner agreed punishment is weaker than reinforcement; punishment works only with significance, immediacy, and consistency
- Spanking is associated with more aggressive behavior in children across racial and ethnic groups and in 59 countries; in no country did it improve socioemotional outcomes
- Skinner recommended extinction instead of punishment: learned behaviors stop when they are no longer reinforced (e.g., ignoring tantrums)

## Schedules of reinforcement {pp. 300–304}
- **Continuous reinforcement:** Reinforcing a behavior every time it occurs; best when a new behavior is being learned
- **Partial reinforcement:** The reinforcement of a desired behavior on some occasions, but not others

| Schedule | Rule | Response pattern | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed ratio (FR) | Reinforcement after a set number of behaviors | Steady responding with a pause after each reward | Piecework pay (paid per 10 shirts) |
| Variable ratio (VR) | Reinforcement after a varying number of behaviors | High, steady rate; no pauses | Slot machines |
| Fixed interval (FI) | First response after a set time interval is reinforced | Long pause, then sharp increase as the interval ends | Congress passing bills at the end of a session; cramming before finals |
| Variable interval (VI) | First response after a varying time interval is reinforced | High, steady rate | Pop quizzes; fishing |

- Variable schedules produce higher rates of responding than fixed schedules
- **Partial reinforcement effect in extinction:** Extinction occurs more rapidly following continuous reinforcement than following partial reinforcement ("OK, just this once" makes begging harder to extinguish)
- **Matching law:** The relative frequency of responding to one alternative matches the relative reinforcement for that alternative (e.g., time spent gaming vs. socializing)
- **Method of successive approximations (shaping):** Increasing behaviors that never or rarely occur by reinforcing closer and closer approximations of the target behavior
- **Chaining:** Breaking a complex behavior into manageable steps, taught forward or backward (animal trainers usually use backward chaining)

## Cognitive, biological, and social influences {pp. 305–309}
- **Latent learning:** Learning that occurs in the absence of reinforcement; Edward Tolman's rats explored mazes without food and performed as well as reinforced rats once food was added
- **Cognitive maps:** Tolman's term for mental representations of a maze; rats chose new direct paths when the trained path was blocked
- **Instinctive drift:** Keller and Marian Breland ("The Misbehavior of Organisms," 1961) found trained animals drifted back to instinctive food behaviors (pigs rooted coins, raccoons washed them)
- Infants learn more language from a live person face to face than from television
- **Social facilitation:** The presence of others makes us perform simple tasks faster but complex tasks slower and worse (even in cockroaches)
- Skinner's Project Pigeon trained pigeons to guide missiles in World War II; it was never implemented
- **Token economy:** An application of operant conditioning in which tokens that can be exchanged for other reinforcers are used to increase desirable behaviors (money, classroom stars, prisons, institutions)
- **Behavior therapies:** Also called applied behavior analysis (ABA); use extinction, counterconditioning, reward, and rarely punishment; combined with cognitive methods they are the most popular and effective treatments for many disorders
- O. Ivar Lovaas pioneered behavior therapy for autism spectrum disorder; it does not cure autism but typically improves functioning
- Breaking a bad habit: keep a diary to understand baseline behavior, choose meaningful consequences (prefer positive reinforcement), track progress, and adjust

## Observational learning {pp. 310–315}
- Observational learning lets us learn without personally experiencing negative consequences; children copy what parents do more than what they say
- Behaviors influenced by observation include aggression, achievement motivation, language, phobias, moral judgment, and suicidal behavior
- **Imitation:** Copying behavior that is unlikely to occur naturally and spontaneously
- **Albert Bandura's Bobo doll studies:** Children who watched an adult attack a Bobo doll were more aggressive with it; children who saw the adult punished showed slightly less aggression
- **Bandura's four processes of modeling:** Attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation
- **Vicarious reinforcement:** Seeing someone else reinforced for a behavior makes us more likely to imitate it (seeing them punished makes us less likely)
- A society is a group of people living together; culture is all the socially transmitted information used by the group
- **Meme:** Richard Dawkins' (1976) term for the basic unit of cultural transmission (ideas, symbols, practices), passed on by observational learning
- Talking briefly with strangers (e.g., a barista) made people feel more cheerful and connected (Gillian Sandstrom)
