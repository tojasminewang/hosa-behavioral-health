## What is trauma? (SAMHSA)
- SAMHSA describes **individual trauma** as an event or circumstance resulting in physical harm, emotional harm, and/or life-threatening harm
- **Individual trauma (full definition):** Results from an event, series of events, or set of circumstances experienced as physically or emotionally harmful or life threatening that has lasting adverse effects on the person's functioning and mental, physical, social, emotional, or spiritual well-being
- Entire generations, communities, or cultures can experience trauma collectively
- Trauma is common for adults and children and especially common in the lives of people with mental and substance use disorders, so addressing trauma is an important part of effective behavioral health care and recovery
- Many people who experience trauma go on without lasting effects, especially with a strong support system, little prior trauma, and many resilient qualities; others have traumatic stress reactions
- Traumatic experiences, especially in childhood, are associated with both behavioral health and chronic physical health conditions, substance use, and risky behaviors

## Trauma-informed care (SAMHSA)
- **Trauma-informed care:** A way to create safer environments for people who have experienced trauma
- A trauma-informed program, organization, or system follows the **four R's**:

| The four R's | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Realizes | The widespread impact of trauma and potential paths for recovery and healing |
| Recognizes | The signs and symptoms of trauma in clients, families, staff, and others |
| Responds | By fully integrating knowledge about trauma into policies, procedures, and practices |
| Resists retraumatization | Actively avoids re-traumatizing people |

![The four R's of a trauma-informed approach](four-rs)

| Trauma-informed principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Safety | Participants and staff feel physically and psychologically safe |
| Peer support | Peer support and mutual self-help establish safety and hope, build trust, and use lived experience to promote healing |
| Trustworthiness and transparency | Organizational decisions aim to build and maintain trust with participants and staff |
| Collaboration and mutuality | Partnering and leveling power differences between staff and service participants |
| Empowerment, voice, and choice | Belief in the primacy of the people served to heal and promote recovery |

- SAMHSA emphasizes **safety, trust, collaboration, and empowerment** to create supportive, recovery-focused environments, and expects all grant recipients to use trauma-informed approaches
- The **Interagency Task Force on Trauma-Informed Care** was established on October 13, 2018, under the SUPPORT for Patients and Communities Act (Public Law 115-271) and reauthorized in the 2025 SUPPORT Act
- **Seclusion:** The involuntary, solitary confinement of an individual
- **Restraint:** Any method, device, material, or drug that immobilizes or reduces a person's ability to freely move their arms, legs, body, or head
- Seclusion and restraint were once seen as therapeutic but are now viewed as traumatizing, to be used only as a last resort when less-restrictive measures fail and safety is at severe risk; they cause psychological harm, injuries, and death, longer stays, and more readmissions
- SAMHSA aims to reduce and ultimately eliminate seclusion and restraint, creating coercion- and violence-free treatment environments

## Child trauma (SAMHSA)
- **Child traumatic stress:** Occurs when a child (0–18 years) feels intensely threatened by an event they experience or witness
- Children's reactions are influenced by how parents, relatives, teachers, and caregivers respond; maintaining routines provides comfort and stability
- Potentially traumatic events: abuse or assault, domestic, school, or community violence, disasters or terrorism, displacement, sudden or violent loss of a loved one, refugee or war experiences, military family stress (parental deployment), serious accidents or illness, and neglect
- Over two-thirds of children report experiencing at least one traumatic event by age 16; at least 1 in 7 children experience abuse or neglect each year in the U.S.; each day 14 youth die from homicide
- Signs by age: preschool (fear of separation, nightmares, crying, poor appetite); elementary (anxiety, guilt, shame, poor concentration, sleep problems, withdrawal, aggression); middle and high school (depression, self-harm, substance use, withdrawal, risky behavior, aggression)
- Long-term effects: learning difficulties, more health service use, juvenile justice involvement, and long-term health issues (heart disease, diabetes)
- How caring adults help: assure the child they are safe, explain they are not responsible for what happened, and be patient (there is no correct timetable for healing)
- Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy is a proven treatment for child trauma
- **National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN):** Raises the standard of care and access to services for children and families who experience or witness trauma
- **ReCAST (Resiliency in Communities After Stress and Trauma):** Helps high-risk youth and families in communities that recently faced civil unrest

## Disaster behavioral health (SAMHSA)
- **Disaster behavioral health (DBH):** The understanding and provision of mental, emotional, and substance use services and interventions for persons and communities impacted by disasters
- SAMHSA supports preparedness, response, recovery, and mitigation of disasters, focused on building resilience
- The memorial date of a disaster can renew feelings of fear, anxiety, and sadness in survivors
- Most stress symptoms after a disaster are temporary, but for at-risk people they may last weeks or months
- Warning signs of distress: eating or sleeping too much or little, anger, overwhelming sadness, withdrawal, low energy, feeling you must keep busy, unexplained aches, hopelessness, excessive smoking, drinking, or drug use, constant worry or guilt, thoughts of hurting yourself or others, and trouble readjusting to home or work
- Higher-risk populations: children and youth, adults balancing roles, first responders and recovery workers (often the last to seek help), and women and girls at risk of intimate partner or family violence
- Coping tips after disasters: take care of yourself, reach out to family and friends, **limit news consumption**, get good sleep, establish a routine, avoid major life decisions, understand there will be changes, care for pets or connect with nature, know when to ask for help, join a support group, and consult a financial adviser
- **Disaster Distress Helpline (DDH):** 1-800-985-5990, the first national hotline for year-round disaster crisis counseling
- **Crisis Counseling Assistance and Training Program (CCP):** A short-term disaster relief grant awarded after a presidential disaster declaration; FEMA funds and implements it to support community outreach and counseling
- **Disaster Technical Assistance Center (DTAC):** All-hazards technical assistance and training for disaster behavioral health
- **SAMHSA Emergency Response Grants (SERG):** Let SAMHSA act immediately when a crisis overwhelms the behavioral health system; "funds of last resort"
- **SAMHSA Disaster Mobile App:** Free app giving first responders resources at every phase of response

## Grief and bereavement (SAMHSA)
- **Bereavement:** The overarching experience of coping with loss and change; a period of grief and mourning after a loss, often involving rituals and support from loved ones
- **Grief:** A person's emotional, mental, and/or physical response to a loss (death, the end of a relationship, loss of identity, or illness); it can occur in anticipation of a loss and generally subsides over time
- Grief can be expressed as sadness, anger, guilt, confusion, relief, anxiety, numbness, helplessness, fatigue, loss of appetite, and sleep disturbances
- Coping with grief: support from friends and family, cultural or religious rituals, grief support groups, grief counseling, grief peer support, artistic expression, physical movement, and outreach to a mental health professional
- **Prolonged grief disorder (PGD):** Added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022; features intense yearning for or thoughts about the deceased, significant persistent distress, decreased functioning, and symptoms lasting more than one year in adults (six months in children and youth)
- Hospice care can be a source of grief counseling for families and caregivers
- **National Grief Awareness Week** is observed in the first week of December to reduce stigma around grief
