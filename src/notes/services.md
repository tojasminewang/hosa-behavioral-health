## Treatment works (SAMHSA)
- Research shows treatment for mental illness works; with appropriate treatment people can manage their illness, overcome challenges, and lead productive lives
- Before the COVID-19 pandemic, about one in five adults had a mental illness; most people with mental illness do not receive the services they need
- Symptoms of mental illness can include shifts in mood, sadness, anxiety, irritability, low energy, delusions, hallucinations, and sleep problems; someone may have only one or two
- Treating a mental illness is not something to attempt on your own; it takes professional diagnosis and treatment
- Mental health services are covered by most health plans, by law; the earlier you get treatment, the better
- Having a serious mental illness is not a choice, a weakness, or a character flaw, and it cannot be "snapped out of" with willpower; risk factors include family history, brain chemistry, and significant life events such as trauma or the death of a loved one

## Where you get care: levels of treatment (SAMHSA)
@ [Types of Treatment](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-support/learn-about-treatment/types-of-treatment)
- The three main types of treatment: outpatient, inpatient, and residential
- **Outpatient:** You have an appointment and leave the same day; usually for ongoing treatment or maintenance; many opioid treatment programs are outpatient
- **Intensive outpatient / partial hospitalization:** A more intensive type of outpatient care with one-on-one appointments, group sessions, and coping-skills learning, usually lasting at least a few hours
- **Telehealth:** Care online or over the phone; helpful for people who have a hard time getting to appointments; usually an option for first-line treatment and maintenance
- **Inpatient:** You stay at a hospital or treatment program overnight for a few days or weeks; for people needing 24-hour care; most programs are connected to a hospital or clinic
- **Residential:** You live at a treatment program, usually for a few weeks to a few months (a year or more for serious conditions)
- **Interim care:** Daily medicine and emergency counseling to keep you safe while waiting for an outpatient, inpatient, or residential opening
- **Peer recovery support:** Support groups that connect you with people who have similar experiences; most often used to support recovery
- The goal of most treatments is to change thoughts and behaviors and, if needed, manage physical dependence on drugs or alcohol; you may need more than one type of treatment

## Types of care (SAMHSA)
- **Therapy and counseling:** Part of most treatment plans, usually with a licensed behavioral health professional one-on-one or in a group; focused on developing healthy coping skills (e.g., family and marriage therapy, motivational therapy, art therapy, CBT)
- **Medication management:** Medicines must be prescribed by a doctor, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant
- **Medications for substance use disorders (MSUD):** Medications for opioid use disorder are most often combined with counseling; the 3 approved medications for opioid use disorder in the U.S. are methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone
- Approved medications also exist for alcohol use disorder and tobacco use disorder; there are currently no FDA-approved medications to help prevent returning to use of methamphetamines or cocaine
- Treatment and support are provided by licensed professionals (doctors, social workers) or by community health workers, peer support specialists, and crisis counselors

## Starting and staying in treatment (SAMHSA)
- Be honest with the health care professional so they can make the best treatment plan; they will involve family only with your permission
- Outpatients should leave knowing their next appointment; inpatients should have a written treatment plan with diagnosis, goals, and next steps
- You may feel better in a few weeks or it may take a few months; it's okay to change professionals or programs, but give treatment time to work
- Getting ready: plan transportation and childcare; bring ID, insurance card, questions, a list of medications, and what treatments you have tried before
- **Signs of quality treatment:** licensed and accredited programs; evidence-based practices; family involvement as desired; support for other parts of life (medical needs, housing, food assistance, job placement, supported employment, long-term recovery); only FDA-approved medications for alcohol, tobacco, or opioid use
- Evidence-based practices to look for: motivational interviewing or motivational enhancement therapy, CBT, cognitive processing therapy (CPT), psychoeducation (education about your condition), supportive therapy, social skills training, and behavioral management training for youth
- Paying for treatment: many programs take Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), Medicare, Veterans Affairs (VA) Health Care, or private insurance; free and low-cost options exist
- Finding care: ask someone you trust for a recommendation; look for care that accepts your insurance (or is free/low-cost), takes new patients, and fits your schedule

## Support groups and recovery support (SAMHSA)
- National support groups include Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, the National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI), and SMART Recovery; many meet in person and online
- There are also support groups for family members and children coping with a loved one's mental health, drug, or alcohol issues
- Cultural, religious, or other social networks, and community or spiritual leaders, can help people find support
- National hotlines for specific groups include the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline, the National Sexual Assault Hotline, and the StrongHearts Native Helpline
- Recovery support services include recovery housing, a clubhouse, Oxford House, peer support, and supportive employment
- Veterans who are homeless or at risk can call the National Call Center for Homeless Veterans (1-877-424-3838), 24/7

- The National Helpline is also called the Treatment Referral Routing Service (TTY 1-800-487-4889); it received 833,598 calls in 2020, a 27% increase from 2019
- The National Helpline does not provide counseling: trained information specialists refer callers to state services, local treatment facilities, support groups, and community organizations; it is confidential and asks for no personal information
- People with no insurance are referred to their state office for state-funded treatment or to facilities with sliding fee scales or that accept Medicare or Medicaid
- The DDH offers crisis counseling, information on recognizing distress, referrals, and coping tips; it is open to survivors, loved ones, first responders, relief workers, clergy, and parents, with counselors in over 100 languages
- Disasters covered by DDH include storms, hurricanes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, drought, extreme heat, mass violence, anniversaries and trigger events, infectious disease outbreaks, and community unrest
- Call 211 for disaster-related information on evacuations, shelters, food and clothing, and volunteer opportunities

## Mental Health Awareness Training (SAMHSA)
@ [MHAT](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/serious-mental-illness/mhat)
- **Mental Health Awareness Training (MHAT):** Prepares individuals and communities to respond appropriately and safely to people with mental health challenges, particularly SMI and SED
- MHAT trains the mental health workforce, schools and higher education, first responders, law enforcement, and human service organizations
- MHAT successes 2018–2023: 244,680 workforce members trained; 487,459 people trained in prevention or mental health promotion; 543,546 people referred to services; 80% improved in knowledge, attitudes, or beliefs

## Treatment settings (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Treatment Settings](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/treatment-settings/)
- Where a person gets treatment depends on their situation and recovery needs; some psychiatrists and other doctors do not accept insurance
- Private practice offers individual, family and group therapy, often weekly, every two weeks or monthly
- **Community or county mental health centers:** Centers run by local governments that provide public mental health care (outpatient services, medication management, case management, intensive community treatment) when a private provider is not possible
  - Most people served receive Social Security disability benefits and rely on Medicaid to fund treatment
  - Some use the Assertive Community Treatment (ACT) team model, and many have emergency walk-in services or mobile crisis units that de-escalate, stabilize and plan next steps
- Substance abuse treatment settings: detoxification facilities (alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening and needs medical supervision; opiate withdrawal is less risky), Acute Residential Treatment programs and Intensive Outpatient Programs
- **Intensive Outpatient Program (IOP):** Lets people keep working or going to school while getting services such as a 12-step program to stay sober
- **Acute Residential Treatment (ART):** Short-term, highly focused programs that help people solidify recovery and sobriety
- Teletherapy and telepsychiatry (by video or phone) are as effective as in-person sessions for most people with most conditions, though possibly less effective for older (geriatric) patients and people with autism

## Crisis services (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Getting Treatment During a Crisis](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/getting-treatment-during-a-crisis/)
- A well-designed crisis response system backs up community providers, connects first-time users to services and reassures the community that people's needs are met
- **24-hour crisis lines:** Often the first point of contact, giving assessment, screening, triage, preliminary counseling and referrals
- **Walk-in crisis services:** Clinics or psychiatric urgent care centers that resolve crises in a less intensive setting than a hospital and may serve as drop-off centers for law enforcement to reduce unnecessary arrests
- **Mobile crisis teams:** Teams that go wherever the crisis is happening, working closely with police, crisis hotlines and emergency staff
- Crisis respite and residential services can be an alternative to hospitalization or a step-down setting after leaving a hospital
  - Respite models: family-based crisis home support (living with a screened and trained "professional family," with daily visits from mental health professionals), crisis respite centers and apartments (24-hour support, sometimes from peer specialists), and in-home support
- **Crisis stabilization units (CSUs):** Small inpatient facilities with fewer than 16 beds for people whose crisis cannot be safely handled in residential settings; less restrictive than a hospital
- **Extended observation units (23-hour beds):** For crises that can be resolved in less than 24 hours
- Voluntary hospitalization means the person chooses to go, and some private hospitals take only voluntary patients
- Involuntary hospitalization requires that a person be a "danger to self or others"; it usually lasts up to 3 days, and extending it requires a court hearing where a judge and two doctors agree; the rules are set by each state
- A discharge plan made with a social worker or case manager ensures continuous, coordinated treatment and a smooth return home
- **Partial (day) hospitalization:** Care and monitoring during the day for someone with acute symptoms who is not a danger to self or others, returning home at night
- Reasons for an emergency room visit include a suicide attempt, assault or threats, hearing voices or paranoia, and drug or alcohol use
- When calling 911, say it is a "mental health emergency" and ask for responders with Crisis Intervention Team (CIT) training
- At the emergency room, a psychiatric exam establishes a "working diagnosis," and a crisis plan can help prevent emergencies from escalating

## Finding a mental health professional (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Finding a Mental Health Professional](https://www.nami.org/living-with-a-mental-health-condition/finding-a-mental-health-professional/)
- Most people treating a mental health condition have at least two professionals: one for medication (the biological side) and one for therapy (the mind side)
- See a physician for a physical exam first, because many illnesses can cause symptoms similar to mental illness
- For medication, NAMI suggests a specialist such as a psychiatrist rather than relying only on a primary care doctor
- NAMI's five steps: think about whom you're looking for, gather referrals (from your insurer, or a community mental health center if uninsured), make the call, ask questions, and build a relationship
- Get at least three names from your insurer; if the wait is long, book anyway, call others, and join a cancellation waiting list
- The most important question is whether you feel comfortable and can work well with the person; ask about sliding-scale fees early if cost is a concern
- While waiting for an appointment, free peer support groups such as NAMI's can help
