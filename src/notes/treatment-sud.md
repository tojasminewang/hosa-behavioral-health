## Medication plus therapy: the whole-patient approach (SAMHSA)
- Using medications in combination with counseling and behavioral therapies provides a **"whole-patient" approach** to treating substance use disorders; medications are FDA-approved and tailored to each patient
- A combination of medication and therapy can successfully treat SUDs and, for some medications, help sustain recovery; medications are also used to prevent or reduce opioid overdose
- This approach improves patient survival, increases retention in treatment, decreases illicit opiate use and other illicit activity, increases employment, and improves birth outcomes among pregnant women with SUDs
- These medications and therapies can lower the risk of contracting HIV or hepatitis C by reducing the potential for relapse
- Medications relieve withdrawal symptoms and psychological cravings; they are evidence-based and **do not just substitute one drug for another**
- **Substance use disorder (SAMHSA):** Occurs when the recurrent use of alcohol and/or drugs causes clinically significant impairment, including health problems, disability, and failure to meet major responsibilities at work, school, or home
- 2024 NSDUH: 48.4 million people aged 12+ (16.8%) had a past-year substance use disorder
- **Low barrier care:** A model that reduces requirements and restrictions limiting access to treatment, meeting individuals where they are with culturally sensitive care
- Under **42 CFR Part 2**, personally identifiable information about substance use and alcohol treatment must be handled with a higher degree of confidentiality than other medical information

## Medications for opioid and alcohol use disorders (SAMHSA)
| Medication | Treats | How it works / key facts |
|---|---|---|
| Methadone | OUD (also pain) | Long-acting **full opioid agonist**, Schedule II; reduces craving and withdrawal and blunts or blocks opioid effects; for OUD can only be dispensed through a SAMHSA-certified opioid treatment program (OTP); liquid, powder, or diskettes taken daily; treatment should last at least 12 months |
| Buprenorphine | OUD | **Opioid partial agonist** with weaker effects than full agonists; the first OUD medication that can be prescribed or dispensed in physician offices; naloxone is added to reduce misuse; patients must abstain 12–24 hours and be in early withdrawal before starting |
| Naltrexone | OUD and AUD | **Not an opioid**, not addictive, no withdrawal; blocks the euphoric and sedative effects of opioids; any licensed prescriber can give it; daily pill for AUD or monthly extended-release injection (approved for OUD and AUD); wait at least 7 days after short-acting opioids (10–14 days after long-acting) |
| Acamprosate | AUD | Restores the natural balance of neurotransmitters disrupted by chronic alcohol use; decreases cravings; started a few days after stopping alcohol; no potential for misuse; not addictive |
| Disulfiram | AUD | Causes a very unpleasant reaction (flushing, sweating, headache, nausea, vomiting, palpitations) when alcohol is used, about 10 minutes after drinking; does not reduce cravings or treat withdrawal; works better if someone observes the daily dose |

- **Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD):** Buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone are the most common FDA-approved medications for OUD; they normalize brain chemistry, block euphoric effects, relieve cravings, and are safe for long-term use (months to a lifetime)
- **Medications for alcohol use disorder (MAUD):** Acamprosate, disulfiram, and naltrexone are the most common; they do not cure AUD but are most effective for people who participate in a treatment program
- Buprenorphine and methadone are considered the **treatments of choice for OUD in pregnant and breastfeeding women**; methadone during pregnancy does not cause birth defects
- **Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS):** Withdrawal in a newborn after birth; most symptoms appear within 72 hours
- People who stop naltrexone or relapse after abstinence have reduced tolerance, so even their old (or lower) opioid doses can be deadly
- Methadone's liquid form is colored and can be mistaken for fruit juice; medications kept at home must be locked away from children
- Federal law (42 CFR 8.12) requires OTP patients to be able to receive counseling along with medical, vocational, educational, and other services
- The Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 eliminated the **DATA waiver** (Section 1262, the MAT Act), so practitioners no longer need a waiver to prescribe buprenorphine; the **MATE Act** (Section 1263) requires DEA registrants to attest to SUD training
- There are currently no FDA-approved medications for methamphetamine or cocaine addiction

## Opioid treatment programs (SAMHSA)
- SAMHSA's **Division of Pharmacologic Therapies (DPT)**, part of the Center for Substance Abuse Treatment (CSAT), oversees federal regulations on methadone and buprenorphine for OUD
- DPT supports certification and accreditation of more than 1,900 OTPs that treat more than 600,000 patients each year
- **42 CFR Part 8:** The federal regulations guiding OTPs (in effect since 2001); SAMHSA released a revised final rule in February 2024, the first major update in more than two decades, shifting to treat OUD as a chronic health issue
- OTPs must complete certification and accreditation; deviations from standards require a Form SMA-168 exception request
- **State Opioid Treatment Authorities (SOTAs)** oversee OTPs in each state

## Recognizing and reversing opioid overdose (SAMHSA)
- Call 911 immediately if a person's face is extremely pale or clammy, body goes limp, fingernails or lips turn purple or blue, they vomit or make gurgling noises, cannot be awakened or speak, or breathing or heartbeat slows or stops
- Signs of opioid overdose also include **pin-point sized pupils** and bluish lips and nose
- What to do: call 911, begin CPR if breathing has stopped or is very weak, and give naloxone or nalmefene if available
- People experiencing an overdose cannot reverse it themselves
- Overdose causes: illicit opioids, methadone, an accidental extra dose, misusing prescriptions, mixing opioids with other drugs or alcohol (especially benzodiazepines like Xanax or Valium), or using someone else's medication
- **Opioid overdose reversal medications (OORMs):** FDA-approved naloxone and nalmefene; they work even when opioids were mixed with sedatives or stimulants, but they do not treat overdoses of benzodiazepines or stimulants like cocaine and amphetamines
- **Naloxone:** An **opioid antagonist** that rapidly reverses opioid overdose by binding to opioid receptors; a temporary treatment whose effects do not last long; given by nasal spray or injection (into muscle, under the skin, or into a vein)
- In March 2023 the FDA approved naloxone nasal spray as the **first over-the-counter** opioid overdose reversal medication
- **Nalmefene:** A prescription-only OORM nasal spray for people 12 and older; it has a longer half-life than naloxone, so withdrawal can be more severe and longer
- OORMs cause opioid withdrawal symptoms, so medical help is needed right after giving them; withdrawal symptoms include restlessness, body aches, diarrhea, fever, chills, goose bumps, and runny nose
- Candidates for OORMs include people on high-dose or long-acting opioids, people recently treated for opioid poisoning, and people with a period of abstinence (such as recent release from incarceration)
- Naloxone should be replaced when expired or exposed to temperatures below 39°F or above 104°F
- SAMHSA updated its **Overdose Prevention and Response Toolkit** in 2024 (first released in 2018)

## Co-occurring disorders and other health conditions (SAMHSA)
- **Co-occurring disorders:** The coexistence of both a mental health disorder and a substance use disorder; any combination of two or more SUDs and mental disorders in the DSM-5-TR
- 2024 NSDUH: about 21.2 million adults had a co-occurring mental illness and substance use disorder
- Combining SUD medications with benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin) can have serious adverse effects
- Drug use is an important driver of the HIV epidemic: about 1 in 10 new U.S. HIV diagnoses are attributed to injection drug use
- Hepatitis A and B have vaccines; there is no vaccine for hepatitis C, and injection drug use is the major source of hepatitis C infection in the U.S.
- HIV testing and risk-reduction counseling decrease drug use and risky behaviors such as unsafe injection and unsafe sex

## Youth and substance use (SAMHSA)
- More than 90% of adults with a substance use disorder began their substance use in adolescence
- Youth are more vulnerable to SUDs because their brains are still developing; youth with SUDs face sexually transmitted infections, justice involvement, school challenges, and mental health problems
- 2023 NSDUH: 27.1% of young adults 18–25 had an SUD, compared with 16.6% of adults 26+ and 8.5% of adolescents 12–17
- In 2022, 15.1% of people aged 12–20 drank alcohol in the past month; in 2023, 11.3% of people 12–20 used marijuana in the past month
- CSAT youth programs use a family-focused model of care (e.g., Youth and Family TREE, PYO TREAT)

## Stigma and language (SAMHSA)
- People with substance use disorders often feel shame that impedes treatment-seeking; negative attitudes, beliefs, and language can be barriers that prevent people from seeking services or sharing that they are in recovery
- SAMHSA's webinar series "The Power of Perceptions and Understanding" (2018) covered overcoming stigma, why addiction is a "disease," and reducing discriminatory practices in clinical settings

## Dual diagnosis (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Substance Use Disorders](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/substance-use-disorders/)
- **Dual diagnosis (co-occurring disorders):** Having both a mental illness and a substance use disorder; either one can develop first
- NAMI defines substance use disorders as the repeated misuse of alcohol and/or drugs, often to cope with overwhelming symptoms of mental illness
- According to the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 17 million U.S. adults had both a mental illness and a substance use disorder in 2020
- SUD symptoms: withdrawal from friends and family, sudden behavior changes, risky behavior, high tolerance and withdrawal symptoms, and feeling you need a drug to function
- **Integrated intervention:** The best treatment for dual diagnosis, giving care for both the mental illness and the substance use disorder; the idea "I can't treat your depression because you're drinking" is outdated
- Inpatient detoxification is generally more effective than outpatient for initial sobriety and safety; staff monitor the person 24/7 for up to seven days and may give tapering doses to ease withdrawal
- Inpatient rehabilitation centers provide 24/7 medical and mental health care, therapy, support and medication
- CBT helps people with dual diagnosis cope and change ineffective thinking patterns that raise the risk of substance use
- Supportive housing such as group homes or sober houses can help people who are newly sober, but sober homes vary in quality because licensed professionals typically do not run them
- Dual diagnosis support groups: Double Trouble in Recovery (a 12-step fellowship for people with both mental illness and SUD), Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous (12-step groups), and SMART Recovery (a sobriety support group not based in faith)
