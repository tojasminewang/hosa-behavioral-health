## What is biological psychology? {pp. 106–109}
- **Biological psychology:** Also known as behavioral neuroscience; the scientific study of the reciprocal connections between the structure and activity of the nervous system and behavior and mental processes
- **Dualism:** René Descartes' philosophy that our mind is somehow different and separate from our physical being
- **Monism:** The approach embraced by biological psychology and the neurosciences: the mind is what the brain does
- Human brains weigh about 3 pounds and contain approximately 86 billion neurons
- **Ghrelin:** A gut hormone released when your stomach is empty; when it reaches the brain, you respond by feeling hungry
- Alia Crum's milkshake study: everyone drank the same 380-calorie shake. When told it was a 620-calorie "indulgent" shake, ghrelin dropped steeply; when told it was a "sensible" 140-calorie shake, ghrelin barely changed. The way people thought about the shake (cognitions) affected their biology.
- Social connection and immunity: a person who feels socially isolated generates hormonal signals that gear the immune system up against bacteria; a person who feels socially connected has immune cells prepared to protect against viruses

## Early attempts to understand the brain {pp. 108–109}
- **Autopsy:** The examination of bodies after death; before modern methods, most knowledge came from clinical observations of injured or mentally ill individuals or from autopsy
- Aristotle (384–322 BCE) mistakenly believed that the heart, not the brain, was the source of mental activity
- **Phrenology:** A late-18th-century idea that the pattern of bumps on the skull correlated with personality traits and abilities; wrong, but correct that some behavioral functions are localized to certain areas of the brain
- Camillo Golgi argued that the nervous system was a single continuous network
- **Neuron doctrine:** Santiago Ramón y Cajal used Golgi's own stain to prove the nervous system is made up of separate cells; both men shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine
- John Hughlings Jackson (studied patients with seizure disorders) proposed the nervous system is organized as a hierarchy, with more complicated behaviors managed by more recently evolved and complex structures
  - Alcohol example: alcohol decreases the activity of brain areas involved with decision making, so higher-level social controls are diminished and more primitive behaviors (aggression, sexuality) are less restrained
- PET and fMRI let scientists observe the activity of the living brain

## Research methods in biological psychology (Table 4.1) {p. 110}
| Method | Description | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Histology | Microscopic examination of the nervous system | How does the structure of nervous system cells correlate with behavior? |
| Skin conductance response (formerly galvanic skin response) | Electricity passed between two surface electrodes on the skin of the hand or finger | What is a person's state of arousal? |
| Electroencephalogram (EEG) | The brain's electrical activity measured with electrodes placed on the scalp | What is a person's state of arousal? |
| Event-related potential (ERP) | Averaging EEG responses to a stimulus, such as a light or tone | Did the person perceive the stimulus? |
| Single cell recording | A single neuron's activity recorded through a surgically implanted electrode | What types of stimulation make this neuron respond? |
| Magnetoencephalography | Recording of the tiny amounts of magnetic output of the brain | What parts of the brain react to this stimulus? |
| Positron emission tomography (PET) | Uses the accumulation of radioactively tagged glucose or oxygen to identify activity levels in parts of the brain | What parts of the brain are active during a particular task? |
| Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) | Uses magnetism to track the flow of oxygen to identify active parts of the brain | What parts of the brain are active during a particular task? |
| Electrical stimulation | Small amounts of electricity applied through a surgically implanted electrode | What behaviors occur if we stimulate this part of the brain? |
| Optogenetics | Genetically inserted light-sensitive proteins allow brain cells to be turned on with light | Which types of cells are active during particular behaviors? |
| Transcranial magnetic stimulation | Magnetic fields applied to the brain through an instrument held near the scalp | What behavioral changes occur when magnetism is applied to the brain? |
| Lesions | Naturally occurring or deliberate damage to the brain | What behavioral changes are correlated with brain damage? |

## Organization of the nervous system {pp. 110–112}
- **Central nervous system (CNS):** The brain and spinal cord
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** The nerves exiting the CNS that carry sensory and motor information to and from the rest of the body
- Nerves of the CNS are encased in bone, but those of the PNS are not
- Divisions: the PNS = somatic nervous system + autonomic nervous system; the autonomic nervous system = sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions
- The brain weighs about 3 pounds (1.5 kilograms) and contains about 86 billion nerve cells; the spinal cord contains about 1 billion nerve cells and is about 18 inches (45 centimeters) long
- The spinal cord stops growing in length at about four years of age, so the adult spinal cord is shorter than the spine
- **Meninges:** Membranes under the skull and vertebrae that further protect the brain and spinal cord; infection of these membranes causes meningitis
- **Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF):** A clear, plasma-like fluid that cushions and "floats" the brain; it seeps out of the lining of the ventricles (hollow spaces in the brain)
- **Hydrocephalus:** "Water on the head"; fluid buildup caused by blockages in the circulation of CSF
- A spinal tap removes a sample of CSF to diagnose some medical conditions

## The spinal cord and reflexes {pp. 111–114}
- **Spinal cord:** A long cylinder of neural tissue extending from the medulla of the brain down to the middle of the back; part of the CNS
- The spinal cord is only 2% of the weight of the CNS; 31 pairs of spinal nerves exit it to serve the body
- Many reflexes (pulling away from pain, the knee-jerk reflex) are initiated by the spinal cord without any assistance from the brain; conditions such as diabetes affect the strength of the knee-jerk reflex
- **Sensory neurons:** Carry information from the external environment or from the body back to the CNS
- **Motor neurons:** Carry commands from the CNS back to the muscles and glands of the body
- **Interneurons:** Neurons with neither sensory nor motor functions; many form bridges between sensory and motor neurons (the knee-jerk reflex does not need them)

## Brainstem and cerebellum {pp. 114–116}
- **Brainstem:** The part of the brain containing the midbrain, pons, and medulla
- In the fourth gestational week the embryo's brain forms three bulges: the forebrain (becomes the cerebral hemispheres), the midbrain, and the hindbrain (these two form the brainstem)
- The hindbrain consists of the medulla, pons, and cerebellum
- **Medulla:** The brainstem structure just above the spinal cord; manages many functions essential to life, such as heart rate, breathing, and blood pressure
- **Pons:** Between the medulla and midbrain; involved with sleep, arousal, and facial expressions; "pons" means "bridge" in Latin and it connects the cerebellum to the rest of the brain
- **Cerebellum:** Attached to the brainstem; essential for balance and motor coordination; participates in skilled movement and, in humans, complex cognitive processing
  - One of the first brain structures affected by alcohol; most sobriety tests are the same tests a neurologist uses to assess the cerebellum
  - Contains more nerve cells than the rest of the brain combined; helps make mental and motor skills more automatic
  - Abnormalities in the cerebellum are common in autism spectrum disorder
- **Midbrain:** The part of the brainstem between the pons and the cerebral hemispheres; involved in sensory reflexes, movement, and pain
- **Periaqueductal gray:** Midbrain area important for pain management because it contains receptors for endorphins (our natural opioids); opiate painkillers such as morphine and OxyContin act here; also associated with maternal and unconditional love
- Cell clusters near the periaqueductal gray are the major sources of serotonin and norepinephrine (arousal, mood, appetite, aggression)
- **Reticular formation:** Structures along the midline of the brainstem (upper medulla into midbrain) that participate in mood, arousal, and sleep; fast firing = awake, slow firing (or damage) = deep sleep or unconsciousness

## Subcortical structures {pp. 116–118}
- Subcortical structures lie below the cerebral cortex within the white matter; they are paired (one on each side of the brain)
- **Limbic system:** "Border"; an older grouping of subcortical structures sometimes called "your emotional brain," a term losing popularity with contemporary anatomists
- **Thalamus:** Involved with processing sensory information, arousal, and learning and memory; called the "gateway to the cortex" because input from most senses (vision, hearing, touch, and taste) travels first to the thalamus, which forwards it to the cerebral cortex
  - During the deepest stages of sleep, the thalamus "tunes out" the outside world, making it difficult to awaken
- **Basal ganglia:** Subcortical structures that participate in reward and the control of movement; help us select movements that bring us closer to our goals
  - Degeneration of the basal ganglia occurs in Parkinson's disease; they also contribute to OCD, autism spectrum disorder, and ADHD
- **Hypothalamus:** Regulates thirst, temperature, hunger, sexual behavior, aggression, and biological rhythms; involved with motivation and homeostasis
  - Contributes to the "4F" behaviors: feeding, fleeing, fighting, and sex (fornication); works by directing the autonomic nervous system and the endocrine system
- **Hippocampus:** Named for the Greek word for seahorse; essential to forming long-term memories (but does not store them permanently); also participates in stress
  - Damage causes profound impairment in forming new memories, while intelligence, personality, and older memories remain intact
  - Has receptors for stress hormones so it can "put on the brakes" if levels get too high; this can be less effective in major depressive disorder
- **Cingulate cortex:** A fold above the corpus callosum; the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) participates in decision making, emotion, anticipation of reward, empathy, and control of the autonomic nervous system; the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) participates in memory and visual processing
- **Amygdala:** Named for the Greek word for "almond"; located in the temporal lobe; participates in emotional processing
  - Historically linked to threat and fear, but it responds to any salient stimuli, positive or negative; important in social behavior, emotion, and learning about reward
  - Patient S.M. (damage to both amygdalae) could not identify fearful expressions, showed no fear of snakes, spiders, or scary movies, and stood about twice as close to strangers as others do
- **Nucleus accumbens:** Part of the brain's reward and pleasure circuitry; participates in reward and addiction (eating, sex, addictive drugs, gambling); its activity is related to a person's sense of social inclusion

## The cerebral cortex {pp. 118–123}
- **Corpus callosum:** A wide band of nerve fibers connecting the right and left cerebral hemispheres
- **Cerebral cortex:** The thin layer of neurons covering the outer surface of the cerebral hemispheres; "cortex" means "bark" in Latin
- White matter (nerve fiber pathways) makes up most of the bulk of the hemispheres; gray matter is made of cell bodies
- The degree of cortical convolution (wrinkling) positively correlates with the general intellectual capacities of a species (human > sheep > rat)
- Each hemisphere has four lobes named after the skull bones covering them: frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal
- Cortex functions fall into three categories: sensory, motor, and association
- **Association cortex:** Areas of the cortex without specific sensory or motor functions; help form bridges between sensation and action, language, and abstract thought

| Lobe | Location | Key functions |
|---|---|---|
| Frontal lobe | Most forward lobe | Primary motor cortex (highest level of voluntary control over movement); Broca's area (speech production); prefrontal cortex (planning, attention, judgment) |
| Parietal lobe | Top of the brain, between the frontal and occipital lobes | Primary somatosensory cortex (touch, pain, skin temperature, body position); coordinating vision and movement; taste |
| Occipital lobe | Back of the brain | Primary visual cortex (borders, shading, color, movement) |
| Temporal lobe | Curves around the side of each hemisphere | Primary auditory cortex; recognizing objects and familiar faces; Wernicke's area (understanding language) |

- **Broca's area:** Frontal lobe area (named for Paul Broca, 1860s) that participates in the production of speech; damage causes difficulty speaking while comprehension stays good
- **Wernicke's area:** Temporal lobe language area; damage causes fluent speech that makes no sense and an inability to comprehend speech
- **Prefrontal cortex:** The most forward part of the frontal lobe; involved with planning of behavior, attention, and judgment; abnormalities may account for features of schizophrenia and ADHD
- **Alien hand syndrome:** Occurs when connections between the prefrontal cortex and lower movement areas are damaged; patients do not control the affected limb
- **Phineas Gage:** Railroad worker who survived an iron tamping rod through his frontal lobes in 1848; afterward he was prone to angry outbursts and unreliability; the rod damaged more fiber pathways (11%) than cells (4%)
- **Frontal lobotomy:** Deliberate damage to the frontal lobes performed on nearly 50,000 American patients in the 1940s and 1950s to reduce fear and anxiety; many patients became impulsive and antisocial
- **Orbitofrontal cortex:** Part of the prefrontal cortex right behind the eyes that participates in impulse control; one of the last brain areas to mature
  - Damage causes dramatic deficits in social behavior and emotion despite intact intelligence (Patient E.V.R.)
  - In people with antisocial personality disorder, prefrontal cortex volume was about 11% less; they fail to anticipate emotional consequences and cannot delay gratification
- A pathway from the occipital lobe to the temporal lobe lets you recognize objects; a pathway from the occipital lobe to the parietal lobe lets you process the movement of objects
- **Neglect syndrome:** Parietal lobe damage that causes difficulty paying attention to the space opposite the side of the brain damage
- Neuroscience in court: brain imaging is used to evaluate "dangerousness"; offenders with low anterior cingulate cortex activity had twice the risk of re-offending

## Mirror neurons and lateralization {pp. 124–127}
- **Mirror neurons:** Discovered by Giacomo Rizzolatti's team in the early 1990s; neurons that fire both when a monkey performs an action and when it watches someone else do it; thought to help us understand others' actions and intentions
- **Lateralization:** The localization of a function in either the right or the left cerebral hemisphere
- Movement and sensation on the right side of the body are processed by the left hemisphere, and vice versa; the left hemisphere's visual cortex processes the right half of the visual field
- **Split-brain operation:** In the 1960s, surgeons cut the corpus callosum to treat life-threatening seizures; it produced no changes in personality, intelligence, or speech (studied by Gazzaniga)
- Language is lateralized to the left hemisphere for most people: about 96% of right-handed people and about 70% of left-handed people
- About 90% of people are right-handed and 10% left-handed; only about 65–70% of chimpanzees are right-handed
- Suspected left-hemisphere processes: mathematical computation and logical reasoning; suspected right-hemisphere processes: some music functions, spatial information, intuition, and the visual arts
- Right-hemisphere damage impairs the comprehension and expression of emotion more than left-hemisphere damage
- Roger Sperry won the 1981 Nobel Prize for studying lateralization and warned against "right brain/left brain" myths; hemisphere dominance does not predict occupation or artistic talent
- Lateralization may allow multitasking and may have made language possible, but it might account for vulnerability to schizophrenia and autism spectrum disorder; people with schizophrenia are more likely to be left-handed or have ambiguous handedness

## EMDR and the amygdala (Thinking Scientifically) {p. 119}
- **Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR):** A psychotherapy developed by Francine Shapiro in the 1980s; the client recalls disturbing images followed by side-to-side eye movements (or hand-tapping)
- EMDR is considered an "evidence-based" treatment, although the research suffers from small samples and possible researcher bias
- fMRI research suggests the eye movements reduce amygdala activity, but other working memory tasks reduce it just as well

## The peripheral nervous system {pp. 129–131}
- **Somatic nervous system:** The part of the PNS that brings sensory information to the CNS and transmits commands to the muscles (voluntary movement); uses the 31 pairs of spinal nerves and 12 pairs of cranial nerves
- **Autonomic nervous system:** The division of the PNS that directs the activity of glands, organs, and smooth muscles; works like the body's "cruise control"; has sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions
- **Sympathetic nervous system:** The division of the autonomic nervous system that coordinates arousal; prepares the body for fight-or-flight (heart races, rapid breathing, pale face, sweaty palms, dilated pupils)
- **Parasympathetic nervous system:** The division of the autonomic nervous system associated with rest, repair, and energy storage (constricts pupils, slows the heart, stimulates digestion)
- The sympathetic and parasympathetic systems usually have opposite (antagonistic) effects on an organ, but they cooperate during sexual activity
- **Enteric nervous system:** Nerve cells embedded in the lining of the gastrointestinal system; called a "second brain"; has as many nerve cells as the spinal cord
  - Communicates with the vagus nerve, forming a gut-brain axis that also carries information about the gut's microbiome
  - Source of 95% of the body's serotonin; disturbances might contribute to autism spectrum disorder

| Cranial nerve | Function |
|---|---|
| I. Olfactory | Smell |
| II. Optic | Vision |
| III. Oculomotor, IV. Trochlear, VI. Abducens | Eye movements |
| V. Trigeminal | Touch, pain, jaw muscles |
| VII. Facial | Face muscles, taste |
| VIII. Auditory | Hearing, balance |
| IX. Glossopharyngeal | Taste, muscles of throat and larynx |
| X. Vagus | Internal organs (parasympathetic) |
| XI. Spinal accessory | Neck muscles |
| XII. Hypoglossal | Tongue movements |

## The endocrine system {pp. 132–133}
- **Endocrine system:** A system of glands that release hormones into the bloodstream; especially involved with arousal, metabolism, growth, and sex; responds to input from the nervous system, especially the hypothalamus
- **Pineal gland:** Releases melatonin, which maintains sleep–wake cycles; melatonin is released in the early evening and breaks down in light (some travelers use it for jet lag)
- **Pituitary gland:** The body's "master gland," located just above the roof of the mouth and regulated by the hypothalamus; many of its hormones activate other glands
  - Releases oxytocin (breastfeeding; bonding, cooperation, trust), vasopressin (fluid levels), and human growth hormone; controls sex hormones that start puberty and maintain fertility
- **Thyroid gland:** Located below the larynx; raises or lowers metabolism; low thyroid levels can mimic the symptoms of depression
- **Adrenal glands:** Located above the kidneys; at times of stress they release hormones including cortisol, a general "wake-up message"
- **Islets of Langerhans:** Located in the pancreas; produce hormones essential to digestion, including insulin
- Sexual desire is driven mainly by testosterone (males) and estrogens (females), while romantic bonding is largely associated with oxytocin; the posterior insula is linked to sexual desire and the anterior insula to romantic love

## Neurons and glia {pp. 134–137}
- **Neuron:** A cell of the nervous system specialized to send and receive neural messages
- **Glia:** Nervous system cells that perform support functions: holding neurons in place, cleaning up debris, and forming the blood–brain barrier and myelin ("glia" comes from the Greek word for "glue")
- **Cell body:** The large, central mass of a neuron, containing the nucleus; handles housekeeping tasks
- **Axon:** The branch of a neuron that usually transmits information to other neurons (each neuron typically has only one)
- **Dendrite:** A branch from the cell body that usually receives input from other neurons
- **Synaptic vesicles:** Round, hollow spheres in the axon terminal that contain molecules of chemical messengers
- Gray matter has a high density of cell bodies; white matter is made of large bundles of myelinated axons
- **Blood–brain barrier:** Formed by glia's tight connections with blood vessels; prevents many toxins from entering brain tissue; psychoactive drugs are substances that can penetrate it with ease
- **Myelin:** The insulating material covering some axons; makes neural signaling fast and energy efficient (up to 3,000 times more information per second)
  - Myelination of the human nervous system takes more than 20 years to complete
  - Sharp "ouch" pain travels on myelinated axons; dull, achy pain travels on unmyelinated axons
- Glia in the CNS form scar tissue, so CNS damage is considered permanent; glia in the PNS help damaged axons regrow

## Neural signaling {pp. 137–141}
- Neural communication is a two-step process: an electrical signal within a neuron, then a chemical messenger between neurons
- Squid axons (Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952) can be as much as 1 millimeter in diameter, large enough to record from easily
- **Resting potential:** The electrical charge across a neural membrane when the neuron is not processing information; the interior is negative relative to the exterior
- **Depolarization:** The difference between the inside and outside charges decreases (moves toward threshold)
- **Hyperpolarization:** The difference between the inside and outside charges increases (moves away from threshold)
- **Threshold:** The point at which an action potential is initiated; once reached, the action potential is inevitable
- **Action potential:** The electrical signal arising in a neuron's axon; sodium channels open and positive sodium ions rush in, then potassium ions leave the cell
- **Refractory period:** A time-out after an action potential when the cell is unable or unlikely to fire again while it returns to rest
- **All-or-none:** Action potentials are always the same size and shape; either one occurs or the cell stays at rest
- **Propagation:** The duplication of the electrical signal down the length of the axon to the axon terminal
- **Nodes of Ranvier:** Gaps between segments of myelin where action potentials form; signals "skip" myelinated sections and travel about 20 times faster
- Puffer fish (fugu) toxin blocks the movement of sodium into cells, causing paralysis

## Chemical signaling at the synapse {pp. 141–143}
- **Synapse:** A point of communication between two neurons; the neurons do not touch but are separated by tiny gaps of fluid
- **Neurotransmitter:** A chemical messenger that communicates across a synapse; more than 50 have been identified
- **Receptor:** A special channel in a neuron's membrane that interacts with neurotransmitters, like a lock and key
- **Reuptake:** Neurotransmitter molecules in the synaptic gap are returned to the axon terminal that released them (through transporters) to be repackaged; Prozac (fluoxetine), cocaine, and amphetamines interfere with reuptake
- **Excitation:** A neurotransmitter slightly depolarizes the receiving neuron, making an action potential more likely
- **Inhibition:** A neurotransmitter slightly hyperpolarizes the receiving neuron, making an action potential less likely
- Tetanus ("lockjaw") toxin damages inhibitory neurons in the motor system, leaving only excitation and extreme muscle contraction
- **Summation:** The neuron adds up all incoming excitatory and inhibitory messages to "decide" whether to fire an action potential

## Important neurotransmitters (Table 4.4) {pp. 141–145}
| Neurotransmitter | Behaviors influenced |
|---|---|
| Acetylcholine (ACh) | Movement, memory, autonomic nervous system function |
| Epinephrine (adrenalin) | Arousal |
| Norepinephrine (noradrenalin) | Arousal, vigilance |
| Dopamine | Movement, planning, reward |
| Serotonin | Mood, appetite, sleep, dominance/aggression |
| Glutamate | Excitation of brain activity |
| GABA | Inhibition of brain activity |
| Endorphins | Pain management |

- Acetylcholine works at the neuromuscular junction; its learning and memory circuits are among the first to deteriorate in Alzheimer's disease; nicotine acts on ACh systems
- Curare causes paralysis by blocking ACh receptors; Botox causes paralysis by preventing the release of ACh
- Norepinephrine is released by the sympathetic nervous system; abnormal norepinephrine activity is implicated in bipolar disorder and PTSD
- Parkinson's disease results when dopamine-releasing neurons in the brain's movement circuits die
- Most addictive drugs, including cocaine and methamphetamine, increase activity in dopamine circuits; disrupted dopamine circuits are implicated in schizophrenia and ADHD
- Depressed people often have lower-than-normal serotonin activity at the synapse
- **SSRI:** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (e.g., Prozac); inhibiting serotonin reuptake keeps more serotonin in the synaptic gap longer, relieving depression
- **Endorphins:** Short for "endogenous morphine"; modify our natural response to pain; "runner's high" results from endorphin release
- Opioid drugs such as morphine, heroin, and oxycodone (OxyContin) relieve pain by mimicking endorphins at the synapse

## Loneliness and the nervous system {p. 145}
- Loneliness produces a watchful anticipation of threat in the sympathetic nervous system (rather than fight or flight), which might be associated with high blood pressure
- When viewing pleasant pictures, lonely people's reward pathways responded more to objects than to people; non-lonely people responded more to people
