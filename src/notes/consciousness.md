## What is consciousness? {pp. 193–199}
- **Consciousness:** Knowing or being aware of ongoing experiences occurring both internally and in the world around us (from the Latin conscientia, "to know")
- Consciousness can mean a state of awareness (alert vs. drowsy), the content of awareness (what you are attending to right now), or self-awareness
- **Self-awareness:** The special understanding of the self as distinct from other stimuli
- William James coined the term "stream of consciousness"
- Sigmund Freud used "consciousness" for parts of the mind that can be retrieved voluntarily, in contrast to the unconscious parts that stay hidden
- Benjamin Libet found a "readiness potential" in the EEG about 350 milliseconds before a person's conscious intent to flex a finger, sparking debate about free will
- Split-brain patients who stood up after the word "stand" was shown to the right hemisphere said they needed to stretch; this supports a view of consciousness as an **interpreter** that makes sense of our actions
- Sleep varies with predation risk and shelter: wild horses (sleep in the open) sleep only 1 or 2 hours per day, while wild rabbits (sleep in burrows) sleep at least 8 hours
- **Rouge test (mirror test):** A dot of dye is placed on a child's forehead; at about 18 months children rub the spot, showing self-awareness; chimpanzees, orangutans, dolphins, Asian elephants, magpies, and parrots have also passed
- Weak AI shows intelligent behavior without conscious states; strong AI would have the full range of human cognition; the Turing test (Alan Turing, 1950) asks whether a person can tell a machine from a human in conversation

## Consciousness and the brain {pp. 197–198}
- Consciousness requires complex interactions between the cerebral cortex and the thalamus; lesions of the thalamus produce profound unconsciousness like that of brain death
- The thalamus and reticular formation "enable" consciousness (raising or lowering thresholds of awareness) but do not produce its content
- **Default mode network (DMN):** Brain network that maintains high background activity during mind wandering and helps prepare the brain for conscious thought; linked to thinking about the self, others, and one's past and future
  - The brain uses only about 5% more energy when focused than when unfocused; DMN activity decreases slightly during attention-demanding tasks
  - Abnormal DMN connectivity has been observed in autism spectrum disorder and Alzheimer's disease

## Circadian rhythms {pp. 199–202}
- **Waking:** A normal state of consciousness characterized by alertness and awareness of external stimuli
- **Sleep:** A normal state of consciousness characterized by reduced awareness of external stimuli
- **Circadian rhythm:** A daily biological rhythm ("circadian" = "about a day")
- Within a day, arousal also peaks every 90 to 120 minutes
- **Biological clock:** An internal mechanism, controlled by the hypothalamus, that provides an approximate schedule for physical processes such as sleep and waking, body temperature, and hunger
- **Zeitgebers:** External "time givers" that reset biological clocks; light is one of the most important for humans, and eating patterns are also significant
- Without light, internal clocks run a few minutes longer than 24 hours (totally blind people and submarine sailors have longer cycles)
- Artificial light (including tablets and phones) breaks down melatonin, a hormone released in the evening that regulates sleep
- **Social jet lag:** Disrupted sleep from inconsistent weekday and weekend sleep times; associated with overweight, obesity, and metabolic syndrome
- **Shift maladaptation syndrome:** Health, personality, mood, and interpersonal problems experienced by 40% to 80% of graveyard-shift workers (11:00 p.m. to 7:30 a.m.); the graveyard shift is the most dangerous work period
- **Jet lag:** Fatigue, irritability, and sleepiness from crossing time zones; traveling east (setting the clock forward) is harder than traveling west
- Daylight saving time: the spring shift forward brought a 7% increase in traffic accidents and more heart attacks; the fall shift back brought a 7% decrease
- **Major depressive disorder with seasonal pattern:** Depression that occurs regularly at the same time each year, usually in winter; formerly called seasonal affective disorder (SAD); affects about 4 to 6% of people; treated with bright light, with or without melatonin or antidepressants
- Seasonal depression rates range from 1.4% in Florida to 9.7% in New Hampshire
- "Morning" people are called larks and "night" people owls; nearly everyone acts like an owl in adolescence and young adulthood, returning to their earlier pattern in their mid to late 20s (possibly a sign the brain is fully mature)
- The average American adult spends only 6 hours and 55 minutes in bed

## EEG waves and stages of sleep {pp. 202–206}
| EEG wave | Frequency | Usually indicates |
|---|---|---|
| Gamma wave | More than 30 cycles per second | Attention to sensory input (especially visual) |
| Beta wave | 15 to 30 cycles per second | Alert waking (rapid, irregular, low amplitude) |
| Alpha wave | 9 to 12 cycles per second | Relaxed waking (also linked to daydreaming and meditation) |
| Theta wave | 4 to 7 cycles per second | Lighter stages of N-REM sleep |
| Delta wave | 1 to 4 cycles per second | Deep N-REM sleep (largest, slowest wave) |

![EEG waves from fastest to slowest](eeg-waves)

- **Daydreaming (mind wandering):** Spontaneous experiences in a no-task, no-stimulus, no-response situation; correlated with DMN activity and alpha waves; we often think about the past and plan the future, so it is not a "waste of time"
- We spend one-third of our lives sleeping
- **Non–rapid eye movement (N-REM) sleep:** Sleep characterized by theta and delta wave activity and deep physical relaxation; divided into four stages
- Stage 1: occurs when we first fall asleep; some theta waves; people may not know they were asleep
- Stage 2: begins after 10 to 15 minutes; K complexes and sleep spindles appear (only in N-REM sleep); spindles are linked to memory consolidation, and fewer spindles are characteristic of Alzheimer's disease
- Stages 3 and 4: delta waves (stage 4 has the most); the deepest sleep; awakening is difficult and causes disorientation
- **Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep:** Sleep with EEG waves resembling waking, rapid eye movements, muscle paralysis, and sympathetic nervous system activation; also called paradoxical sleep
- The first REM episode occurs 90 to 120 minutes after sleep onset; awakened REM sleepers usually report vivid, storylike dreams
- REM paralysis prevents acting out dreams; in **REM sleep behavior disorder**, the paralysis fails and people may injure themselves or their partners
- Sleepwalking occurs in stages 3 and 4 of N-REM sleep (not REM, because the body is paralyzed); sleep talking is most common in lighter N-REM stages
- The first 4 hours of the night are dominated by N-REM sleep (most stage 3 and 4 sleep); the second 4 hours feature large amounts of REM sleep
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale:** A score greater than 10 indicates excessive daytime sleepiness

## Why we sleep {pp. 206–208}
- Sleepiness contributed to disasters including the Three Mile Island meltdown, the Challenger explosion, and the Exxon Valdez grounding
- Staying awake for 17 to 19 straight hours produces worse reaction time than being legally drunk; sleep deprivation can also lead to obesity and type 2 diabetes
- **Restorative function:** Stages 3 and 4 of N-REM sleep repair the body; nearly all human growth hormone is released during stages 3 and 4
- Sleep deprivation slows healing, reduces immune activity, and reduces new neurons in adult brains; N-REM sleep decreases about 30 minutes per decade after age 50
- **Memory function:** Memories are reactivated and transformed for long-term storage during N-REM sleep, and REM sleep stabilizes them; forgetting unnecessary information also happens during sleep
- **REM rebound:** After REM deprivation, people spend an unusually large amount of sleep time in REM sleep
- Only birds and mammals show clear REM sleep
- Human infants spend about half their sleep in REM (premature infants up to 80%) compared with 20% in adolescents and adults; REM may help "wire" the immature brain
- People with depression enter REM sleep earlier and spend more time in REM; antidepressants suppress REM sleep; people with bipolar disorder have an abnormally low need for sleep
- Brainstem neurons that release serotonin and norepinephrine are especially quiet during REM sleep

## Dreams {pp. 208–209}
- **Dreaming:** A mental state that usually occurs during sleep and features visual imagery
- Freud's The Interpretation of Dreams argued that the unconscious mind expresses itself symbolically through dreams
- **Activation–synthesis theory:** Dream content reflects the mind's effort to make sense of ongoing neural activity (e.g., dreams of being unable to move mirror REM paralysis; flying or falling dreams reflect vestibular activity)
- Dreaming and daydreaming are on a continuum; dream circuits overlap with the DMN; lower frontal lobe activity may explain bizarre dream content
- **Lucid dreaming:** A conscious awareness of dreaming accompanied by the ability to control the dream's content; about 55% of adults report at least one; linked to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and more common in people with narcolepsy
- **Nightmare:** An especially upsetting dream that occurs during REM sleep

## Sleep disorders {pp. 208–210}
- **Sleep terror:** A sleep disorder in N-REM sleep (usually the first 3 hours) in which the sleeper wakes suddenly in great distress without the imagery of a nightmare; usually not remembered; twin studies suggest a genetic predisposition
- **Insomnia:** A sleep disorder characterized by an inability to either initiate or maintain normal sleep; the most common sleep problem after nightmares
  - Onset insomnia: trouble falling asleep, often caused by stress and anxiety
  - Maintenance insomnia: frequently interrupted sleep or early waking, typically from stress, substance use, or psychological disorders
  - Zolpidem (Ambien) produces sleep only about 15 minutes faster and lengthens it about 30 minutes, with side effects such as dependence and sleepwalking; amber glasses that block blue light may help
- Most people need about 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night
- **Narcolepsy:** A sleep disorder characterized by the intrusion of REM sleep phenomena ("sleep attacks") into waking; linked to missing or damaged orexin-secreting cells in the hypothalamus (genetic vulnerability plus autoimmune processes); Harriet Tubman is suspected to have had narcolepsy
- **Cataplexy:** REM-type muscle paralysis during wakefulness without loss of consciousness
- **Sleep paralysis:** Muscle paralysis just before sleep or lingering after waking; ended simply by touching the person
- **Sleep apnea:** A sleep disorder in which the person stops breathing while asleep, then wakes gasping for air; increases heart disease risk; treated with weight loss, airway surgery, or a machine that regulates airflow
- **Sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS):** A healthy infant dies while asleep; about 3,500 U.S. infants per year; rates were cut in half after the "back to sleep" recommendation; the most common cause of death in the first 6 months of life in industrialized nations
  - AAP prevention advice: back to sleep, firm surface, room-sharing without bed-sharing, no soft bedding or overheating, no smoke, alcohol, or drugs, breastfeeding, immunization, pacifiers, and skin-to-skin care
- **Restless legs syndrome (RLS):** Involuntary movement of an extremity, usually one leg, with a tingling feeling; reported by 15% of U.S. adults; common in people with ADHD (both linked to dopamine)
- Good sleep hygiene: a regular sleep schedule, monitoring caffeine and stimulants, daily sunlight, limiting screen time before sleep, and regular exercise

## Consciousness and brain damage {pp. 211–214}
- **Prosopagnosia:** Inability to recognize faces, caused by damage to the fusiform face area of the temporal lobe; patients may still show autonomic responses to familiar faces
- **Capgras syndrome:** Belief that imposters have replaced familiar people; face recognition is intact but the feeling of familiarity is distorted (the opposite of prosopagnosia)
- **Coma:** A profound state of unconsciousness ("deep sleep" in Greek) with no sleep–wake cycles, no response to pain or light, and no voluntary behavior; usually caused by disruption of the reticular formation or both cerebral hemispheres
  - Comas last from a few days to several weeks; after 2 to 4 weeks most patients recover, move into a vegetative state, or die
- **Vegetative state (VS):** An abnormal state following brain injury featuring wakefulness without consciousness (eyes open, sleep–wake cycles); about 20% of patients diagnosed as VS are actually conscious but unable to respond
- **Brain death:** An irreversible loss of all functions of the brain, including the brainstem
- **Near-death experience:** An altered state reported by people close to death, featuring out-of-body experiences, light-at-the-end-of-a-tunnel perceptions, and calmness; reported by as many as 1 in 10 people
- **Seizure:** An abnormal level of brain activation with a sudden onset; people with recurring seizures are diagnosed with epilepsy; drugs that enhance GABA (like barbiturates) prevent or control seizures
  - Partial seizures start in a focal area and often have an aura; temporal lobe partial seizures can cause déjà vu ("already seen") or jamais vu ("never seen")
  - Generalized seizures do not start in a focal area and cause complete loss of consciousness: tonic-clonic seizures (loss of consciousness, intense contractions, then violent rhythmic contractions) and absence seizures (blinking, head turns, eye movements)

## Altering consciousness without drugs {pp. 224–227}
- **Hypnosis:** An altered state of consciousness characterized by relaxation and increased suggestibility (focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness)
  - Hypnotized people do not comply with dangerous requests more than non-hypnotized people
  - Hypnosis does not sharpen memory and is likely to make it worse
  - Legitimate uses include habit change (such as quitting smoking), relieving irritable bowel syndrome, and pain relief
  - Highly suggestible people given a posthypnotic suggestion nearly eliminated the Stroop effect
- **Stroop test:** People name the ink color of color words; reaction is 100–200 milliseconds slower when the word and ink color do not match
- **Meditation:** A voluntary alteration of consciousness characterized by positive emotion and absence of thought
- **Mindfulness:** "Nonjudgmental awareness of experiences in the present moment"; mindfulness meditation is used to treat substance abuse and various psychological disorders
- Meditation benefits immune function, blood pressure, stress hormone levels, and cognition; it increases alpha waves, reduces DMN activity (linked to bliss), and is associated with increased cortical thickness
- Other methods: high altitudes, scuba diving, starvation, sleep deprivation, sensory deprivation, twirling (Sufi whirling), drumming and dancing, and Native American vision quests; these may reduce prefrontal cortex activity
- Rhythmic drumming increases theta waves; raves combine dance and drugs with goals of unity and positive emotion
- We unconsciously mimic others; socially excluded people mimic more, and mimicry increases liking
- The COVID-19 pandemic brought lasting insomnia problems (a 37% increase in clinically significant insomnia in China)

## Sleep disorders and mental health (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Sleep Disorders](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/sleep-disorders/)
- Sleep problems can be secondary to a medical illness such as sleep apnea or a mental health condition such as depression, and can signal an upcoming condition such as bipolar disorder
- **Insomnia (NAMI):** Being unable to get the amount of sleep needed to function well during the day, because of trouble falling asleep, trouble staying asleep, or waking too early
- About 1 in 3 people in the U.S. report difficulty sleeping at least one night per week
- Insomnia is rarely an isolated illness; it is usually a symptom of another illness or a result of lifestyle or work schedule
- **Sleep apnea (NAMI):** A separate medical condition that affects breathing during sleep and can cause insomnia
- Short-term insomnia (from stress or travel) often improves with sleep hygiene; long-term insomnia lasts more than three weeks and should be checked by a physician
- About 50% of insomnia cases are related to depression, anxiety or psychological stress
- Early morning wakefulness can be a sign of depression, while a sudden big drop in sleep with more energy may be a sign of mania
- Panic attacks during sleep may suggest panic disorder, nightmares may be linked to PTSD, and OCD is often associated with poor sleep
- Alcohol is sedating in small amounts, but intoxication causes repeated waking at night; LSD and ecstasy also interrupt sleep
- Good sleep habits (a regular schedule, avoiding stimulating activity before bed, a comfortable sleep environment) are the first-line treatment for insomnia
- Other insomnia treatments: relaxation techniques, medication (usually for no more than a few weeks), CBT, exercise, and light therapy (phototherapy) for delayed sleep phase syndrome
- **Sleep restriction:** Therapy that improves sleep efficiency by strictly limiting time awake in bed, then gradually increasing time in bed
- Melatonin and valerian root are herbal remedies whose effectiveness has not been proven for most people, and neither is FDA approved

## HOSA Canada study deck: consciousness
@ HOSA Canada Behavioural Health study deck "The Mind (Consciousness)" (HOSA Canada app, Module 2)
- Brain death is shown by two flatline EEG recordings taken 24 hours apart, or by a lack of blood circulation to the brain; the patient shows no response to pain and no cranial-nerve reflexes
- Alcohol at low doses dilates blood vessels (a warm, flushed feeling) and reduces anxiety; high doses lead to aggression, risky behavior and poor coordination; very high doses can cause coma and death from suppressed breathing or choking on vomit
- Marijuana acts on the brain's own (endogenous) cannabinoid system; most users feel some excitation, vivid imagery and mild euphoria, but some respond with depression and social withdrawal
- HOSA Canada summary: varying states of alertness may help animals repair their bodies, conserve energy and stay safe, while conscious awareness of sensations lets us choose a response instead of reacting only by reflex or instinct
