## Roots of psychology {pp. 5–9}
- **Psychology:** The scientific study of behavior, mental processes, and brain functions (from the Greek psyche, "mind," and logos, "the objective study of")
- **Mind:** The brain and its activities, including thought, emotion, and behavior
- **Introspection:** A personal observation of your own thoughts, feelings, and behavior; hard for others to confirm, so it does not fit the scientific method well
- Psychology is a relatively young discipline, dating only to the 1870s; people in Assyria described their dreams as long ago as 6000 to 5000 BCE
- Psychology has two major roots: philosophy and the natural sciences
- **Philosophy:** The discipline that systematically examines basic concepts, including the source of knowledge
- **Natural sciences:** Sciences that study the physical and biological events that occur in nature
- Aristotle (384–322 BCE) believed all knowledge is gained through sensory experience; British empiricists such as John Locke viewed the mind as a "blank slate" at birth
- Microscopes (Anton von Leeuwenhoek discovered red blood cells in 1676) and discoveries about sensory nerves showed the mind was physical, not magical
- Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894) measured reaction time to touches on the thigh and toe, showing the mind could be studied scientifically
- Gustav Fechner (1801–1887) measured the softest sound a person could hear (psychophysics)
- Ancient people may have drilled holes in the skull (trepanation) to cure headaches, seizures, or psychological disorders
- Psychology is one of the seven major "hub sciences," strongly connected to medicine, the social sciences, and education

## Early schools of psychology {pp. 10–19}
- **Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920):** Considered the first psychologist; conducted the first documented psychological experiment in 1879 (reaction time to a ball dropping onto a platform) and opened the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany
- **Structuralism:** An approach in which the mind is broken into the smallest elements of mental experience (Edward Titchener, a student of Wundt)
- **Gestalt psychology:** An approach that saw experience as being different from the sum of its elements ("gestalt" = form or whole); founded by Kurt Koffka, Max Wertheimer, and Wolfgang Köhler
- **Functionalism:** An approach that saw behavior as purposeful and contributing to survival; influenced by Charles Darwin's The Origin of Species (1859)
- **William James (1842–1910):** Chief proponent of functionalism; his textbook Principles of Psychology (1890) dominated psychology for 50 years; coined "stream of consciousness"
- **Mary Whiton Calkins (1863–1930):** A student of William James who could not officially register at Harvard because of her gender; studied memory and the self and became the first female president of the American Psychological Association in 1905
- **Sigmund Freud (1856–1939):** A physician whose psychodynamic theory dominated the first half of the 20th century; ideas on the unconscious mind, dream analysis, and psychoanalysis; his theories are hard to test experimentally
- Historically, the most common view of psychological disorders was the supernatural approach (evil spirits), with harsh "treatments" such as spinning devices
- Between the 17th and 19th centuries the supernatural view gave way to a **medical model** (physical causes, medical treatments) and a **psychological model** (traumatic life experiences)
- **Humanistic psychology:** An approach that saw people as inherently good and motivated to learn and improve; called a "third force" separate from behaviorism and psychodynamic theory
- **Abraham Maslow (1908–1970):** Humanistic psychologist interested in what makes a person "good"; theory of motivation
- **Carl Rogers (1902–1987):** Developed client-centered therapy, calling people clients rather than patients
- **Behaviorism:** An approach that concentrated on observable, measurable behavior (often studying animals) for about the first half of the 20th century
- **Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (1849–1936):** Discovered classical conditioning while studying digestion in dogs
- **John B. Watson (1878–1958):** Believed in the blank-slate approach; later applied behaviorism to advertising (pairing products with appealing images)
- **Edward Thorndike (1874–1949):** Proposed the law of effect from studying cats in puzzle boxes
- **B. F. Skinner (1904–1990):** Studied the effects of consequences using Skinner boxes; treated thinking and feeling as behaviors following the same rules
- **Cognitive revolution:** By the 1950s, scientists from linguistics and computer science challenged behaviorism; Ulric Neisser named the field cognitive psychology in his 1967 book
- Allen Newell and Herbert Simon wrote early artificial intelligence programs; by the 1990s cognitive neuroscience emerged
- **Francis Cecil Sumner (1895–1954):** In 1920 became the first African American to receive a doctorate in psychology
- Kenneth and Mamie Clark's doll studies (1939–1940) were used as evidence in U.S. Supreme Court hearings on the harm of segregated schools
- Gail Wyatt became the first African American woman licensed as a psychologist in California in 1975

## History of mental health treatment {pp. 553–554, 599–616}
- The first official U.S. effort to gather data on psychological disorders was the single category "idiocy/insanity" in the 1840 census
- **Philippe Pinel (1745–1826):** "The father of modern psychiatry"; removed patients' iron shackles (following Jean-Baptiste Pussin's example) and replaced bleeding, purging, and blistering with conversation
- **Benjamin Rush (1749–1813):** "The father of American psychiatry"; believed disorders were caused by "irritation" of blood vessels in the brain; invented the tranquilizer chair; wrote Medical Inquiries and Observations Upon Diseases of the Mind (1812)
- **Franz Anton Mesmer (1734–1815):** Used hypnosis to treat hysteria (gives us the word "mesmerized"); Benjamin Franklin denounced his methods as fraudulent
- Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893) gave hypnosis legitimacy; Josef Breuer and Sigmund Freud admired his work; Freud later abandoned hypnosis for free association
- **Hysteria:** A 19th-century condition that would be diagnosed today as somatic symptom disorder
- The discovery that untreated syphilis causes hallucinations and delusions showed abnormal behavior can have biological causes
- 1937: Charles Bradley found a stimulant (Benzedrine) dramatically improved children's school performance, leading to stimulant treatment for ADHD
- 1949: John Cade discovered lithium for bipolar disorder (approved in the U.S. in 1970)
- Chlorpromazine (Thorazine), introduced after Henri Laborit's observations, began the era of antipsychotic medication
- U.S. institutionalized populations dropped by about 50% between 1955 and 2000 as patients on medication returned to their communities
- Frontal lobotomies: Egas Moniz won the 1949 Nobel Prize; Walter Freeman performed more than 3,400 lobotomies; 40,000 to 50,000 patients received the procedure between the 1930s and 1950s
- ECT was first used for schizophrenia in the 1930s (Ladislas Meduna) with poor results, then for mood disorders
- The DSM was first published by the American Psychiatric Association in 1952 and has become less Freudian and more culturally sensitive
- In 2002, New Mexico became the first state to allow specially trained psychologists to prescribe medication

## SAMHSA: history and mission (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: About Us](https://www.samhsa.gov/about) · [FAQs](https://www.samhsa.gov/about/faqs) · [Strategic Priorities](https://www.samhsa.gov/about/strategic-priorities)
- **SAMHSA:** The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, established by Congress in 1992; the agency within the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) that leads public health efforts to advance the behavioral health of the nation
- In December 2016, the **21st Century Cures Act** reauthorized SAMHSA and established the position of Assistant Secretary for Mental Health and Substance Use
- SAMHSA's vision: people with, affected by, or at risk for mental health and substance use conditions receive care, achieve well-being, and thrive
- SAMHSA helps the nation act on the knowledge that behavioral health is essential to health, prevention works, treatment is effective, and people recover
- SAMHSA mission (Strategic Priorities): leads public health and service delivery efforts that treat mental illness, especially serious mental illness, prevent substance abuse and addiction, and provide treatments and supports to foster recovery
- **SAMHSA's five priority areas (FAQ page):** Preventing Overdose; Enhancing Access to Suicide Prevention and Crisis Care; Promoting Resilience and Emotional Health for Children, Youth and Families; Integrating Behavioral and Physical Health Care; and Strengthening the Behavioral Health Workforce
- **SAMHSA's six strategic priorities:** preventing substance misuse, abuse, and addiction; addressing serious mental illness; expanding crisis intervention care and services; improving access to evidence-based treatment for mental illness, substance use, and co-occurring disorders; helping individuals achieve long-term recovery and sobriety; and identifying and addressing emerging behavioral health threats
- SAMHSA's three cross-cutting principles: its work is data-driven, innovative, and based on gold-standard science
- Programs named under the strategic priorities: a HEP-C Initiative, State Opioid Response Grant supplements for recovery housing, expanding Assisted Outpatient Treatment and Civil Commitment, a Dear Colleague Letter ending harm reduction practices, and a Technical Expert Panel on Ending Homelessness
- Emerging threats named by SAMHSA: illicitly made fentanyl and other potent synthetic opioids such as nitazenes, xylazine, medetomidine, illicit benzodiazepines, and increasing polysubstance abuse
- Oral antiviral medications can cure hepatitis C in 8 to 12 weeks, giving the U.S. the opportunity to eliminate hepatitis C
- Key outcomes SAMHSA tracks include fewer overdose and suicide deaths, lower rates of substance misuse, mental illness, suicidal ideation, and homelessness, and more people in treatment and recovery

## 2024 national data (SAMHSA, NSDUH)
- 2024 NSDUH: 48.4 million Americans 12 or older (16.8%) met criteria for a past-year SUD: 27.9 million with alcohol use disorder, 28.2 million with drug use disorders, and 7.7 million with both
- Young adults 18–25 had the highest SUD rate: 25.9% (9.1 million)
- 2024 NSDUH: 61.5 million adults (23.4%) had a past-year mental illness, including 14.6 million (5.6%) with serious mental illness
- Among adolescents 12–17 in 2023, 15.4% (3.8 million) had a past-year major depressive episode and 18.8% (4.9 million) had moderate or severe anxiety symptoms

- **Center for Mental Health Services (CMHS):** Leads federal efforts to promote prevention, treatment, and recovery supports for Americans with mental health conditions; Congress created CMHS to bring new hope to adults who have serious mental illness and children with serious emotional disturbances
- **Center for Substance Abuse Prevention (CSAP):** Advances prevention across the lifespan; aims to prevent initiation of substance use, prevent progression of substance use, and prevent consequences associated with substance use
- **Center for Substance Abuse Treatment (CSAT):** Promotes community-based substance use disorder treatment and recovery support services; works to close the gap between available treatment capacity and demand
- **Center for Behavioral Health Statistics and Quality (CBHSQ):** The lead federal agency for behavioral health data and research; codified by the 21st Century Cures Act (December 2016)
- CBHSQ data systems: the **National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH)**; the **Behavioral Health Services Information System** (connects people to treatment through the Behavioral Health Treatment Services Locator); and the **Drug Abuse Warning Network (DAWN)**, which monitors emergency department visits related to recent substance use
- **Office of Tribal Affairs and Policy (OTAP):** SAMHSA's primary point of contact for tribal governments on behavioral health issues facing American Indians and Alaska Natives; brings together tribal affairs, policy, consultation, advisory, and Tribal Law and Order Act responsibilities
- The **National Tribal Behavioral Health Agenda (TBHA)** is a blueprint for collaborative action by tribes, federal partners, and states on five foundational elements; the **Tribal Action Plan (TAP)** helps tribes prevent, treat, and recover from SUDs
- SAMHSA grants for tribes include Tribal Opioid Response Grants, Circles of Care, and Native Connections

## NAMI history
@ [NAMI: Who We Are](https://www.nami.org/who-we-are/)
- **NAMI (National Alliance on Mental Illness):** The nation's largest grassroots mental health organization, dedicated to building better lives for Americans affected by mental illness
- NAMI started in 1979 as a small group of families gathered around a kitchen table
- Today NAMI is an alliance of more than 650 local Affiliates and 49 State Organizations
- NAMI challenges stigma, champions supportive policies, and supports and empowers individuals and families
- Most of NAMI's funding comes from individual donors and contributors
- NAMI advocated for the 2020 National Suicide Hotline Designation Act that created 988, and in 2025 helped get the FDA to remove the clozapine REMS paperwork barrier
