## Diagnosing ADHD (textbook) {pp. 558–560}
- **Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD):** A disorder characterized by either unusual inattentiveness, hyperactivity with impulsivity, or both
- ADHD is one of the most contentious DSM-5 categories because its criteria are hard to distinguish from typical childhood behavior
- Some people show both inattention and hyperactivity; others show only one
- Core feature of inattention: inability to maintain sustained attention or on-task behavior for an age-appropriate time (trouble following instructions, organizing work, avoiding careless mistakes)
- Hyperactivity: high motor activity, trouble in structured activities like waiting in line or sitting quietly, acting without thinking
- Adults diagnosed with ADHD as children have more traffic accidents
- Fewer than 40% of surveyed pediatricians used DSM criteria, and more than half of children taking ADHD medication did not meet even relaxed criteria
- Using DSM criteria, 3% to 5% of children should be diagnosed, but in 2011, 11% of U.S. children aged 4–17 had been diagnosed (a 42% increase since 2003)
- About 2.5% of adults over 17 meet criteria for ADHD
- ADHD is diagnosed at least twice as often in males; females are more likely to have inattention without hyperactivity
- Low socioeconomic status is considered a risk factor; diagnosis rates vary widely by region

## Causes and treatment of ADHD (textbook) {pp. 560–561}
- NIMH: "scientists are not sure what causes ADHD," but twin and adoption studies show heritability of 70% or more
- Environmental risks: lead contamination, low birth weight, prenatal exposure to tobacco, alcohol, and other drugs, and markers of prenatal acetaminophen exposure
- The frontal lobes show structural abnormalities and may be underactive; because they inhibit unwanted behavior, low activity may cause hyperactivity and impulsivity
- Smaller volume in the amygdala, basal ganglia, hippocampus, and whole brain; corpus callosum disruption correlates with symptom severity
- ADHD involves delayed brain maturation: peak cortical thickness came at about 7.5 years in typical children but about 10.5 years in children with ADHD
- Brain areas involved (prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia) have heavy dopamine activity; most ADHD medications (methylphenidate/Ritalin, dextroamphetamine/Dexedrine, amphetamine salts/Adderall) boost dopamine, suggesting dopamine activity may be low
- Myths: carefully controlled studies do not support sugar causing hyperactivity, and "poor parenting" is unlikely to cause ADHD, though parents can learn behavior management techniques
- Selective attention may be disrupted in ADHD; restless legs syndrome is common in people with ADHD (both linked to dopamine)

## Treating ADHD (textbook) {pp. 620–622}
- In 1937 Charles Bradley gave a stimulant (Benzedrine) to children with learning and behavior problems and saw "a spectacular improvement in school performance"
- The most commonly prescribed ADHD drugs are the stimulants methylphenidate (Ritalin), dextroamphetamine (Dexedrine or Dextrostat), and amphetamine salts (Adderall), which increase dopamine and norepinephrine
- Side effects: weight loss, sleep disturbance, higher heart rate and blood pressure, and suppressed growth with long use; methylphenidate produces tolerance, so stopping should be medically supervised
- Stimulants raise activity in underactive planning areas (basal ganglia, frontal lobes); it is a myth that they calm only people with ADHD, so they are commonly misused
- Medication use for ADHD in the U.S. is five times higher than in any other nation; 75% to 80% of medicated children improve short-term, but there is no demonstrated long-term benefit into adolescence or adulthood
- Two-thirds of children with ADHD receive medication, but fewer than half receive behavioral treatment; **behavioral parent training** and **behavioral classroom management** are especially effective

## ADHD facts (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: ADHD](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/adhd)
- ADHD is exhibited by not being able to focus, being overactive, not being able to control behavior, or a combination; symptoms must be out of the normal range for a person's age and development
- ADHD is the most commonly diagnosed behavioral disorder in children; it usually begins in childhood but affects many adults as well
- It is not clear what causes ADHD, but a combination of genes and environmental factors likely plays a role
- Symptoms fall into three groups: inattention (not being able to focus), hyperactivity (being extremely active), and impulsivity (difficulty controlling behavior)
- Diagnoses: **ADHD, Inattentive Presentation** (mainly inattention) and **ADHD, Hyperactive/Impulsive Presentation** (mainly hyperactivity and impulsivity)
- Inattention signs: careless mistakes, trouble keeping attention, not seeming to listen, not finishing tasks, problems organizing, avoiding tasks needing sustained mental effort, losing items, easily distracted and forgetful
- Hyperactivity signs: fidgeting, difficulty staying seated, running or climbing in inappropriate situations, difficulty doing things quietly, talking excessively
- Impulsivity signs: blurting out answers before questions are finished, difficulty awaiting turn, interrupting or intruding on others
- There is no one test for ADHD; clinicians observe, use questionnaires and rating scales, conduct psychological tests, interview relatives and teachers, and rule out other conditions

## ADHD facts (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: ADHD](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/adhd/)
- NAMI describes ADHD as a condition of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that is most commonly diagnosed in young people
- About 8.8% of children aged 4–17 and about 4.4% of adults aged 18–44 have ADHD
- A person with ADHD has trouble controlling these behaviors and shows them far more often, and for longer than 6 months
- NAMI inattention signs: easily distracted, quickly bored, trouble finishing homework, losing things, daydreaming, struggling to follow directions
- NAMI hyperactivity signs: fidgeting and squirming, non-stop talking, touching everything, difficulty with quiet tasks
- NAMI impulsivity signs: impatience, blurting things out, trouble taking turns or waiting, interrupting others
- Causes: genetics (ADHD often runs in families and is one of the conditions most strongly linked to genetics) and environmental factors such as smoking or alcohol use during pregnancy and lead exposure in childhood
- Teachers are often the first to notice ADHD symptoms because they see children learning with peers every day
- There is no single test for ADHD; an evaluation tries to rule out outside causes such as environmental changes, school difficulties or medical problems
- About two-thirds of children with ADHD also have another condition, such as learning disabilities, anxiety, depression, OCD, bipolar disorder, Tourette's syndrome, sleep disorders, bed-wetting or substance use disorders
- **Oppositional defiant disorder:** Refusing to accept directions or authority from adults or others (a condition that often occurs with ADHD)
- **Conduct disorder:** Persistent destructive or violent behaviors (a condition that often occurs with ADHD)
- NAMI lists three kinds of ADHD treatment: medications (stimulants, nonstimulants and antidepressants), behavioral therapy, and self-management, education and support through school or work
- **Stimulants:** The most prescribed and usually first-choice ADHD medications; they work quickly but may reduce appetite, affect sleep and slightly shorten a child's height if taken for many years
  - Examples: methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta), the methylphenidate skin patch (Daytrana), dextroamphetamine (Dexedrine), amphetamine (Adderall) and lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse)
  - Because they act fast, stimulants can be misused to boost school or sports performance; having them without a prescription is illegal
- **Nonstimulants:** ADHD medications that act more indirectly and take longer to work, such as atomoxetine (Strattera), guanfacine (Intuniv) and clonidine (Kapvay)
- Antidepressants such as bupropion (Wellbutrin) may be suggested, especially when a person has both ADHD and depression
- Common ADHD medication side effects are appetite changes, sleep problems, anxiety and irritability; they often lessen over time or with a dosage change
- Complementary approaches for ADHD: elimination diets (removing sugar and artificial additives), omega-3 supplements, interactive metronome training, chiropractic care and neurofeedback
- **Neurofeedback (EEG biofeedback):** Training that teaches people with ADHD to raise activity in the frontal areas of the brain, where arousal tends to be low
- ADHD is not caused by parenting style, and people with ADHD do not lack intelligence or discipline
- Girls with ADHD are sometimes overlooked because they usually show less disruptive behavior than boys
- Adult workplace accommodations include regular meetings with a supervisor, checklists, a distraction-free workspace, breaking big assignments into smaller tasks, structured breaks and a job coach (the Job Accommodation Network has more information)
- **ADHD coaching:** A support that helps people with ADHD with scheduling, goal setting, organizing, prioritizing and staying accountable
- Parent tips: keep a positive attitude, create structure, post clear rules, encourage exercise and sleep, and build social skills; rewards should be immediate activities with a parent, and consequences should target the behavior, not the child
- School accommodations include modified homework and tests, sitting near the teacher and away from doors and windows, a note-taking partner and a behavior plan; more intensive supports include school-based counseling and resource room services
