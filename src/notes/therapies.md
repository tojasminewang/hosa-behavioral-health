## Overview of treatment {pp. 598–601}
- Many people with disorders still do not seek help, possibly because of stigma or not knowing effective help is available; people with more severe depression are more likely to get treatment
- About 70% of contemporary patients with schizophrenia get some relief from treatment; about 30% do not seem to respond
- From 1990 to 2015, treatment (mostly antidepressants) increased in Australia, Canada, the UK, and the U.S., yet rates of MDD and anxiety did not decrease, pointing to a need for quality care and prevention
- **Psychotherapy:** A treatment designed to improve symptoms of psychological disorder through conversation between the therapist and the patient or client; often combined with biological treatments such as medication
- Biological approaches grew from medical knowledge; untreated syphilis produces hallucinations, delusions, and mood changes, showing abnormal behavior can have a biological cause
- **Humanistic therapy:** A therapy approach patterned after the theories proposed by humanistic psychologists
- **Counseling:** A treatment originally proposed by humanistic therapists (Carl Rogers) that focuses on personal strengths and development; now refers to treatment for adjustment problems rather than severe disorders
- **Evidence-based practice (EBP):** Combines the best research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values and expectations to give the best outcome; began in medicine and spread to psychology
- EBP's five steps: ask an answerable question, search the current scientific literature, critically evaluate it, integrate it with the patient's values and circumstances, and evaluate outcomes and adjust
- Clinical assessment usually uses interviews, observations, and standardized tests

## Formats of therapy {pp. 605–608}
- Traditional psychoanalysis meets four to five times per week for years; CBT might meet once a week for about six weeks; many insurance plans limit therapy to 10 sessions per year
- **Brief therapy:** Avoids searching for the historical basis of problems and focuses on "solution-building rather than problem-solving"; three to five sessions (never more than eight)
- **Group therapy:** Psychotherapy conducted with a group (usually 6 to 12 people) rather than individually; benefits include lower cost, hope from seeing others' progress, and reduced isolation
- People with severe psychotic, suicidal, or antisocial tendencies are usually excluded from groups until they progress
- Leaderless self-help groups such as Weight Watchers and Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) operate outside licensure and supervision
- **Family therapy:** A type of therapy in which family members participate individually and in combination with other family members; often used when a child or teen has problems
- **Couples therapy:** A type of family therapy that focuses on intimate relationships
- Online therapy offers privacy and access from home; online CBT has been shown to be as effective as face-to-face CBT (therapists using CBT liked online therapy during COVID-19 more than psychoanalysts did)
- Culturally competent therapy: interventions consistent with the client's values, communicating in culturally sensitive ways (such as the client's native language), empathy, and not equating differences with deficits
- Managed care has shifted decisions about treatment length and type toward insurance companies; the Psychological Clinical Science Accreditation System (PCSAS) aims to increase the scientific basis of clinical training

## Psychoanalysis (historical approach) {pp. 609–610}
- **Psychoanalysis:** Freud's "talk therapy"; a psychotherapist who uses it is a psychoanalyst; the analyst takes a directive role and the person is called a patient
- **Insight therapy:** A therapy that improves symptoms by building people's understanding of their situation (psychoanalysis is a classic example)
- **Free association:** The psychoanalytic technique of encouraging a patient to say whatever comes to mind, without censoring the content
- **Dream analysis:** Freud saw dreams as reflections of unconscious wishes; "Freudian slips" were thought to reveal unconscious wishes
- **Transference:** The patient uses an earlier relationship (usually with a parent or authority figure) as a model for the relationship with the therapist
- **Resistance:** The patient's reluctance (changing the subject, disagreeing, missing appointments) as they get closer to uncomfortable insight
- Freud's case of Little Hans (fear of horses) was explained by the Oedipus complex; a behaviorist would explain it as a classically conditioned fear
- Psychoanalysis taught clinicians the importance of life histories and attentive listening

## Humanistic therapy {pp. 610–611}
- Humanistic psychologists believed humans are inherently good with natural drives to grow; the therapist is nondirective and the person is called a "client"
- **Person-centered therapy:** Carl Rogers's humanistic approach to help clients achieve congruence, an alignment of the real and ideal selves
- Techniques: empathy (seeing the world through the client's eyes), unconditional positive regard (nonjudgmental acceptance), reflection (restating the client's feelings), and clarification
- Rogers believed unhappiness comes from conditional regard, learning you have value only when pleasing authority figures
- Humanistic approaches are better suited to adjustment problems than to serious disorders like schizophrenia

## Behavior and cognitive therapies {pp. 612–614}
- **Behavior therapy:** Applications of classical and operant conditioning principles to treat symptoms of psychological disorders and adjustment problems
- Behavior modification (now applied behavior analysis, ABA) gained respect after success with previously untreatable cases such as ASD; the first book with "behavior modification" in its title was published in 1965 (Ullman and Krasner)
- Behavioral techniques include desensitization, counterconditioning, peer modeling, token economies, and changing reinforcers and high-risk situations (e.g., quitting smoking)
- Virtual reality exposure (e.g., SpiderWorld) reduced fear of real spiders in three sessions
- **Cognitive therapy (Aaron Beck, beginning in the 1940s):** People run into trouble when they adopt irrational, self-defeating ways of thinking
- **Cognitive restructuring:** A technique in which new, rational beliefs replace earlier, irrational beliefs held by the client
- **Rational emotive behavioral therapy (REBT):** Albert Ellis's version of cognitive therapy (beginning in the 1950s); more confrontational, openly criticizing irrational thinking
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):** A combination of cognitive restructuring with behavioral treatments that has been shown to reduce symptoms of many disorders; typically fewer than 16 sessions, so it is a favorite of insurance providers; especially successful for MDD
- **Biopsychosocial approach:** An integrated approach combining treatments that address the biological, personal, and social underpinnings of disorders; the therapist uses what works for each client

## Biological therapies {pp. 614–617}
- The major biological treatments are medication, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), psychosurgery, brain stimulation, and neurofeedback
- Medication is the most commonly used medical therapy, yet 75% of patients (especially younger patients and women) prefer psychological treatment
- **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT):** A biological treatment in which seizures are induced in an anesthetized patient; used mainly for mood disorders that have not responded to medication or other treatments
  - Typically 6 to 12 treatments, three per week; over one million patients worldwide each year
  - Ladislas Meduna recommended it for schizophrenia in the 1930s (poor results), but improved mood led to its use for depression and bipolar disorder
  - Considered as safe as minor surgery under general anesthesia, but about one-third of patients have persistent memory loss (especially autobiographical memory)
- **Psychosurgery:** The attempt to improve symptoms of psychological disorders by operating on the brain; ancient skull drilling (trepanation) may be the first example
- The frontal lobotomy was promoted by Egas Moniz (1949 Nobel Prize) and popularized in the U.S. by Walter Freeman (more than 3,400 lobotomies, the "lobotomobile"); 40,000 to 50,000 patients received it between the 1930s and 1950s, and it was performed into the 1960s
- **Deep brain stimulation:** Electrical stimulation through surgically implanted electrodes used to treat some anxiety and mood disorders; stimulating the nucleus accumbens improved mood in 50% of patients with severe, treatment-resistant depression
- **Repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS):** Magnetic pulses from a handheld device on the scalp, without surgery; promising for MDD, mixed for schizophrenia, negative results for OCD
- **Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS):** Surface electrodes; improvement in auditory hallucinations, addictions, and MDD; low cost and usable at home
- **Neurofeedback:** A type of biofeedback that trains people to keep brain activity (EEG or fMRI) within a range; rigorous studies have not strongly supported it for ADHD, ASD, OCD, GAD, or MDD
- **Biofeedback:** Gaining conscious control over normally automatic functions (e.g., overcoming "white coat hypertension," reducing migraines)

## Medications at a glance {pp. 618–629}
| Medication type | Examples | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulants | Methylphenidate (Ritalin), dextroamphetamine (Dexedrine), amphetamine salts (Adderall) | ADHD (boost dopamine and norepinephrine) |
| First-generation ("typical") antipsychotics | Phenothiazines such as chlorpromazine (Thorazine) | Schizophrenia (block dopamine receptors) |
| Second-generation ("atypical") antipsychotics | Clozapine (Clozaril) | Schizophrenia, including negative symptoms (affect dopamine and serotonin) |
| Mood stabilizer | Lithium carbonate | Bipolar disorder (also uniquely prevents suicidal behavior) |
| SSRIs (antidepressants) | Prozac, Zoloft | MDD, anxiety disorders, OCD, bulimia |
| Benzodiazepines (antianxiety) | Diazepam (Valium), chlordiazepoxide (Librium), alprazolam (Xanax) | Anxiety, panic disorder, GAD (enhance GABA) |
| Barbiturates | — | Seizures; no longer desirable for anxiety (addictive, used in suicides) |

- **Antidepressant medication:** A medication designed to alleviate symptoms of depression but often prescribed for other conditions; the third most common prescription drug type in the U.S. (about 10% of Americans over 12 use them)
- **Tardive dyskinesia:** A movement syndrome ("slow difficulty moving") caused by antipsychotic medications, with tremors and involuntary movements of the face and tongue that often continue after the medication stops
- Mixing alcohol, barbiturates, and benzodiazepines (which all act at the GABA receptor) is frequently fatal
- Gottman marriage therapy ("Sound Relationship House"): couples take a break when heart rate gets too high during conflict; a 10-hour seminar reversed dissatisfaction in 77% of couples expecting a baby
- Progressive muscle relaxation: tense each muscle group for 5 seconds, then relax for 20 seconds, moving from head to feet

## Treatment approaches and medications (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Treatments & Approaches](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/) · [Mental Health Medications](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/mental-health-medications/)
- Treatment varies by diagnosis and by person; there is no "one size fits all" treatment
- Outcomes improve when people help design their own treatment plan, including defining recovery goals, choosing services and evaluating progress
- NAMI's tools for recovery: medication, counseling (therapy), social support and education
- Psychiatric medications influence the brain chemicals that regulate emotions and thought patterns, and they usually work better combined with psychotherapy (for example, easing low energy so a person can engage in talk therapy)
- Most psychiatric medications work slowly, sometimes taking several weeks or months; side effects often go away within a few weeks
- People who have trouble remembering daily medication may do better with a shot at the doctor's office once or twice a month
- Providers usually start at a low dose and increase it slowly; when stopping, taper off with a doctor, because stopping suddenly can cause uncomfortable effects
- Some psychiatric medications are short-term and others long-term or lifelong; most people find medication helps them take charge of their lives rather than changing their personality

## Types of psychotherapy (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Psychotherapy](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/psychotherapy/)
- **Psychotherapy (talk therapy):** Speaking with a trained therapist in a safe, confidential environment to explore feelings and behaviors and gain coping skills; it can be individual, family, couples or group
- Best practice for many mental health conditions combines medication and therapy
- CBT explores how thoughts, feelings and behaviors relate; its core principles are identifying negative or false beliefs and testing or restructuring them, often with homework such as a thought journal
  - Example: replacing "I can't do anything right" with "I can do this most of the time, based on my prior experiences"
  - CBT works for depression, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, eating disorders and schizophrenia, and people who undergo it show changes in brain activity
- DBT was originally developed for chronically suicidal people with BPD; it is based on CBT but emphasizes validation (accepting uncomfortable thoughts, feelings and behaviors instead of struggling with them) and a balance between acceptance and change
- EMDR, used to treat PTSD, has a person make back-and-forth eye movements for 20–30 seconds (or use tapping or tones) while recalling a traumatic event, a protocol called dual stimulation; it is debated whether the movement or the exposure is what helps
- **Exposure therapy (NAMI):** A type of CBT most often used for OCD, PTSD and phobias, in which a person confronts their triggers in a controlled environment
  - **Flooding:** Presenting a large amount of the triggering stimulus all at once
  - **Desensitization:** Presenting small amounts of the trigger first and escalating over time
- Interpersonal therapy improves interpersonal skills by recognizing negative patterns such as social isolation or aggression; it is most often used for depression
- **Mentalizing:** The intuitive process of understanding one's own inner feelings and thoughts and guessing at other people's; MBT exercises this skill and is less structured and usually longer-term than CBT
- Psychodynamic therapy uses open-ended questions and free association to uncover unconscious patterns rooted in past experiences; it is often used for depression, anxiety disorders and BPD
- Therapy pets (most often dogs) can reduce anxiety, depression, fatigue and pain; time with pets lowers anxiety more than other recreational activities
- **Service animals:** Not therapy pets; they are working animals with higher training that do specific tasks for one person long-term, showing promise for PTSD and panic disorder

## Psychosocial treatments (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Psychosocial Treatments](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/psychosocial-treatments/)
- **Psychosocial treatments:** Psychotherapy plus social and vocational training that give support, education and guidance to people with mental illness and their families; they can lead to fewer hospitalizations
- Many of NAMI's education programs are examples of psychoeducation, and family psychoeducation can ease tension at home
- Self-help and support groups address feelings of isolation and help people gain insight into their condition
- **Psychosocial rehabilitation:** Helps people develop the social, emotional and intellectual skills to live happily with the least professional help possible, by learning coping skills and developing resources that reduce future stress
- ACT is a team-based model giving multidisciplinary, flexible treatment and support 24/7, mostly for people who have left inpatient care
- **Vocational rehabilitation (VR):** State programs that provide career counseling and job search help for people with disabilities, including mental illness
- **Clubhouses:** Community-based centers where members with mental illness gain skills, find jobs and housing, pursue education and work side by side with staff
- **Case manager:** A professional (certified in case management or with a social work degree) who coordinates medical, housing, employment and financial assistance services and helps with forms and transportation

## Complementary health approaches (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Complementary Health Approaches](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/complementary-health-approaches/)
- The FDA does not review or approve most complementary and alternative treatments
- **NCCIH (National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health):** The main government agency studying non-traditional treatments
- NCCIH's three areas: complementary (added to standard treatment), alternative (used instead of established treatment) and integrative (combining traditional and non-traditional)
- Omega-3 fatty acids (found in fish) may lower the risk that young people with a first episode of psychosis develop a more chronic form of schizophrenia
- **Folate (folic acid, vitamin B9):** A vitamin the body cannot make; the only FDA-approved form for depression and schizophrenia is L-methylfolate (Deplin), as an add-on rather than a primary treatment
- Medical foods (such as gluten-free foods for celiac disease) are not monitored by the FDA as closely as prescription medicines
- Mind and body treatments (yoga, exercise, meditation, tai chi) can improve mood and anxiety and reduce medication side effects such as weight gain and fatigue
- **Equine therapy (equine-assisted psychotherapy):** Animal-assisted therapy in which people groom, care for and ride horses, which are highly sensitive to others' emotions
- Even vitamins can interact with medication, and people on some psychiatric medicines need to stay cool and drink water to avoid heat stroke

## Brain stimulation therapies (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: ECT, TMS and Other Brain Stimulation Therapies](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/ect-tms-and-other-brain-stimulation-therapies/)
- Brain stimulation therapies stimulate the brain directly with electricity, magnets or implants when medication and therapy have not relieved symptoms
- **ECT (electroconvulsive therapy):** A treatment done under anesthesia that uses small electric currents to trigger a brief, controlled seizure; it is most often used for major depression or bipolar disorder
  - ECT is effective but underused because of stigma, misinformation, limited availability and side-effect concerns
  - ECT is given in a series, usually a couple of times a week; people wake 5–10 minutes afterward and resume normal activity in about an hour
  - 70–90% of people who receive ECT report improvement in their depression
  - ECT side effects are usually mild: headache, muscle soreness, nausea, brief short-term memory loss, and rarely permanent longer-term memory loss
- **TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation):** A non-invasive treatment that uses magnetic pulses from a coil placed near the forehead to stimulate the brain area that controls mood; there is no anesthesia, the person stays awake, and sessions last 3–30 minutes
  - TMS is used for depression, OCD, PTSD, pain and substance use disorders; 30–64% of patients report improvement in depression
  - The most common TMS side effect is headache; seizures are the most serious risk but are very rare (about 0.03%)
  - Rapidly delivered pulses are called repetitive TMS (rTMS)
- **Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS):** A stopwatch-sized pulse generator placed in the upper left chest sends mild pulses through the vagus nerve to the brainstem; it is used for seizure disorders and treatment-resistant depression (estimated 50–70% response)
- **Deep brain stimulation (DBS):** Electrodes surgically placed in the brain connected to a pulse generator in the chest; it is used for Parkinson's tremors, dystonia, epilepsy and severe OCD, and is being studied for Tourette's and treatment-resistant depression

- HOSA Canada's summary of the main psychological and biological treatments for each disorder (from the textbook):

| Disorder | Psychological treatment | Biological treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Autism spectrum disorder | Applied behavior analysis | Antipsychotic medications for self-injury |
| ADHD | Behavior therapy | Stimulant medications |
| Schizophrenia | Psychosocial rehabilitation and evaluating expressed emotion in the family | Antipsychotic medications |
| Bipolar disorder | Supportive CBT | Lithium carbonate, and ECT for depression |
| Major depressive disorder | CBT and exercise | Antidepressants and ECT |
| Anxiety disorders, OCD, body dysmorphic disorder, PTSD | CBT | Antidepressant or anti-anxiety medications |
| Personality disorders | Learning models for antisocial PD, DBT for borderline PD, CBT for narcissistic PD | No effective biological treatments |

- Before ECT, patients are anesthetized and given a muscle relaxant, then electricity applied through electrodes on the head induces a general seizure
