## Obsessive-compulsive disorder (textbook) {pp. 581–582}
- DSM-5 groups obsessive-compulsive disorder (formerly an anxiety disorder) with body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, and related problems
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD):** Being haunted by distressing, intrusive thoughts (obsessions), the need to engage in repetitive, ritualistic behaviors (compulsions), or both; 2% to 3% of the U.S. population report OCD in their lifetime
- **Obsession:** An intrusive, distressing thought; different from everyday worries about real problems
- Common obsessions: contamination, repeated doubts (wondering whether you hurt someone), ordering, inappropriate impulses, and sexual imagery
- **Compulsion:** Repetitive, ritualistic behavior associated with high anxiety; compulsions are efforts to ward off the anxiety produced by obsessions
- Common compulsions: hand washing (hundreds of times per day), checking (locks, faucets), counting, ordering objects, and requesting or demanding assurances
- DSM-5: engaging in compulsive behavior for more than 1 hour per day is considered abnormal
- Biology: twin studies show a strong genetic vulnerability; abnormal activity in circuits linking the frontal lobe (orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortices) with the basal ganglia and thalamus
- The basal ganglia normally "approve" or stop proposed behaviors; misfiring of these circuits may produce compulsions
- Low serotonin activity is a possible causal factor
- Evolutionary view: OCD may be exaggerated normal grooming and territorial behavior; dogs that lick their paws raw improve with the same medications used for human OCD
- Learning view: compulsions are reinforced because they reduce anxiety (operant conditioning)
- Social view: OCD resembles exaggerated cultural rituals (washing, special colors, stereotyped actions, rigid rules)
- COVID-19 hand-washing messages may worsen OCD symptoms, and stocking up on supplies could feed hoarding disorder
- Both medication and behavioral treatments reduce abnormal basal ganglia activity in OCD

## Body dysmorphic disorder (textbook) {p. 582}
- **Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD):** A disorder characterized by the unrealistic perception of physical flaws; affects as many as 2% of people, slightly more females
- Suicide rates for BDD are higher than for OCD and anorexia nervosa; many people undergo numerous cosmetic surgeries or excessive bodybuilding
- BDD shows significant heredity; people with BDD may actually perceive subtle facial distortions better than normal

## Treating OCD and body dysmorphic disorder (textbook) {pp. 597–598, 629–630}
- OCD is treated with antidepressant medication, CBT, or both; CBT is highly effective
- CBT for OCD trains the person to anticipate the urge to perform a compulsion and engage in a competing behavior instead (e.g., gardening instead of hand washing), then reward themselves
- PET scans show that CBT and medication produce the same decrease in basal ganglia activity
- CBT (cognitive restructuring of perceived flaws) is particularly helpful for body dysmorphic disorder, though many patients prefer cosmetic surgeons to psychotherapy

## OCD (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Anxiety Disorder – OCD](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/anxiety)
- With OCD, a person has repeated, upsetting thoughts (obsessions) and does the same thing over and over to try to make the thoughts go away (compulsions)
- Researchers think brain circuits work differently in people with OCD; it tends to run in families, and symptoms often begin in children or teens
- Left untreated, OCD can take over your life; treatments that combine medicines and therapy are often effective
- Example obsessions: fear of germs or of being hurt, needing order or symmetry, difficulty allowing uncertainty, dreadful thoughts about harming others or losing control
- Example compulsions: repeated hand washing, counting items, checking that the door is locked or stove is off, repetitive cleaning, avoiding situations that prompt obsessions (such as not touching doorknobs)

## OCD (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Obsessive-compulsive Disorder](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/obsessive-compulsive-disorder/)
- NAMI describes OCD as repetitive, unwanted, intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and irrational, excessive urges to do certain actions (compulsions); people often know these make no sense but cannot stop them
- OCD symptoms typically begin in childhood, the teen years or young adulthood, and males often develop them younger than females; 1.2% of U.S. adults experience OCD each year
- In OCD, symptoms generally last more than an hour each day and interfere with daily life
- **Obsessions (NAMI):** Intrusive, irrational thoughts or impulses that keep coming back, such as thoughts of harming someone, doubts about locking a door or turning off the stove, or fears of shouting inappropriate things
- **Compulsions (NAMI):** Repetitive acts that temporarily relieve the stress of an obsession, such as hand washing from a fear of germs, recounting money, checking locks, or "mental checking"
- Causes of OCD: several brain areas may not respond normally to serotonin, and genetics are very important; if you, a parent or a sibling has OCD, there is about a 25% chance another immediate family member has it
- A physical exam with blood tests checks that symptoms are not caused by drugs, medications, another mental illness or a medical condition; sudden symptoms in children or older people need a thorough medical evaluation
- An OCD diagnosis requires obsessions, compulsions or both that are upsetting, interfere with life, and typically last at least an hour a day
- OCD cannot be cured, but it can be treated effectively; combining medication and psychotherapy is usually best
- SSRIs are the medication most helpful for reducing obsessions and compulsions
- Antidepressants for OCD often take longer to work than for depression and may need larger doses for a longer time
- **Exposure and response prevention (OCD):** Therapy in which a person faces the cause of their anxiety without performing the compulsion, such as touching a doorknob and waiting longer and longer before washing, until the compulsion fades
- CBT for OCD helps a person recognize negative thoughts and gradually lessen their intensity until they are harmless; therapy is believed to retrain the brain to recognize "false threats"
- Aerobic exercise is a key complementary approach for OCD because it lowers a person's baseline level of anxiety
- **Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD):** An obsession with perceived flaws in one's appearance, often for many hours a day; it can lead to injury from skin picking, excessive exercise or unnecessary surgery
- **Hoarding disorder:** A drive to collect large amounts of valueless items plus extreme distress at throwing anything away; many people with it do not see the harm, so they may resist diagnosis or treatment
- **Trichotillomania:** A compulsive urge to pull out (and possibly eat) one's own hair, including eyelashes and eyebrows; swallowed hair can form life-threatening hairballs
- **Excoriation disorder:** A compulsive urge to scratch or pick at the skin
- The International OCD Foundation is a resource on the obsessive-compulsive spectrum, including hoarding, BDD and trichotillomania
