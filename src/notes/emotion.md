## Emotion, mood, and motivation {pp. 231–233}
- **Emotion:** A combination of arousal, physical sensations, and subjective feelings that occurs spontaneously in response to environmental stimuli; a brief state that prepares an individual for action
- We communicate emotions through facial expression, body language, gestures, and tone of voice
- Emotions can be viewed as discrete states (like primary colors) or as points on a continuum; Russell (1980) placed emotions on dimensions of pleasant/unpleasant and high/low arousal
- **Mood:** A more general and longer-lasting state than an emotion; you can be in a good mood while feeling several specific emotions
- Depression criteria specify depressed mood for at least half a day every day for 2 weeks, longer than any single emotion would last
- **Motivation:** A process that arouses, maintains, and guides behavior toward a goal (e.g., thirst leads you to seek water)
- Motivation arouses and directs behavior more directly and precisely than emotion: thirst leads to one thing (drinking), while sadness can lead to many different behaviors
- Silver medalists often look less happy than bronze medalists because silver medalists compare themselves to gold medalists, while bronze medalists compare themselves to those who got no medal

## Classic theories of emotion {pp. 233–238}
| Theory | Sequence | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| James–Lange theory | Stimulus → specific physical response → subjective feeling | Physical sensations lead to subjective feelings: "We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble" |
| Cannon–Bard theory | Stimulus → physical sensations and subjective feeling at the same time | Physical sensations and subjective feelings occur simultaneously and independently |
| Schachter–Singer two-factor theory | Stimulus → general arousal → assessment of surroundings → subjective feeling | General arousal leads to a cognitive appraisal, which identifies the emotion |
| Somatovisceral afference model of emotion (SAME) | Physical response (specific to general) → more or less cognitive processing → feeling | Specific physical responses (fear) are recognized quickly; general arousal (pride) needs more appraisal |
| Appraisal theory | Detection and assessment of a stimulus → feelings and physical responses | Emotions begin with the detection and assessment of stimuli relevant to well-being |

- **James–Lange theory:** A theory of emotion proposing that physical sensations lead to subjective feelings; developed independently by William James and Danish physiologist Carl Lange
- **Catharsis:** A theory viewing emotion as a reservoir that fills up and spills over; it predicts that expressing an emotion will reduce arousal (contradicts the James–Lange theory)
- "Psyching up" (like the Maori haka performed by the New Zealand All Blacks rugby team) challenges catharsis and supports James–Lange: changing physical sensations changes feelings
- Making facial movements of an expression (without knowing it) led people to report feeling that emotion
- Power posing: claims that it changed hormones and risk-taking failed to replicate, but it does make people report feeling more powerful
- **Capilano Canyon bridge study (Dutton & Aron, 1974):** Men interviewed by an attractive woman on a frightening suspension bridge were about four times as likely to telephone her than men on a solid bridge, suggesting people misread fear arousal as sexual attraction (flaw: men were not randomly assigned to bridges)
- **Cannon–Bard theory:** Walter Cannon (modified by Philip Bard) proposed that physical sensations and subjective feelings occur simultaneously and independently
- **Schachter–Singer two-factor theory:** Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962) proposed that general arousal leads to a cognitive assessment of the situation, which then identifies the emotion
  - Their experiment: people injected with epinephrine (told it was a vitamin) felt happy near a silly actor and negative near an angry actor; people told the drug caused arousal attributed their feelings to the drug
  - A later attempt to replicate the study did not succeed
- Arousal from one source can transfer to and intensify another emotion (people who just exercised become angry or sexually aroused more easily)
- Botox study: people treated with Botox (less facial movement) were worse at identifying others' emotions than people given dermal filler, supporting facial feedback (James–Lange)
- **Somatovisceral afference model of emotion (SAME):** A model in which a range of physical sensations from precise to general requires varying degrees of cognitive processing before subjective feelings; a middle ground between James–Lange and Schachter–Singer
- **Affective neuroscience:** The neuroscience of emotion; it supports sequential models in which feelings emerge from our physical states
- **Appraisal:** The detection and assessment of stimuli with relevance for well-being; appraisal theory explains why people react differently to the same event (e.g., a wrecked car vs. a surviving spouse)

## Why we have emotions {pp. 239–241}
- Charles Darwin (1872) found that all primates form facial expressions with the same muscles, concluding that emotional expression evolved
- **Anthropomorphism:** Attributing human qualities to other animals
- Emotions allow more flexible responses than reflexes without the cost of conscious planning; "emotion" comes from the Latin word meaning "to move"
- **Yerkes–Dodson law:** A description of the relationships among task complexity, arousal, and performance; for simple tasks greater arousal improves performance, but for complex tasks (like a difficult exam) moderate arousal is best and high arousal hurts ("choking")
- Each emotion has a valence (positive or negative) that moves us toward approach or avoidance (disgust tells us to avoid rotting food)
- Emotions communicate nonverbally (facial expression, body language); infants communicate needs before speaking
- Emotions let us enjoy and share the arts; language and music use the same brain pathways
- Antonio Damasio's patient Elliot (frontal lobe surgery) kept his intelligence but could not set priorities; emotions provide a bridge to past experiences that guides decisions

## The biology of emotion {pp. 241–245}
- The sympathetic nervous system produces the fight-or-flight arousal of emotion; the hypothalamus directly controls the autonomic nervous system
- Autonomic measures cannot reliably predict specific emotions, but negative emotions produce stronger autonomic responses than positive emotions
- Stimulating the hypothalamus during deep brain stimulation produced the physical and subjective feelings of a panic attack
- **Amygdala:** Identifies emotional stimuli, both positive and negative, and initiates responses through its connections with the hypothalamus
- Klüver and Bucy (1939) removed the temporal lobes (including the amygdalae) of rhesus monkeys, which became tamer and less fearful
- Rats with damaged amygdalae fail to learn to fear a tone that predicts shock; Patient S.M. had difficulty identifying fear and made fewer eye fixations
- Abnormal development of the amygdala is one of the most consistent biological correlates of autism spectrum disorder
- **Insula:** Regions of cortex at the junction of the frontal and temporal lobes; important for subjective feelings; the back of the insula handles localized body sensations, while the front handles global feelings, including a strong reaction to disgust
- **Cingulate cortex:** A gateway between the amygdala and the frontal cortex; processes the emotional quality of physical pain; the anterior cingulate cortex is highly active during worry and catastrophizing
- **Basal ganglia:** Coordinate movements in response to emotional stimuli; along with the insula they participate in disgust (people with Huntington's disease have trouble recognizing disgust)
- The nucleus accumbens (part of the basal ganglia) is associated with pleasure, reward, and addiction
- Frontal lobe damage reduces fear and anxiety and removes "gut feelings," leading to impulsive, risky behavior
- There are no "happy centers" or "sad centers"; different emotions produce different patterns of cortical activity
- The right hemisphere plays a stronger role in emotion, so facial expressions are not symmetrical

## Expressing emotion {pp. 245–248}
- Voluntary expressions (smiling for a camera) come from the motor cortex; spontaneous expressions (laughing at a joke) come from subcortical areas including the basal ganglia
- A tumor in the right motor cortex makes voluntary smiles crooked but spontaneous smiles natural; people with Parkinson's disease show the opposite pattern
- Some emotional expressions appear universal across cultures: anger, sadness, happiness, fear, disgust, surprise, contempt, and embarrassment
- Evidence for innate expression: social smiles appear around 3 months even in infants born blind; children in diverse cultures show separation distress at the same ages; blind and sighted Olympic and Paralympic athletes show the same win/lose expressions
- Words for emotions are similar across 60 languages (an exception is the Japanese ijirashi, the feeling of watching someone overcome an obstacle)
- Jerome Kagan: highly responsive children often become cautious, anxious adults, while unresponsive children may become bold risk takers
- **Emotion regulation strategies:** Suppression (inhibiting expression) and cognitive reappraisal (changing the meaning of an event); people who use reappraisal more experience less depression and higher life satisfaction
- **Display rule:** A cultural norm that specifies when, where, and how a person should express an emotion; Japanese students showed less emotion in front of strangers than alone, while Americans did not
- Cultures with a long history of immigration (like the U.S.) are more emotionally expressive and interpret smiling as bonding rather than dominance

## Interpreting emotion {pp. 248–251}
- People can judge whether a teacher likes a student from only 10 seconds of video; detecting fake expressions is good but far from perfect
- **Emotional intelligence:** Our abilities to perceive, use, understand, and manage emotions; predicts success in work and personal relationships
- Japanese observers assumed low-intensity expressions hid stronger feelings; Americans assumed high-intensity expressions were exaggerated
- People with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder have trouble distinguishing facial expressions; people with autism spectrum disorder and antisocial personality disorder have specific difficulty recognizing fear
- Jurors detect lies only slightly better than chance; polygraphs are rejected in court for poor accuracy
- Signs of lying: inappropriate smiling, stiff upper body, fewer nods and gestures, swinging feet, "um" and "uh," and scripted stories lacking detail (truth tellers give 20 to 30% more detail); telling a story backward exposes liars
- John Gottman: happy couples make five times more positive than negative comments (a 5:1 ratio)
