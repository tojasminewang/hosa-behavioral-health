## Health psychology and the biopsychosocial model {pp. 660–662}
- **Health psychology:** A branch of psychology that investigates the relationships between psychological variables and health
- Medicine historically defined health as a lack of disease; today health is more often defined as a positive state of well-being
- **Biopsychosocial model:** A model that sees health as the result of biological (genetics, infection, injury), psychological (lifestyle, stress, health beliefs), and social factors (culture, family, social support)
- **World Health Organization (1948) definition of health:** "A state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity"
- In 1900 the leading U.S. causes of death were pneumonia, influenza, and tuberculosis (infectious, short duration); by 2005 they were heart disease, cancer, lung disease, and stroke (noninfectious, long term)
- Today's leading causes of death are strongly linked to behavior: obesity, smoking, lack of exercise, and alcohol use
- Relationships between lifestyle and health come from large populations, not individuals, so we should not "blame the victims" of poor health
- Following four simple habits (never smoking, exercising 30 minutes a day, a non-obese BMI, and a healthy diet with fruits and vegetables) cut the risk of chronic disease by 78%, but only 9.1% of adults followed all four

## Tobacco {pp. 663–665}
- Cigarette smoking is the leading preventable cause of death in the United States, responsible for one in five deaths each year
- Over 8 million people worldwide die from tobacco each year; smokers die 13–14 years earlier than nonsmokers on average
- Nicotine is a well-established gateway drug that can produce epigenetic changes increasing the likelihood of cocaine addiction
- Secondhand smoke causes 7,300 lung cancer deaths, 34,000 heart disease deaths, and 8,000 stroke deaths among U.S. nonsmokers each year, plus more SIDS, childhood asthma, and ear infections
- U.S. adult smoking dropped from 21% in 2005 to 13.7% in 2018; global adult smoking fell from 22.5% to 19.2% (2007–2017)
- Smoking is highest among American Indians or Alaska Natives (22.6%) and lowest among Asians (7.1%); LGB individuals smoke more (20.6%) than heterosexuals (13.5%); smoking drops with education (36% with a GED vs. 3.7% with a graduate degree); 26.1% of adults below the poverty line smoke
- About 3.2% of U.S. adults used e-cigarettes in 2020; e-cigarette use among middle (4.7%) and high school students (19.6%) is dropping; serious lung injuries led to sales restrictions
- Most tobacco users begin in childhood or early adolescence; about half of those who experiment continue (addiction, peer pressure, overestimating how many others use tobacco, and smoking friends and parents)
- 40.6% of adults with any mental disorder smoke; tobacco use among people with schizophrenia and severe mental illness is as high as 88%; people with mental disorders smoke 31.6% of all U.S. cigarettes
- In the U.S., ex-smokers outnumber smokers; quitting methods include cold turkey, gradual reduction, nicotine patches and gum, and counseling and support groups
- Chances of quitting drop 67% if a spouse smokes, 25% if a sibling smokes, 36% if a friend smokes, and 34% if a coworker smokes; tailored, self-relevant interventions work better
- After 5 smoke-free years, stroke risk equals that of nonsmokers; after 10 years, lung cancer risk is cut in half; quitting before age 30 preserves normal life expectancy

## Nutrition and obesity {pp. 665–666}
- Being either underweight or obese reduces health; obesity raises rates of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, arthritis, and breast and colon cancers
- Returning the U.S. population to normal weight would improve life expectancy about as much as eliminating smoking
- U.S. adult obesity rose from 12% in 1991 to 42% in 2018; obesity is highest in non-Hispanic Black adults (49.6%) and lowest in non-Hispanic Asian adults (17.4%)
- WHO describes a "double burden" in low- and middle-income countries: undernutrition and obesity in the same households
- Healthy food is expensive: $1.50 buys about 1,100 calories of fast food but only 250 calories of fresh apples

## Alcohol {pp. 666–668}
- 86.3% of American adults have used alcohol in their lifetime, 70% in the past year, and 55.3% in the past month
- **Binge drinking:** Five or more alcoholic beverages on the same occasion (about 26% of adults in the past month)
- **Heavy drinking:** Five or more drinks on one occasion on each of five or more days in the past month (7% of adults)
- 6% of adults and 1.6% of adolescents meet criteria for alcohol use disorder
- Alcohol is the third-leading preventable cause of death in the U.S. (after tobacco, and poor diet and inactivity); about 88,000 U.S. deaths per year at a cost of $249 billion; alcohol-impaired driving causes 31% of driving fatalities
- Globally, alcohol contributes to 5.3% of deaths and causes more deaths than tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, and diabetes
- **Alcohol abuse:** Repeated use despite adverse consequences
- **Alcohol dependence:** Alcohol abuse accompanied by tolerance, withdrawal, and a compulsive urge to drink more
- Genes found mainly in Asians are correlated with lower rates of alcohol dependence; Native Americans have high rates despite many having "protective" genes
- Age of first intoxication predicts later drinking problems better than age at first drink
- Socially anxious teens are especially at risk because alcohol reduces anxiety (operant conditioning)
- Withdrawal from very heavy drinking needs medical supervision because it can cause life-threatening seizures
- **Drunkorexia:** Deliberately restricting calories before planned drinking to avoid weight gain

## Exercise and sitting {pp. 668–669}
- Americans spend about 55% of the day sitting; sitting time correlates with risk of death, but 60–75 minutes per day of moderately intense activity seems to offset some risk
- Exercise improves mood (a brisk 30-minute walk helps treat MDD) and cognition, possibly by delivering more oxygen and boosting neurogenesis in the hippocampus
- Regular moderate exercise lowers infection rates; prolonged high-intensity exercise (marathons, triathlons) temporarily lowers immune function for about a day

## Loneliness, culture, and health {pp. 669–671}
- **Loneliness:** A perceived deficit in social connectivity; produces a 26% increase in the odds of early death
- 20% to 60% of older adults report being lonely some of the time, and 5% to 10% feel lonely frequently or always
- Loneliness harms health through poor sleep, HPA axis activity, sympathetic activity, altered immune gene expression, inflammation, reduced impulse control, and depression
- Simple daily interactions, such as chatting with a barista, can alleviate loneliness
- The U.S. ranks 36th in life expectancy despite its wealth; within the U.S., the life expectancy gap between the top and bottom 1% of income is 15 years for men and 10 years for women
- The White–Black life expectancy gap narrowed from 5.5 years (2000) to 3.8 years (2010), but COVID-19 hit non-Hispanic Black Americans disproportionately
- Minorities in the U.S. have less access to mental health services, are less likely to receive needed services, receive poorer quality care, and are underrepresented in research
- People at the lowest socioeconomic levels have two to three times greater rates of psychological disorder
- **Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study:** A CDC-affiliated study of how adverse childhood experiences (abuse, neglect) lead to social, emotional, and cognitive impairment, health-risk behaviors, and later disease, disability, and early death
