## The five perspectives (types) of psychology {pp. 22–27}
- By the second half of the 20th century, psychologists specialized in perspectives; today they call themselves "social psychologists," "developmental psychologists," and so on

| Perspective | What it studies |
|---|---|
| Biological psychology (behavioral neuroscience) | Relationships between mind and behavior and their underlying biological processes: genetics, biochemistry, anatomy, and physiology |
| Evolutionary psychology (a branch of the biological perspective) | How physical structure and behavior have been shaped by their contributions to survival and reproduction (a modern extension of James's functionalism) |
| Cognitive psychology | Information processing, thinking, reasoning, and problem solving |
| Developmental psychology | The normal changes in behavior that occur across the life span |
| Social and personality psychology | Effects of the social environment, social and cultural diversity, and individual differences on behavior |
| Clinical psychology | Explaining, defining, and treating psychological disorders; now also promoting well-being (positive psychology) |

- **Biological psychology:** The psychological perspective that focuses on the relationships between mind and behavior and their underlying biological processes; also known as behavioral neuroscience
- **Evolutionary psychology:** A perspective that investigates how physical structure and behavior have been shaped by their contributions to survival and reproduction
- **Cognitive psychology:** A perspective that investigates information processing, thinking, reasoning, and problem solving
- **Developmental psychology:** Explores the normal changes in behavior that occur across the life span (e.g., how memory works at different ages)
- **Social psychology:** A perspective that examines the effects of the social environment on the behavior of individuals
- **Personality:** An individual's characteristic way of thinking, feeling, and behaving
- **Cultural diversity:** Variations in the practices, values, and goals shared by groups of people; culture and diversity are a "cross-cutting theme" across all perspectives
- **Clinical psychology:** A perspective that seeks to explain, define, and treat abnormal behaviors
- The American Psychological Association (APA) recognizes 54 divisions (e.g., Division 7 Developmental Psychology; Division 8 Society for Personality and Social Psychology; Division 35 Society for the Psychology of Women)
- Newer combined fields: cognitive neuroscience, social neuroscience, behavioral neuroscience
- Combining perspectives can solve puzzles single perspectives cannot: maltreated children with a low-activity version of the MAOA gene became antisocial, while those with the high-activity version did not (gene–environment interaction)

## Becoming a psychologist {pp. 28–30}
- In 2016–2017 nearly 117,000 U.S. students earned bachelor's degrees in psychology (about 6% of all bachelor's degrees), the fourth most common major
- Calling oneself a psychologist is restricted to holders of graduate (usually doctorate) degrees
- Bachelor's graduates work in research facilities, rehabilitation centers for drug abuse or brain damage, group homes, camps for troubled youth, and people-oriented jobs (management, sales, human resources, teaching, probation, journalism); about one-third get jobs closely related to psychology
- More than half of people with graduate psychology degrees work in health care, counseling, financial services, or legal services
- A master's degree (1 to 2 years beyond the bachelor's) allows teaching at community colleges and licensing as a therapist in most states
- **School psychologists** (often master's level) work in elementary, middle, and high schools on academic and career counseling and removing barriers to student success
- Doctoral degrees take 2 to 5 years beyond the master's; about 34% of new doctoral psychologists teach and/or research in colleges, and about 45% work as therapists
- Clinical or counseling tracks add extensive internships and supervised training before government-regulated licensure (at least 1 extra year)
- **Forensic psychologists** try to understand the criminal mind and develop treatments for criminal behavior; **sports psychologists** help athletes perform
- Only about 21 to 24% of the world's psychologists live in the U.S. today, down from 80% in the 1980s

## Who provides therapy? {pp. 602–605}
- Licensing of mental health professionals is administered by state governments in the U.S. (provincial governments in Canada); the term "therapist" is not regulated consistently
- **Psychotherapist:** A licensed professional who provides psychotherapy
- **Psychiatrist:** A medical doctor (MD) who specializes in psychiatry (three to four additional years) and can use medical procedures, such as prescribing medication, to treat psychological disorders; tends to look for biological causes
- **Clinical psychologist:** Earns a PhD (with an additional year of supervised internship) or a PsyD; treats psychological disorders and problems with adjustment
- **PsyD (Doctor of Psychology):** Developed in the 1970s, modeled on dental and medical schools, to train practitioners with less research training than the PhD; now about as many clinical PsyDs as PhDs are awarded each year
- 65% of PhD faculty teach scientifically supported CBT techniques compared with about 30% of PsyD faculty
- Prescription privileges for specially trained psychologists: New Mexico (2002), Louisiana (2004), Illinois (2014), Iowa (2016), and Idaho (2017)
- **Counselors:** Hold master's or doctoral degrees (psychology, education, social work); specialize in adjustment problems such as marriage and family, substance abuse, vocational, grief, rehabilitation, and prison counseling, not disorders like schizophrenia
- Religious leaders may counsel without further licensure in most states; the Master of Divinity (MDiv) includes counseling courses
- **Hypnotherapists:** Among the least regulated practitioners (only nine states regulate hypnotism); psychotherapist Steve Eichel got his cat, Zoe D. Katze, certified to show weak accreditation
- **Life coaches:** Require no license; coaching is "partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential"; appealing to men and less stigmatized, but unqualified coaches could harm people with disorders
- Other professionals who provide counseling and psychotherapy include general medical practitioners, social workers, and nurses
- Consumers should investigate credentials carefully (e.g., "Dr. John Gray" earned his PhD from an unaccredited "diploma mill")
- The American Psychological Association accredits PhD and PsyD clinical programs; the Psychological Clinical Science Accreditation System (PCSAS) has accredited 43 programs since 2007

## The behavioral health workforce (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Behavioral Health Workforce](https://www.samhsa.gov/about/careers/behavioral-health-workforce)
- SAMHSA works with federal and other partners to increase the supply of trained and culturally aware professionals to address the nation's behavioral health needs
- Workforce settings: prevention programs, community-based programs, inpatient treatment programs, primary care, emergency rooms, criminal justice systems, schools, and higher education institutions
- Behavioral health careers include psychiatrists, psychologists, social workers, advanced practice psychiatric nurses, marriage and family therapists, certified prevention specialists, addiction counselors, mental health/professional counselors, psychiatric rehabilitation specialists, psychiatric aides and technicians, paraprofessionals (case managers, homeless outreach specialists, parent aides), peer support specialists, and recovery coaches
- The field is growing because of increased insurance coverage for mental health and substance use services and the rising rate of military veterans seeking behavioral health services, yet serious workforce shortages exist across the United States
- **Behavioral Health Career Navigator:** A SAMHSA tool (launched November 2024) that shows state-specific education, credentialing, licensing, and renewal requirements for behavioral health careers, plus credential reciprocity for professionals who relocate
- Developing the workforce trained to prevent and treat substance use disorders is one of SAMHSA's highest priorities; CSAP aims to increase the number of qualified prevention professionals
- **BHbusiness Plus Initiative:** SAMHSA initiative that helps behavioral health organizations get ready to compete using new practices and technologies
- **License vs. certification:** A license is issued by a state governmental agency and grants legal authority to practice; a certification is awarded by a professional organization or other nongovernmental body and is not typically a legal provision to practice independently
- Strengthening the Behavioral Health Workforce is one of SAMHSA's five priority areas

## Behavioral health career descriptions (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Behavioral Health Career Descriptions](https://www.samhsa.gov/about/careers/behavioral-health-workforce/careers-by-state/descriptions)

| Career | What they do | Typical education |
|---|---|---|
| Addiction counselor | Advises people on substance use issues and substance use disorders | From a high school diploma (or GED) to a graduate degree |
| Advanced practice registered nurse (APRN) | Provides primary and specialty health care | At least a master's degree, a state license, and a national certification exam |
| Social worker / clinical social worker | Helps people cope with everyday issues, deal with relationships, and solve personal and family problems | Bachelor's or master's in social work from a Council on Social Work Education–accredited program |
| Community health worker (CHW) | Connects people to health and social services; supports preventive care and chronic disease management; culturally appropriate services | At least a high school diploma and some training |
| Marriage and family therapist (MFT) | Diagnoses and treats disorders in the context of marriage, couples, family systems, and other relationships | Typically a master's degree; every state requires a license |
| Mental health counselor / licensed professional counselor (LPC) | Advises individuals, families, couples, or groups on mental health issues | Generally at least a master's degree |
| Peer recovery support specialist (PRSS) | Uses lived experience of recovery to support others in nonclinical activities | At least a high school diploma; varies widely by state |
| Physician assistant (PA) | Examines, diagnoses, and treats patients under a physician's supervision | Master's degree from an accredited PA program; all states require a license |
| Prevention specialist | Coordinates and delivers evidence-based prevention policies, programs, and practices | High school diploma or bachelor's degree; certification varies by state |
| Psychiatric aide / technician | Helps people with daily living activities and overall well-being | At least a high school diploma and training |
| Addiction medicine physician | Licensed MD or DO providing prevention, evaluation, diagnosis, treatment, and recovery services for substance use | Accredited fellowship after internship and residency |
| Psychiatrist | MD or DO who specializes in diagnosing and treating mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders | Accredited 4-year residency after medical school |
| Psychologist | Studies cognitive, emotional, and social processes and behavior | Master's or doctoral degree; supervised experience and an exam for licensure |

- **Addiction counselor:** Works with and advises people on a range of issues related to substance use and substance use disorders, in treatment facilities, mental health centers, community health centers, and private practice
- Addiction counselor credentialing bodies include the International Certification and Reciprocity Consortium (IC&RC), the Association for Addiction Professionals (NAADAC), and the National Board for Certified Counselors
- **Social worker:** Assists people by helping them cope with issues in their everyday lives, deal with their relationships, and solve personal and family problems (child welfare and human service agencies, health care facilities, schools)
- **Community health worker (CHW):** Connects individuals to health and social services, supports preventive care and chronic disease management, and provides culturally appropriate health services; several states offer CHW certification
- **Marriage and family therapist (MFT):** Diagnoses and treats cognitive, behavioral, or similar disorders in the context of marriage, couples, family systems, and other relationships
- **Peer recovery support specialist (PRSS):** Also called a peer support worker; uses lived experience in recovery from a mental health or substance use disorder (directly or through a family member) for advocacy, navigation and linkage to resources, sharing experiences, social support, group facilitation, skill building, and mentoring
- **Prevention specialist:** Coordinates and delivers evidence-based policies, programs, and practices to prevent problem substance use; SAMHSA's Prevention Technology Transfer Centers offer Prevention Fellowships that prepare fellows for the IC&RC Certified Prevention Specialist (CPS) exam
- **Psychiatric aide:** Provides care for people with mental health conditions, substance use disorder, or developmental disabilities by helping with daily living activities; works mainly in psychiatric hospitals or residential facilities
- **Addiction medicine physician:** A licensed medical or osteopathic doctor with advanced training in prevention, evaluation, diagnosis, treatment, and recovery services for substance use disorders
- **Psychiatrist (SAMHSA):** A medical or osteopathic doctor who specializes in the diagnosis and treatment of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders; completes an accredited 4-year residency after medical school
- **Psychologist (SAMHSA):** Studies cognitive, emotional, and social processes and behavior by observing, interpreting, and recording how people interact with others and their environments
- HRSA programs fund training for many of these careers (for example, the Behavioral Health Workforce Education and Training Program, the Advanced Nursing Education Workforce program, the Graduate Psychology Education Program, and the Addiction Medicine Fellowship Program)

## Minority Fellowship Program and SAMHSA careers (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: About the Minority Fellowship Program](https://www.samhsa.gov/about/careers/fellowships/minority-fellowship-program/about)
- **Minority Fellowship Program (MFP):** Started in 1973; aims to reduce health disparities and improve behavioral health outcomes for underserved racial and ethnic populations by training culturally competent mental and substance use disorder professionals
- The MFP is administered by CMHS, CSAT, and CSAP and works through eight national behavioral health professional organizations
- The MFP was first for doctoral-level professionals only; in 2014, under President Obama's Now Is The Time initiative, it expanded to master's-level professionals serving youth ages 16 to 25 (MFP-Y) and master's-level addiction counselors (MFP-AC)
- Three MFP program areas: MFP-Traditional (doctoral level), MFP-Y (master's level), and MFP-AC (master's level)
- SAMHSA offers careers, internships for students, and fellowships; its Student Loan Repayment Program offers up to $10,000 per calendar year (lifetime maximum of $60,000)

## Types of mental health professionals (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Types of Mental Health Professionals](https://www.nami.org/treatments-and-approaches/types-of-mental-health-professionals/)
- Mental health professionals work in inpatient settings (general hospitals, psychiatric facilities) and outpatient settings (community mental health clinics, schools, private practices); job titles and specialties vary by state
- **Psychologist (NAMI):** Holds a doctoral degree (Ph.D. or Psy.D.); evaluates mental health with clinical interviews, psychological evaluations and testing, makes diagnoses, and provides individual and group therapy; licensed by state boards
- **Counselors, clinicians and therapists (NAMI):** Master's-level professionals (M.S. or M.A.) who evaluate mental health and use therapy techniques; licenses include LPC (Licensed Professional Counselor), LMFT (Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist) and LCADAC (Licensed Clinical Alcohol & Drug Abuse Counselor)
- **Clinical social worker (NAMI):** Holds a master's in social work (MSW); evaluates mental health, provides therapy, and is trained in case management and advocacy; licenses include LICSW, LCSW and ACSW
- **Psychiatrist (NAMI):** A licensed medical doctor (MD or DO) who completed a psychiatry residency; diagnoses conditions, prescribes and monitors medications, and provides therapy; may be board certified by the Board of Neurology and Psychiatry
- **Psychiatric or mental health nurse practitioner:** A nurse with an MS or Ph.D. focused on psychiatry who provides assessment, diagnosis and therapy, and in some states can prescribe medication (credential example: PMHNP-BC)
- Primary care physicians, pediatricians and family nurse practitioners (FNP) can prescribe medication, but NAMI suggests considering a mental health specialist, and they should work together on a treatment plan
- **Psychiatric pharmacist:** An advanced-practice pharmacist (PharmD) who specializes in mental health medication management and may prescribe or recommend medications where state law allows
- **Certified peer specialist:** A trained professional with lived experience of a mental health condition or substance use disorder who helps people set recovery goals and provides support and mentoring
- Social workers with a bachelor's degree (B.A. or B.S.) provide case management, inpatient discharge planning and placement services
- **Pastoral counselor:** A clergy member trained in clinical pastoral education who can diagnose and provide counseling
- The NAMI HelpLine can help people find mental health professionals but does not recommend specific providers
