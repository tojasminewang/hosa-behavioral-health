## Core concepts of motivation {pp. 252–253}
- **Motivation:** A process that arouses, maintains, and guides behavior toward a goal
- Motivational systems arouse an animal only when necessary (such as when it needs food) and reduce arousal once the problem is solved, saving energy
- **Homeostasis:** A steady internal balance or equilibrium; term introduced by Walter Cannon (1932)
- **Set point:** A value that is defended to maintain homeostasis (like a thermostat); e.g., core body temperature of 98.6 °F (37 °C) is restored by shivering or sweating
- **Drive:** A state of tension and arousal triggered by cues important for survival (Hull, 1943)
- **Drive reduction:** The state of relief and reward produced by removing the tension and arousal of the drive state
- Drive theories are "push" theories (drive pushes an organism toward a goal)
- **Incentive:** A reward that pulls an organism's behavior in a particular direction; incentive theories explain motivated behavior without any unpleasant drive state
- **Intrinsic reward:** A reward that arises internally, such as feelings of accomplishment
- **Extrinsic reward:** A reward from an outside source, such as grades, money, or praise
- Extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation (paying a child who loves reading may reduce enjoyment), but only when the behavior was intrinsically motivated in the first place
- Roland Fryer's school incentive experiments: paying children to read dramatically boosted reading comprehension, while paying for test scores produced no measurable change

## Hunger signals {pp. 253–257}
- Hunger is more influenced by emotion, learning, and culture than body temperature regulation
- External hunger cues: time of day, sights and smells of food, "clean your plate," social settings
- Walter Cannon and A. L. Washburn (balloon swallowing study): stomach contractions correlated with hunger, but people without stomachs still feel hunger
- **Glucose:** A type of sugar that plays an important role in hunger levels; hunger results whenever cells cannot obtain the glucose they require
- **Insulin:** A hormone released by the pancreas that moves circulating glucose from the blood into cells
- Type 1 diabetes = lack of insulin production; type 2 diabetes = the body's resistance to insulin; people with poorly managed diabetes feel hungry all the time because their cells are starving
- A person at a healthy weight has enough body fat to survive 5 to 6 weeks of total starvation (one person survived a supervised fast of 382 days)
- **Leptin:** A hormone secreted by fat cells ("leptos" = thin) that helps the body maintain an appropriate level of stored fat; leptin levels measure fat stores
- **Lateral hypothalamus (LH):** Initiates feeding when glucose, fat stores, and leptin are low; LH lesions make rats starve, and stimulating it starts eating
- When nutrients are low, the parasympathetic nervous system is activated and metabolism slows so nutrients are stored
- **Ghrelin:** A feeding-stimulating hormone released by the pancreas and stomach lining; increases with time since the last meal
- **Orexins:** Produced in the lateral hypothalamus; stimulate feeding and also participate in sleep
- **Satiety:** A sense of feeling full and not requiring further food
- **Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH):** Participates in sensing satiety; VMH lesions cause large weight gains (rats nearly tripled their weight)
- **Cholecystokinin (CCK):** A gut hormone released in response to food (especially fatty food) that limits meals
- When glucose, insulin, fat, and leptin rise, the sympathetic nervous system is activated, metabolism increases, and feeding is inhibited
- Obese humans have very high leptin levels but become resistant to its satiety message, so extra leptin is unlikely to cause weight loss
- Dieting lowers leptin, which triggers a feeding cycle, so maintaining weight loss is often harder than losing the weight

## Obesity {pp. 257–260}
- **Body mass index (BMI):** A height-to-weight ratio: weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters
- BMI under 18.5 = underweight; 18.5–24.9 = healthy; 25–30 = overweight; above 30 = obese; over 40 = severely/morbidly obese
- BMI does not account for unusually heavy skeletons or musculature (many elite athletes score as overweight)
- U.S. adult obesity tripled from 12% in 1991 to 37% in 2014; by 2018 no state had less than 20% of adults obese
- Causes of obesity: genetic predispositions (twin studies), gut bacteria, a sedentary lifestyle, preference for sugary and fatty foods, and social comparison
- At rest we use only 12 calories per pound per day; a 150-pound person needs only about 1,800 calories per day
- Having an obese spouse raises your risk of obesity by 37%; having obese friends raises it by 57%
- Once a person is obese, a new set point is established and defended; liposuction fat returns or redistributes within one year
- Successful diets are sustainable lifestyle changes; removing one sugared soft drink per day should produce about a 10-pound loss in a year; limit loss to a pound or two per week
- Amphetamines were prescribed for weight loss in the 1950s and 1960s but are addictive; orlistat (Xenical) reduces fat absorption; gastric bypass patients lose about 90 pounds in 3 months but face surgical complications

## Sexual motivation {pp. 262–267}
- Females of most mammals undergo estrus (a period of sexual receptivity); in humans menstrual cycles replace estrus, and women report more sexual interest around ovulation
- **Estradiol:** An important type of estrogen, or female sex hormone; female sexual interest has a negative relationship with progesterone and a weaker positive one with estradiol
- **Testosterone:** A male hormone; clearly correlated with male sexual interest, but within the normal range it does not strongly predict sexual frequency
- Men's testosterone rises before competition, keeps rising in winners, and drops in losers (more so when losing at home); it is lower in men in stable relationships and drops after the birth of a child
- Romantic love is associated with oxytocin and vasopressin; prairie voles (monogamous, shared parenting) have higher levels than montane voles
- Sexual desire is linked to the back of the insula; romantic love is linked to the front of the insula
- Committed relationships produce the highest sexual and emotional satisfaction; one-night stands produce the lowest and the most regret
- **Sexual orientation:** A stable pattern of attraction to members of a particular sex; includes behavior, attraction, and identity
- National Health Interview Survey: about 97.7% of females and 97.8% of males identified as straight; self-reported orientation falls along a continuum
- Male orientation tends to be stable, while females experience more fluidity in attraction over time
- Genetics: if one identical male twin is homosexual, the other has about a 50% chance of being homosexual; a GWAS of nearly 500,000 people found genetics explained about one-third of the variation in same-sex behavior
- Prenatal hormones matter: females with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) are exposed to high prenatal male hormones; males with older brothers from the same mother are more likely to be gay
- Simon LeVay (1991) found INAH-3 in the hypothalamus was two to three times smaller in homosexual men than heterosexual men (correlational data)

## Social and cognitive motives {pp. 267–271}
- **Affiliation:** Being associated with other people; most people rate love, intimacy, and social affiliation above wealth, fame, and even physical health
- Social isolation can be as harmful to health as high blood pressure, lack of exercise, obesity, or smoking; loneliness is an aversive signal (like hunger, thirst, and pain) that social connections need repair
- People have a genetically influenced set point for social activity; a gap between actual and desired connection produces loneliness
- People told they would end up alone showed less empathy, less helping, and more aggression
- Stanley Schachter (1959): people expecting a painful shock preferred to wait with others, but people expecting embarrassment preferred to be alone; surgery patients preferred roommates who had already had the surgery
- **Achievement motivation:** A desire to excel or outperform others on tasks at which a person could objectively succeed or fail; high achievers seek competitive, entrepreneurial occupations
- Carol Dweck: a **growth mindset** (belief that abilities can be developed) outperforms a **fixed mindset** (belief that abilities do not change)
- Expectancies (what people anticipate) and values (the weight people put on outcomes) shape task choice, persistence, and performance
- Endorsing participation-trophy culture was associated with higher entitlement and lower competitiveness (correlational)

## Prioritizing motives {pp. 271–274}
- Approach involves positive, desirable outcomes; avoidance involves negative, undesirable outcomes
- Conflict types: approach–approach (two positive choices), avoidance–avoidance (two unpleasant choices), and approach–avoidance
- Neal Miller (1944) shocked rats while they ate in a goal box; the rats stopped short at a point of equilibrium between approach and avoidance
- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs (Abraham Maslow, 1943):** Lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs are pursued; from bottom to top: physiological needs (food, water, shelter), safety, belongingness (love, affection), esteem, and self-actualization
- **Self-actualization:** A state of having fulfilled your potential ("Be all you can be"); Maslow believed few people (e.g., Abraham Lincoln, Gandhi, Eleanor Roosevelt) reach it
- In a starvation study, men on about 1,500 calories per day for 6 months became obsessed with food and lost interest in sex
- Individualistic nations like the U.S. value self-actualization more than collectivist Asian nations
- Kenrick et al. (2010) updated Maslow's pyramid, replacing self-actualization with mate acquisition, mate retention, and parenting
- During the COVID-19 pandemic many people placed affiliation above safety, which the priority models do not predict well
