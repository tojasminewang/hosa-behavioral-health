## Dissociative disorders (textbook) {pp. 584–585}
- **Dissociative disorder:** A disorder characterized by disruptions in a person's identity, memory, or consciousness
- **Dissociative amnesia:** Forgetting important information about specific events, often following a frightening or traumatic event
- **Dissociative fugue:** Confusion over one's identity, often combined with sudden travel and assuming a new identity; typically lasts a few hours or days
- **Depersonalization/derealization disorder:** Strong feelings of unreality about the self or the surrounding environment (watching oneself from outside the body, the world seeming dreamlike), while knowing the feelings are not realistic
- **Dissociative identity disorder:** Previously called multiple personality disorder; characterized by two distinct "personality states"; possibly the most controversial dissociative disorder
- Dissociative identity disorder diagnoses spiked in the mid-1990s after media attention (the books and films about "Sybil" and "Eve") and have since dropped; most cases were diagnosed in the U.S. and Canada
- Extreme stress often causes detachment, numbness, or altered memory, which well-meaning therapists may misinterpret as dissociative identity disorder
- People with dissociative disorders score very high on suggestibility (hypnosis susceptibility) and fantasy proneness
- Herschel Walker (Heisman Trophy winner, NFL star) has spoken about his dissociative identity disorder to encourage veterans to seek help
- **Somatic symptom disorder:** Having one or more chronic somatic (body) symptoms about which a person is excessively concerned, preoccupied, or fearful; patients visit physicians often and risk dependence on pain medication

## Treating dissociative and somatic disorders (textbook) {pp. 631–632}
- Some therapists treat dissociative identity disorder by integrating the personalities after "working through" traumatic memories, often using hypnosis, which is not supported by research and may create false memories
- In one case, hypnosis convinced a woman she had more than 120 personalities (including a duck); people labeled with the disorder may show worse symptoms afterward
- Critics suggest therapists should treat the stress, anxiety, and depression without referring to dissociation
- Somatic symptom disorders are treated with antidepressants, more physical activity, and CBT; 50% to 80% of patients referred for psychological treatment do not go

## Dissociative disorders (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Dissociative Disorders](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/dissociative-disorders/)
- NAMI describes dissociative disorders as an involuntary escape from reality, with a disconnection between thoughts, identity, consciousness and memory
- Up to 75% of people have at least one depersonalization/derealization episode in their lives, but only 2% meet full criteria for chronic episodes; women are more likely to be diagnosed
- Symptoms usually first develop as a response to trauma (such as abuse or military combat) to keep those memories under control, and stress can make them worse
- Signs: significant memory loss of specific times, people and events; out-of-body experiences; depression, anxiety or suicidal thoughts; emotional numbness; and a lack of a sense of self-identity
- The DSM defines three types of dissociative disorders: dissociative amnesia, depersonalization disorder and dissociative identity disorder
- **Dissociative amnesia:** Difficulty remembering important information about oneself, often surrounding an event such as combat or abuse; onset is usually sudden, and an episode can last minutes or, rarely, years
- **Depersonalization:** Feeling detached from one's own actions, feelings and thoughts, as if watching a movie of oneself
- **Derealization:** Feeling that the people and things in the world around you are unreal
- Depersonalization disorder has an average onset age of 16; fewer than 20% of people with it start having episodes after age 20
- **Dissociative identity disorder (DID):** Formerly called multiple personality disorder; alternating between multiple identities, each possibly with its own name, traits and voice, along with gaps in memory
- Women are more likely to be diagnosed with DID; men are more likely to deny symptoms and trauma and show violent behavior, which can lead to false-negative diagnoses
- Dissociative disorders most often form in children exposed to long-term physical, sexual or emotional abuse; natural disasters and combat can also cause them
- Doctors first rule out physical causes of memory loss and unreality, such as head injury, brain lesions or tumors, sleep deprivation or intoxication
- Culture shapes symptoms: where possession is part of cultural beliefs, DID identities may appear as spirits, deities, demons or animals, and voluntary depersonalization in meditation is not a disorder
- NAMI dissociative treatments: psychotherapies such as CBT and DBT, EMDR, and medications such as antidepressants for related symptoms; no drug treats dissociation itself
- **EMDR (eye movement desensitization and reprocessing):** A therapy that combines CBT techniques with visual stimulation exercises to reduce distress from traumatic memories and replace negative beliefs with positive ones
- Treatment goals: safely recall and process painful memories, build coping skills, and in DID integrate the identities into one functional person
- Self-help: journaling, and grounding mindfulness that uses the senses (touching fabric, smelling a strong scent, slow deep breathing) to come back to the present
- Related conditions: PTSD, BPD, substance use disorders, depression and anxiety, because dissociative disorders are on the trauma spectrum
- Resources: the Sidran Institute (trauma education and advocacy) and the International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation
