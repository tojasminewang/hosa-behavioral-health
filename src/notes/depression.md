## Major depressive disorder (textbook) {pp. 568–569}
- **Major depressive disorder (MDD):** Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day, for a period of at least 2 weeks (DSM-5); patients feel sad and empty, with periods of tearfulness
- **Anhedonia:** Loss of pleasure; disinterest in activities that previously provided pleasure (sex, eating, social activities)
- Diagnosis requires at least five symptoms, one of which must be depressed mood or anhedonia
- Physical symptoms: disturbed appetite (loss or overeating), disturbed sleep (frequent waking or oversleeping more than 9 hours), fatigue or restlessness
- Cognitive symptoms: difficulty concentrating, hopelessness, worthlessness, and in some cases thoughts of suicide
- MDD affects about 7% of adults each year; people aged 18–29 have rates three times higher than people over 60
- Women experience MDD more than men regardless of race, ethnicity, social class, or country; possible reasons include hormones (postpartum depression, menopause), women being more likely to admit depression and seek help, and men masking depression with alcohol
- **Major depressive disorder with seasonal pattern:** Depression at the same time each year, usually winter; formerly seasonal affective disorder (SAD); treated with bright light therapy

## Causes of MDD (textbook) {pp. 569–573}
- **Learning explanation:** MDD occurs with a reduction in positive reinforcement or an increase in negative outcomes (e.g., losing an important relationship)
- **Learned helplessness:** A state in which experiencing random or uncontrolled consequences leads to feelings of helplessness and possibly depression
- Maier and Seligman (2016) now argue inhibited escape is the default response to long aversive events; it is the ability to control events (learned hope) that must be learned
- **Cognitive explanation (Aaron Beck):** MDD results from negative thoughts about the self, the world, and the future (the basis of cognitive therapy for depression)
- **Rumination:** Repetitively focusing on the fact that one is depressed and on its symptoms, causes, meanings, and consequences (Susan Nolen-Hoeksema); it interferes with problem solving, delays recovery, and increases alcohol abuse risk
- Women are more likely to ruminate, while men more often use distraction, which may help explain gender differences
- **Attributional style:** People who make internal, stable, and global attributions ("it's my fault, it never changes, I'm always this way") are more prone to depression
- **Social explanation:** Loneliness (perceived social isolation) is a biological signal like hunger; in older adults loneliness increased depressive symptoms, but depression did not increase loneliness
- **Biological explanation:** Heritability of MDD is about 35%; more than 80 genes are relevant; serotonin is strongly involved, and most effective antidepressants boost serotonin at the synapse
- MDD may be part of a larger disturbance in circadian rhythms; untreated MDD involves too much REM sleep and loss of deep (stage 3 and 4) sleep, and most antidepressants reduce REM sleep
- People with MDD are more sensitive to subtle sad facial expressions and may misread neutral faces as sad
- **Stress:** Severe stress frequently comes right before a depressive episode; stressed mice and depressed humans have low levels of the protein p11, which rebounds with antidepressants
- **Diathesis–stress model:** Biological predispositions for MDD interact with a person's experience of stress; absolute cortisol levels fail to predict most cases
- Low thyroid levels can mimic the symptoms of depression; the hippocampus's stress "brake" works less well in MDD

## Suicidality (textbook) {p. 574}
- Depression is a risk factor for suicidal ideation (thinking about suicide) and suicide attempts
- Bisexual students have greater risk of mental health problems and suicidality than heterosexual or gay/lesbian students; transgender students report significantly higher rates than males or females
- Among young adults 18–24, males were far more likely to die by suicide than females in all racial and ethnic categories; non-Hispanic Whites and American Indian or Alaska Native individuals had the highest rates
- Risk factors that together may increase suicide risk: depression, impulsivity, alcohol or substance use, a history of suicide attempts, and a recent breakup or other traumatic event

## Treating MDD (textbook) {pp. 624–627}
- Medication is the most popular treatment; U.S. antidepressant use rose nearly 400% between 1988 and 2008, and about 10% of Americans over 12 use antidepressants
- **SSRIs** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors such as Prozac and Zoloft) are the most widely prescribed antidepressants; they slow serotonin reuptake so more serotonin stays in the synapse
- Antidepressants may also increase the number of neurons in the hippocampus
- SSRI side effects: increased suicidal thinking (especially under age 30, with warnings for close supervision), nausea, reduced REM sleep, headache, weight gain, and sexual dysfunction (about 40%)
- When medication fails: ECT, deep brain stimulation, or rTMS
- **CBT** for depression restructures negative thoughts and increases activity and social skills; 40% to 60% get relief and are less likely to relapse than with medication alone
- **Aerobic exercise** is comparable to medication and psychotherapy for mild to moderate depression
- **Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT):** Combines CBT with mindfulness-based stress reduction ("Thoughts are not facts"); cut recurrence by nearly 50% in people with three or more past episodes

## Depression (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Depression](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/depression) · [Seasonal Affective Disorder](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder)
- Depression is a disorder of the brain and a serious mental illness, more than feeling "down in the dumps" or "blue" for a few days; more than 20 million people in the U.S. have depression

| Type of depression | Description |
|---|---|
| Major depressive disorder (clinical depression) | A consistent dark mood that inhibits daily functions and causes loss of interest in usually pleasurable activities |
| Persistent depressive disorder | Low mood lasting two or more years in adults and at least one year in children and adolescents |
| Postpartum depression | Intense, long-lasting anxiety, sadness, and fatigue after having a baby; can start weeks to months after childbirth |
| Psychotic depression | Depression with psychosis, such as delusions and/or hallucinations |
| Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) | Depression associated with changes in seasons, usually in fall and winter when there is less sunlight |

- Bipolar disorder is different from depression, but a person with bipolar disorder can have episodes of major depression
- Causes of depression include genetic, environmental, psychological, and biochemical factors; risk rises with family history, trauma, major life changes, stress, certain physical illnesses (diabetes, cancer, Parkinson's), or as a side effect of medications
- To be diagnosed with MDD, symptoms must be present nearly every day for at least two weeks: sadness, hopelessness, fatigue, sleep problems, irritability, worthlessness or guilt, loss of interest, appetite or weight changes, slowed or fidgety movement, forgetfulness, trouble concentrating, and thoughts of death or suicide
- Most people with major depression never seek the right treatment, yet it is one of the most common and most treatable mental health disorders
- Treatment: medications (antidepressants) plus talk therapy (most people do best with both); ECT and other brain stimulation therapies if standard treatments fail
- Healthy habits: self-care (most adults need 7–9 hours of sleep), set small achievable goals, know your warning signs, educate family and friends, seek support, and stick to the treatment plan (stopping medication abruptly can cause withdrawal and relapse)
- A **self-management education (SME)** program can help patients manage depression symptoms
- SAD is not a separate disorder but a type of depression; it usually lifts in spring and summer; symptoms include oversleeping, overeating with carbohydrate cravings, weight gain, and social withdrawal ("hibernating")

## Major depressive disorder (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Major Depressive Disorder](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/depression/)
- **Major depressive disorder (MDD):** Also called clinical depression; persistent sadness for at least two weeks along with more severe symptoms such as low energy and hopelessness across many areas of life
- About 15.5% of U.S. adults experience major depressive disorder in a given year
- Some people have only one depressive episode, but for most the episodes recur; untreated episodes may last a few months to several years
- NAMI causes of depression: early trauma (it changes how the brain responds to fear and stress), genetics (mood disorders run in families), life circumstances, brain changes, other medical conditions, and drug and alcohol misuse
- Brain imaging shows the frontal lobe becomes less active during depression, which is also linked to changes in how the pituitary gland and hypothalamus respond to hormones
- Hypothyroidism can mimic depression, and some medications can cause depressive symptoms
- DSM-5-TR criteria for MDD: at least 5 symptoms most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks, and one of them must be depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure
- Symptoms that count toward MDD: changes in sleep or appetite, poor concentration, loss of energy, loss of interest, hopelessness or guilt, aches or feeling slowed down or agitated, and suicidal thoughts
- **PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire):** A short screening questionnaire that helps detect depression and measure how severe it is (the Beck Depression Inventory is another)
- There is no blood test or brain scan for depression; if a person has had manic or hypomanic episodes, the diagnosis may be bipolar disorder instead
- Depression treatment can include psychotherapy, medications, exercise, brain stimulation, light therapy, complementary approaches and newer treatments such as ketamine; for most people, therapy plus medication works better than either alone
- **Interpersonal therapy (IPT):** A time-limited therapy that improves problems in personal relationships that may contribute to depression
- **Psychodynamic therapy:** Therapy that works to understand and resolve problems rooted in past experiences and unconscious processes
- **Psychoeducation:** Teaching people about their illness, its treatment and the warning signs that symptoms are returning
- Antidepressants often take 2–4 weeks to start working and up to 12 weeks to reach full effect; in a person with underlying bipolar disorder they can trigger a manic episode
- **SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors):** The most commonly prescribed antidepressants; they act on serotonin (for example fluoxetine/Prozac, sertraline/Zoloft, escitalopram/Lexapro)
- **SNRIs (serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors):** Antidepressants that increase serotonin and norepinephrine (for example duloxetine/Cymbalta, venlafaxine/Effexor)
- **Bupropion (Wellbutrin):** An NDRI antidepressant that increases dopamine and norepinephrine and has fewer (and different) side effects than other antidepressants
- Mirtazapine (Remeron), a tetracyclic antidepressant, is used less often because it causes more weight gain and sleepiness
- **Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):** Older antidepressants with more side effects, used when others have not worked; they may also ease chronic pain
- **MAOIs (monoamine oxidase inhibitors):** Older antidepressants with dietary restrictions that must never be combined with SSRIs
- Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics can be used for treatment-resistant depression
- ECT can cause side effects including memory loss; **rTMS** (repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation) uses a magnet instead of electric current, is FDA approved for treatment-resistant depression, and is not effective as a maintenance treatment
- **Light therapy:** Sitting near a light box that mimics sunlight, usually in the morning, to help depression with a seasonal pattern (the devices are not FDA approved)
- Aerobic exercise can help treat mild depression by increasing endorphins and stimulating norepinephrine
- St. John's wort has chemical properties similar to some SSRIs, and combining it with SSRIs carries substantial risks
- **Treatment-resistant depression:** Depression that has not responded to at least two antidepressants
- **Esketamine (Spravato):** An FDA-approved nasal spray form of ketamine for adults with treatment-resistant depression, given under supervision in a certified clinic; ketamine itself (usually given by IV) is not FDA approved for MDD
- Ketamine and esketamine work on the brain's glutamate system, unlike most antidepressants, which act on serotonin, dopamine or norepinephrine
- Psilocybin-assisted therapy for depression is still in clinical trials and is not FDA approved
- Helping a loved one with depression: recognize early signs (low mood, fatigue, trouble sleeping), don't tell them to "try harder" to "just be happy," make specific offers to help, and stay calm
- 988 (call, text or chat) is the national Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week
