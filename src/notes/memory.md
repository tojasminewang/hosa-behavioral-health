## What is memory? {pp. 317–319}
- **Memory:** The ability to retain knowledge
- **Henry Molaison (patient H.M.):** In 1953 surgeons removed most of his hippocampus in both hemispheres to reduce severe seizures; his personality and intellect remained, but he could not form new long-term declarative memories; studied by Brenda Milner and Suzanne Corkin for more than four decades
- **Information processing:** A continuum including attention, sensation, perception, learning, memory, and cognition; information flows both bottom-up and top-down
- Memory has three steps: encoding, storage, and retrieval
- **Encoding:** The transformation of information from one form to another (acquiring information and transferring it into memory); information can be encoded as visual, acoustic (sound), or semantic (meaningful) codes
- **Storage:** The retention of information; lasts from fractions of a second (sensory memory) to seconds (short-term/working memory) to indefinitely (long-term memory)
- Unlike a computer, human memory does not store exact records; bits of information are stored and later reconstructed, so errors and distortions can occur
- **Retrieval:** Locating stored information when it is needed; two common causes of retrieval failure are interference and stress
- Memory evolved to use past information to coordinate present behavior, but it costs energy: fruit flies that formed memories died about 4 hours earlier
- Encoding failure (not paying attention in a lecture) is one of the most common memory problems students face

## The Atkinson–Shiffrin model {pp. 319–325}
- **Atkinson–Shiffrin model (1968):** Information flows through separate stages: sensory memory → short-term memory → long-term memory; if information is not transferred to the next stage, it is lost
- **Sensory memory:** The first stage; holds enormous amounts of sensory data for brief periods (usually a second or less), like a fading "echo" of the input
- **Iconic memory:** Visual sensory memory; **echoic memory:** acoustic sensory memory, which lasts longer than iconic memory (to hear whole words and phrases); haptic codes handle touch
- George Sperling showed participants could recall whole rows of a briefly flashed letter matrix when cued by a tone within a quarter of a second, proving the whole matrix was briefly held in sensory memory
- **Short-term memory (STM):** The second stage of the Atkinson–Shiffrin model that holds a small amount of information for a limited time (30 seconds at most without rehearsal)
- Peterson and Peterson (1959): recall of letter triplets dropped quickly when people counted backward by 3s
- **Rehearsal:** Repeating information to keep it in short-term memory; information in STM is easily displaced by new incoming information
- George Miller (1956): STM holds "the magical number 7 plus or minus 2" items (5 to 9); more recently psychologists set the limit at about four items
- **Chunking:** Grouping similar or meaningful information together to expand STM capacity (FBI IRS CIA EPA = 4 chunks instead of 12 letters)
- Chess masters recall real game positions better than nonplayers (chunking) but not randomly placed pieces
- **Working memory:** An extension of short-term memory that includes the active manipulation of multiple types of information simultaneously (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974)
- Working memory components: the phonological loop (verbal and auditory information), the visuospatial sketch pad (visual and spatial information), the central executive (directs attention to tasks), and the episodic buffer (combines long-term memory with working memory)
- **Long-term memory:** The final stage, with few limitations in capacity or duration; information can last a lifetime

## Moving information into long-term memory {pp. 325–328}
- **Maintenance rehearsal:** Simple repetition of material
- **Elaborative rehearsal:** Linking new material to things you already know; more effective for moving information into long-term storage
- **Levels of processing theory:** The depth (shallow to deep) of processing applied to information predicts its ease of retrieval; encoding meaning is deeper than encoding sound, which is deeper than encoding appearance (Craik & Tulving, 1975)
- **Serial position effect:** When recalling a list in any order, people remember the first and last items best (a U-shaped curve)
- **Primacy effect:** Superior recall of the first items on a list, because they were rehearsed into long-term memory
- **Recency effect:** Superior recall of the last items on a list, because they are still in working memory; a 30-second delay erases the recency effect but not the primacy effect
- Patient K.F. had the opposite problem from H.M.: normal long-term memory but severely impaired working memory (only one or two digits)

## Types of long-term memory {pp. 328–332}
- **Declarative memory:** A consciously retrieved memory that is easy to verbalize, including semantic, episodic, and autobiographical information; also known as explicit memory
- **Nondeclarative memory:** An unconsciously and effortlessly retrieved memory that is difficult to verbalize, such as classical conditioning, procedural learning, and priming; also known as implicit memory
- **Semantic memory:** Your store of general knowledge in the form of word meanings and facts; organized by categories; gives an objective understanding of the world
- **Episodic memory:** A memory for personal experience; organized as a timeline; can result from a single experience; a reference point for the self
- **Autobiographical memory:** Semantic or episodic memories that reference the self
- **Highly superior autobiographical memory (HSAM):** A rare condition of nearly perfect recall for dates and personal and public events
- **Procedural memory:** A nondeclarative (implicit) memory for how to carry out skilled movement; also called skill memory; it automates performance (you "just drive")
- **Priming:** A change in a response to a stimulus as a result of exposure to a previous stimulus; studied with the lexical decision task (related words like doctor–nurse are recognized faster)
- People rate ads they have seen before more positively, even if they cannot remember seeing them

## How long-term memory is organized {pp. 333–335}
- **Connectionism:** Views the mind as a network of simpler units or concepts; thinking about one concept leads to related concepts
- **Spreading activation model:** People organize memories in personal networks in which activating one concept spreads to connected concepts; closer links are activated faster (an orange is recognized as a fruit faster than an avocado)
- **Schema:** A set of expectations about objects and situations; details consistent with schemas are retained or even added, while inconsistent details are left out (Frederic Bartlett, 1932)
- Schemas can create false memories (people "remember" the word sleep in a list of sleep-related words) and improve memory for confusing passages (the laundry passage)

## Retrieving memories {pp. 335–342}
- Searching short-term memory happens one item at a time (Sternberg): each additional item adds about 38 milliseconds
- **Cue:** A stimulus that aids retrieval; the most effective cues are those we generate ourselves (90% recall vs. 55%)
- Recognition tasks (true–false, matching) provide complete cues and are easier; recall tasks (essays) require a generate–recognize step
- **Encoding specificity:** A process in which memories incorporate unique combinations of information when encoded, so anything present during encoding can later serve as a cue
- **Context-dependent memory:** Scuba divers recalled more words when tested in the same place they learned them (land or underwater); studying in a test-like setting helps
- State-dependent effects: people with bipolar disorder recalled words best when in the same mood state (mania or depression) as when they learned them
- **Tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) phenomenon:** Retrieval is gradual, not all-or-none; people often know the first letter and number of syllables (e.g., "sextant")
- **Own-race bias:** Better memory for faces of one's own race; influenced by perception and in-group/out-group factors
- **Reconstruction:** Building a memory out of stored bits by blending retrieved information with new content in working memory; each retrieval weakens a memory, which must be reconsolidated
- Elizabeth Loftus: after a question mentioning a barn, 20% "remembered" a barn (vs. under 5%); hearing "smashed" instead of "hit" made people "remember" broken glass
- **Fuzzy trace theory:** We form verbatim (exact) memories or gist (general idea) memories based on our needs; relying on gist can lead to false memories
- **Source monitoring:** Distinguishing internal sources ("I thought that") from external sources ("That happened"); errors produce false memories
- **Flashbulb memory:** An especially vivid and detailed memory of an emotional event (e.g., 9/11), though not always accurate
- Stress at the same time as learning can enhance memory; stress before or after learning impairs it; chronic stress is linked to loss of hippocampal volume
- Eyewitnesses: about 75% of DNA exonerations involved mistaken eyewitness identification; sequential lineups produce fewer mistakes than simultaneous lineups; confidence at the time of the first identification predicts accuracy better than courtroom confidence

## Why we forget {pp. 342–345}
- **Forgetting:** A decrease in the ability to remember a previously formed memory (the memory must have been formed first)
- **Amnesia:** Loss of information due to brain injury or disease
- Forgetting is adaptive: it prioritizes what we should remember (the prefrontal cortex suppresses less-used memories, like old passwords)
- **Decay:** The ability to retrieve unused information fades over time; most psychologists believe time alone does not predict forgetting well
- **Method of savings:** Compares the rate of learning material the first time with relearning it later; people retained most high school Spanish vocabulary 50 years later
- **Interference:** Competition between newer and older information in memory
- **Proactive interference:** Reduced memory for target information because of earlier learning
- **Retroactive interference:** Reduced memory for target information because of later learning
- Head injuries, general anesthesia, and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) can interrupt consolidation, causing memory loss around the event
- **Motivated forgetting:** Failure to retrieve negative memories (e.g., more people who owe taxes "forget" to sign their returns); ranges from suppression (conscious) to repression (no conscious memory)
- Loftus implanted a false memory of a childhood hot air balloon ride in 50% of participants using a doctored photo; a cautious approach to recovered memories is recommended

## The biology of memory {pp. 345–350}
- Eric Kandel (Nobel Laureate) showed lasting changes in synaptic strength during learning in the sea slug; axon terminals increase after sensitization and decrease after habituation
- **Long-term potentiation (LTP):** A process that enhances communication between two neurons; like memory, it is long-lasting and forms after brief exposure to stimuli
- Working memory uses the same posterior brain areas as perception, with attention from the prefrontal and parietal cortex
- The hippocampus participates in consolidating semantic and location information into long-term memory
- **Anterograde amnesia:** The inability to form new memories (H.M.); H.M. still learned the mirror-tracing task normally, showing procedural memory is separate from declarative memory
- Semantic memories are widely distributed across the cortex (naming animals activates the occipital lobe; naming tools activates frontal and parietal movement areas)
- Early Alzheimer's disease causes more dramatic episodic than semantic memory deficits
- **Source amnesia:** Prefrontal damage in which people keep semantic knowledge but forget how they acquired it
- Procedural memories are correlated with the basal ganglia; people with Huntington's and Parkinson's disease have trouble learning new procedures
- Acetylcholine (ACh) affects encoding of new information; Alzheimer's disease involves degeneration of ACh circuits, and Alzheimer's medications boost ACh
- Glutamate's NMDA receptor is a prime candidate for learning-related changes such as LTP
- Caffeine can enhance memory by blocking adenosine
- Working memory capacity correlates with GABA activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex; working memory impairments occur in schizophrenia and dementia

## Improving memory {pp. 350–357}
- **Distributed practice:** Practice spread out over time; produces better memory than massed practice (cramming)
- **Testing effect:** Taking tests produces better long-term memory than restudying; retrieval practice also protects memory from stress
- **Interleaving:** Mixing up topics (ABCABC) works better than blocking (AABBCC)
- Taking notes by longhand improved performance on conceptual questions compared with laptops (laptop users copied word-for-word)
- Vigorous exercise increases neurogenesis in the hippocampus (in mice)
- Sleep actively consolidates memories; all-nighters harm memory, and two nights of normal sleep do not make up for it
- **Recitation:** Putting material into your own words; uses the advantage of self-referential information
- **Mnemonics:** Memory aids that link new information to well-known information; e.g., acronyms (OCEAN for the Big Five) and the **method of loci** (placing items along an imagined familiar route)
- **Transactive memory:** A division of memory labor in couples and groups, where each knows what the other knows
- Working memory capacity predicted compliance with social distancing recommendations during COVID-19
