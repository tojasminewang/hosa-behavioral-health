## Signs you may need help (SAMHSA)
- It may be time to ask for help if you've had **two or more weeks** with changes to your thoughts, moods, or body that make it hard to manage work, school, home, or relationships
- Feelings: very worried, afraid, sad, or down; much more angry or irritated than normal; big mood changes; tired; feeling unable to manage the day
- Thoughts: about suicide; mixed up; trouble focusing or remembering; all about one thing; that things aren't real; focused only on getting and using alcohol or drugs
- Body changes: unexplained headaches or stomachaches; changes in sleep or eating; needing to use drugs or alcohol more often to keep from getting sick
- Behavior changes: not taking care of yourself (not showering, skipping doctor visits); consequences at work or with friends; involvement with the legal system
- Connecting with people: trouble relating to others, avoiding friends and family, changing friends often in a short time
- Signs in young children: changes at school, too much worry, being unusually fearful and clingy, frequent nightmares, hyperactivity, not following directions or aggression, frequent tantrums, needing a great deal of comfort
- Mental Health America offers online screenings for drug and alcohol misuse, depression, anxiety, postpartum depression, and children's mental health

## Signs of a substance use disorder (SAMHSA)
- SUDs can be hard to identify because people may function well and hide their use
- Behavioral changes: drop in attendance and performance at work or school, frequent trouble (fights, accidents, illegal activities), secretive behavior, appetite or sleep changes, unexplained personality change, mood swings, unusual hyperactivity or giddiness, lack of motivation, appearing fearful or paranoid
- Physical changes: bloodshot eyes and abnormally sized pupils, sudden weight loss or gain, deteriorating appearance, unusual smells on breath, body, or clothing, tremors, slurred speech, impaired coordination
- Social changes: sudden change in friends, hangouts, and hobbies; legal problems; unexplained need for money; continuing to use despite relationship problems

## Warning signs of suicide (SAMHSA)
- Intervene immediately if you notice these signs; risk is greater if a behavior is new or has increased, especially if related to a painful event, loss, or change

| Adult warning signs | Youth warning signs |
|---|---|
| Talking about or making plans for suicide | Expressing hopelessness about the future |
| Acting anxious, agitated, or behaving recklessly | Severe or overwhelming emotional pain or distress |
| Talking about being a burden to others | More physical symptoms (fatigue, headaches, stomachaches) linked to emotional distress |
| Talking about feeling trapped or in unbearable pain | Changing or withdrawing from social connections |
| Increasing use of alcohol or drugs | Changes in sleep (increased or decreased) |
| Talking about feeling hopeless or having no reason to live | Anger or hostility that seems out of character |
| Sleeping too little or too much; withdrawing or isolating | Recent increased agitation or irritability |
| Showing rage or talking about seeking revenge; extreme mood swings | |

## Helping someone who may be at risk of suicide (SAMHSA)
- Call 911 if danger of self-harm seems imminent
- Call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline
- **Ask them if they are thinking about killing themselves**; this will not put the idea into their head or make an attempt more likely
- Listen without judging and show you care
- Stay with the person or make sure they are in a private, secure place with another caring person until you can get further help
- Remove any objects that could be used in a suicide attempt

## Self-harm (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Self-Harm](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/self-harm)
- **Self-harm:** When a person hurts their own body on purpose; more common among women than men
- A person who self-harms usually does not mean to kill themselves, but they are at higher risk of attempting suicide and dying by suicide if they do not get help
- Self-harm tends to begin in the teen or early adult years; for many it gives a sense of relief and is used to cope (teens say they are trying to stop feeling lonely, angry, or hopeless)
- Examples: cutting or piercing the skin, hitting oneself or punching things, burning oneself, breaking bones or bruising oneself
- Warning signs: scars; wearing long sleeves or pants in hot weather; talking about feeling worthless or helpless; fresh cuts, bruises, bite marks, or burns; keeping sharp objects; frequent "accidental" injuries; emotional instability

## The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (SAMHSA)
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline:** A national hotline offering free, confidential, 24/7 one-on-one support by call, text, or chat (988lifeline.org) for suicidal, mental health, and substance use crises
- 988 serves as a **universal entry point**: no matter where you live in the U.S., you can reach a trained crisis counselor
- People can contact 988 for themselves or if they are worried about a loved one; there is no reason too big or too small (substance use, emotional distress, anxiety, depression, loneliness, trauma, bullying, stress, relationship troubles)
- 988 operates through what was formerly the **National Suicide Prevention Lifeline** (1-800-273-8255, which still works)
- 988 is a network of over 200 local, independent, and state crisis centers, backed up by a national network; it is funded by SAMHSA and administered by **Vibrant Emotional Health** (a not-for-profit that does not sell Lifeline data)
- Specialized services: Veterans Crisis Line (press 1, or text 838255), Spanish (press 2, or text "ayuda"), videophone in ASL for people who are Deaf or Hard of Hearing, and interpretation in more than 240 languages through Language Line Solutions
- No payment or insurance information is required; callers do not have to give personal information
- Studies show most callers feel less depressed, less suicidal, less overwhelmed, and more hopeful after talking with a 988 counselor; most contacts are resolved without involving 911
- **Georouting:** Routes calls to a nearby crisis center based on general location without sharing the caller's precise location; **geolocation** (pinpoint location) is not used by 988
- In 2020 and 2021–2022 the U.S. had about one death by suicide every 11 minutes; suicide was the second-leading cause of death for ages 10–14 and 25–34
- HHS has invested nearly $1.5 billion in 988; the National Suicide Hotline Designation Act of 2020 lets states enact telecommunication fees to fund 988

## 988, 911, and 211 (SAMHSA)
| Number | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 988 | Emotional and behavioral health support, crisis de-escalation, and community connections, with minimal law enforcement involvement unless there is an immediate physical safety threat |
| 911 | Medical emergencies, fire, crimes in progress, and situations needing immediate physical intervention; not specialized for behavioral health crises |
| 211 | Health and social service information and referrals (also disaster information such as shelters and food) |

![Which number does what](numbers-988)

- A 988 counselor contacts 911 only when there is imminent risk to life that cannot be reduced during the contact (e.g., a suicide attempt in progress, a specific plan with means at hand, a suspected overdose, or chest pain or shortness of breath that could be a medical emergency)
- The 988 response promotes stabilization and care in the **least restrictive** manner; every crisis center follows the 988 Lifeline's Suicide Safety Policy
- Federal funding for 988 is separate from 911 funding

## Crisis care systems (SAMHSA)
- SAMHSA sees 988 as a first step toward a transformed crisis care system, which will grow like 911 and emergency medical services did over five decades
- **National Behavioral Health Crisis Care Guidance:** A framework for crisis care built on three foundational elements:
  - **Someone to contact:** Services like the 988 Lifeline and other hotlines
  - **Someone to respond:** Mobile crisis teams that deliver rapid, on-site de-escalation and connect people to care
  - **A safe place for help:** Stabilization services where people receive immediate treatment and support
- **Mobile crisis teams:** Teams of mental health and substance use professionals, paraprofessionals, and peer support workers who respond to people in crisis in the community as an alternative to law enforcement, avoiding unnecessary emergency department use and hospitalization
- Mobile crisis teams triage and screen for suicidality, assess, de-escalate, provide peer support, coordinate follow-up care, create a safety plan, and connect people to community support
- Two main models: **mobile crisis teams** (behavioral health staff and peers) and **co-responder teams** (law enforcement or EMS staff with a mental health professional)
- Usually a two-person team responds; responders are trained in trauma-informed care, de-escalation, cultural sensitivity, screening, crisis intervention, and safety planning
- Under the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021, states covering mobile crisis services under Medicaid can receive an enhanced 85% federal match; SAMHSA recommends no one be denied crisis services based on ability to pay
- Most people with suicidal thoughts will not require involuntary transport or hospitalization; involuntary hospitalization may be needed with imminent risk when other efforts fail

## Talking about mental health (SAMHSA)
- Hesitation to talk about mental health adds to the notion that it is taboo; normalizing these conversations empowers people to seek help
- Learning about mental health leads to improved recognition of early signs, earlier treatment, and greater understanding and compassion
- To support a friend or family member: find out if they're getting care, express concern, remind them help is available and conditions can be treated, ask and listen, reassure them, help with everyday tasks, keep inviting them, educate others, and treat them with respect
- Conversation starters include "I've been worried about you. Can we talk about what you are experiencing?" and "I'm concerned about your safety. Have you thought about harming yourself or others?"
- Speak in a straightforward way at a level appropriate to the person's age (preschool children need fewer details than teens), when and where they feel safe, and slow down if they seem upset
- Comparing to physical illness helps: many people get a cold, but a few get pneumonia and need medicine or the hospital; likewise, when common feelings become intense, long-lasting, and interfere with life, it may be a mental health problem
- Helping someone with a mental health or substance issue: you can't force someone to get care; listen without judgment and use **open-ended questions** (starting with how, what, or where), comment on strengths, reflect back what you heard ("I heard you say..."), summarize, and offer to follow up
- Asking for help is a sign of strength, not weakness: think about what you need, choose someone you trust, pick a private time and place, describe your feelings clearly, and keep reaching out if the first person cannot help
- Warning signs in children and students that should prompt consulting a school counselor, nurse, or provider: feeling very sad or withdrawn for more than two weeks, trying or planning to harm themselves, sudden overwhelming fear, many fights, out-of-control behavior, not eating or using laxatives, intense worries, drug or alcohol use, severe mood swings, drastic behavior changes, and dropping grades
- Pay special attention to children after the loss of a loved one, divorce, a major move or school change, trauma, bullying, or school difficulties
- Educators are often the first to notice mental health problems; faith and community leaders are often the first point of contact in a crisis and can serve as an expanded safety net for people with suicidal thoughts
- Effective school mental health programs promote healthy social and emotional development, recognize when young people are at risk, and identify how to intervene early
- **Shared decision-making:** Participating fully with your provider to make informed treatment decisions together (recognize a decision is needed, treat partners and options as equal, explore expectations, identify preferences, negotiate, share the decision, and arrange follow-up)
- Peer support helps recovery because people with common experiences can uniquely help each other and serve as living examples that people recover

## SBIRT (SAMHSA)
- **SBIRT (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment):** A comprehensive, integrated, public health approach to early intervention and treatment for people with substance use disorders and those at risk of developing them
- SBIRT is delivered in primary care, emergency rooms, trauma centers, schools, and other community settings, with a focus on screening for underage drinking, opioid use, and other substance use

| SBIRT component | What it does |
|---|---|
| Screening | Quickly assesses the severity of substance use and identifies the appropriate level of treatment |
| Brief intervention | Increases insight and awareness about substance use and motivation toward behavioral change |
| Referral to treatment | Gives those needing more extensive treatment access to specialty care |

![SBIRT in three steps](sbirt)

- SBIRT grants (2021–2023) served more than 450,000 people; clients reporting no alcohol or illegal drug use in the past 30 days increased 128.8% from screening to six-month follow-up
- The Drug Abuse Screening Test (DAST-10) is one screening tool; screening and brief intervention can be reimbursed through CPT codes, Medicare G codes, and Medicaid HCPCS codes
- Problem identification and referral (a prevention strategy) is different from SBIRT because it does not determine whether a person needs treatment

## Warning signs of mental illness (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Warning Signs and Symptoms](https://www.nami.org/about-mental-illness/warning-signs-and-symptoms/)
- There is no easy test to tell expected behavior apart from signs of mental illness or the effects of a physical illness
- NAMI's common signs of mental illness in adults and adolescents:
  - Excessive worrying or fear, or feeling excessively sad or low
  - Confused thinking or problems concentrating and learning
  - Extreme mood changes, including uncontrollable "highs" or euphoria, and prolonged irritability or anger
  - Avoiding friends and social activities, or difficulty understanding or relating to others
  - Changes in sleep, energy, eating habits or sex drive
  - Difficulty perceiving reality (delusions or hallucinations) and lack of insight (anosognosia)
  - Overuse of substances like alcohol or drugs
  - Multiple physical ailments without obvious causes (headaches, stomach aches, vague aches and pains)
  - Thinking about suicide, or being unable to carry out daily activities or handle daily problems and stress
  - An intense fear of weight gain or concern with appearance
- In young children, symptoms are mostly behavioral because they are still learning to identify and talk about thoughts and emotions
- NAMI signs in children: changes in school performance, excessive worry (such as fighting to avoid bed or school), hyperactive behavior, frequent nightmares, frequent disobedience or aggression, and frequent temper tantrums
- Where to get help: your health insurance, a primary care doctor, the state or county mental health authority, the NAMI HelpLine, and 988 (call, text or chat 988lifeline.org)
- Unlike diabetes or cancer, no medical test can accurately diagnose mental illness; professionals use the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), published by the American Psychiatric Association
- Treatment varies by diagnosis and by person (there is no "one size fits all") and can include medication, counseling, social support and education

## Anosognosia (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Anosognosia](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/anosognosia/)
- **Anosognosia:** Lack of insight or awareness; being unaware of one's own mental health condition or unable to perceive it accurately (from the Greek for "to not know a disease")
- Anosognosia is not the same as denial: a person with acute mental illness may not be thinking clearly enough to choose denial, and awareness can shift back and forth over time
- Updating one's self-image depends on the brain's frontal lobe, which can be damaged by schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and dementia
- Early studies found that about 30% of people with schizophrenia and 20% of people with bipolar disorder had severe lack of awareness of their diagnosis
- Lack of insight is the most common reason people stop taking their medications, and it raises the risk of homelessness or arrest
- A helpful book on anosognosia is I Am Not Sick I Don't Need Help (Xavier Amador)

## Suicide risk (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Risk of Suicide](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/risk-of-suicide/)
- According to the CDC and NIMH, nearly 46,000 lives were lost to suicide in 2020
- **Suicidal ideation:** Comments or thoughts about suicide, which may start small ("I wish I wasn't here") and become more explicit and dangerous over time
- NAMI suicide warning signs: increased alcohol and drug use, aggressive behavior, withdrawal from friends and family, dramatic mood swings, and impulsive or reckless behavior
- Suicidal behaviors are a psychiatric emergency (call 988): collecting pills or buying a weapon, giving away possessions, tying up loose ends such as organizing papers or paying debts, and saying goodbye
- 46% of people who die by suicide had a known mental health condition
- Suicide risk factors: family history of suicide, substance use, intoxication (about 1 in 5 people who die by suicide had alcohol in their system), access to firearms, serious or chronic illness, trauma or abuse, prolonged stress, and a recent loss
- More women than men attempt suicide, but men are 4 times more likely to die by suicide
- In a suicide-related crisis: talk openly (it is okay to ask "Do you have a plan?"), remove means such as guns, knives or stockpiled pills, ask simple direct questions, have one person speak at a time, and don't argue, threaten, raise your voice or debate whether suicide is right or wrong
- Active listening (reflecting feelings and summarizing thoughts) helps a person feel heard, instead of arguing with their negative statements
- CBT and DBT help people with suicidal thoughts recognize unhelpful patterns and learn coping skills; suicidal thoughts are a symptom that can be treated
- NAMI created the guide Navigating a Mental Health Crisis for people having mental health emergencies and their loved ones

## Self-harm (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Self-harm](https://www.nami.org/co-occurring-conditions/self-harm/)
- **Self-harm (self-injury):** Hurting yourself on purpose, such as cutting, burning, pulling out hair or picking at wounds
- Self-harm is not a mental illness but a behavior showing a need for better coping skills; it is associated with BPD, depression, eating disorders, anxiety and PTSD
- Self-harm is most common in the teenage and young adult years; people who experienced trauma, neglect or abuse are at most risk, and alcohol and drugs raise the risk by lowering self-control
- Self-injury can release endorphins (the body's pain-killing hormones) or let a person who feels numb feel something "real"; shame afterward can create a dangerous cycle
- Self-harm is not the same as a suicide attempt, but it signals emotional pain and can raise the risk of feeling suicidal
- Psychotherapy (psychodynamic therapy, CBT, DBT) is important in treating self-harm; a diagnostic interview with a psychiatrist may last an hour or more
- Possible signs of self-harm: frequent bruises or bandages, or wearing long sleeves and pants in hot weather
- Helping someone who self-harms: ask and listen, don't dismiss emotions or joke, gently encourage treatment, and don't make the person promise to stop, because it takes more than willpower

## Suicide and youth statistics (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Mental Health By the Numbers](https://www.nami.org/mental-health-by-the-numbers/) · [Youth Suicide](https://www.nami.org/kids-teens-and-young-adults/what-you-need-to-know-about-youth-suicide/)
- More than 1 in 20 U.S. adults (5.5%) have serious thoughts of suicide each year
- Suicide was the 10th leading cause of death overall in the U.S. in 2024
- In 2022, nearly 49,000 lives were lost to suicide in the U.S., about 1 death every 11 minutes
- 90% of people who die by suicide may have had symptoms of a mental health disorder, based on a psychological autopsy (interviews with family, friends and medical professionals)
- 79% of people who die by suicide in the U.S. are male, and the suicide rate is nearly 4 times higher among males than females
- Transgender adults are nearly 9 times more likely to attempt suicide in their lifetime than the general population
- 40% of U.S. high school students had persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in 2023 (65% of LGBTQ+ students)
- About 20% of U.S. high school students had serious thoughts of suicide in 2023 and 9% report a suicide attempt; LGBTQ+ high school students are over 3 times more likely to attempt suicide than straight, cisgender peers
- About 12% of young adults ages 18–25 had serious thoughts of suicide in the past year, and about 2% report a suicide attempt
- Suicide rates among Black youth have increased in recent years, and the Congressional Black Caucus formed the Emergency Taskforce on Black Youth Suicide and Mental Health in 2019
- Indigenous communities such as Alaska Natives have historically had higher suicide rates because of generational trauma, poverty and stigma
- States that passed same-sex marriage laws saw adolescent suicide attempts drop by 7%
- Youth suicide risk factors: prior attempts, family history of suicide, mental health conditions, substance misuse, impulsivity, family problems, breakups, access to means (unsecured firearms, medications, poisons), social isolation, trauma or bullying, lack of care, and exposure to suicide or unsafe media coverage
- Signs of escalating risk: talking, joking or posting about dying; hopelessness or feeling like a burden; extreme emotional pain; researching ways to die; "goodbye" messages or giving away possessions; and changes in substance use
- If a friend says they are thinking about self-harm, do not agree to keep it a secret; tell a trusted adult, call or text 988, or call 911 or go to an emergency room if there is immediate danger
- Only 56% of U.S. public schools say they can effectively provide mental health services to all students in need

## Crisis planning and response (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: What to Do in a Crisis](https://www.nami.org/living-with-a-mental-health-condition/what-to-do-in-a-crisis/) · [Crisis Intervention](https://www.nami.org/advocacy-at-nami/crisis-intervention/)
- If a person has developed a plan to kill themselves (thought about where, how or when), it is an immediate crisis: go to an emergency room or call 911
- When unsure, ask a friend or family member to stay with you, contact 988, and tell your mental health professionals; you don't need to be sworn to secrecy
- Planning ahead with your treatment team covers where to go for intensive treatment, time off work, and ways to calm yourself in an emergency
- **CIT (Crisis Intervention Team) officers:** Police officers trained to handle crisis situations involving mental illness; CIT programs are a community-based approach to improve outcomes, because law enforcement officers often serve as first responders to mental health crises
- **Warmline:** A phone line where trained volunteers (often peers living with a mental health condition, not crisis counselors) offer sympathy and support; dial 211 or visit 211.org to find one
- If you have no mental health professional, make an urgent appointment with a primary care physician, as you would for the flu
- A personal "toolbox" of coping mechanisms (talking to a friend, meditating, napping, walking) can help during difficult times
- The National Suicide Hotline Designation Act (2020), which NAMI advocated for, created the nationwide three-digit number 988 for mental health and suicidal crises
- NAMI calls for a continuum of crisis services in every community to end the revolving door of ER visits, arrests, incarceration and homelessness
