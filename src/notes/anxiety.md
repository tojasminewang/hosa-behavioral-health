## What is an anxiety disorder? (textbook) {p. 576}
- **Anxiety disorder:** A disorder featuring anxiety that is not proportional to a person's circumstances (unrealistic and counterproductive levels of anxiety)
- Anxiety has two major components: strong negative emotions and physical tension caused by the anticipation of danger
- Fear is a response to something happening in the present; anxiety is worry about the future
- About 30% of Americans experience one or more anxiety disorders in their lifetime
- People inherit a vulnerability for anxiety disorders in general, not for specific types; anxiety disorders and MDD share an underlying genetic basis
- Women are more likely to be diagnosed than men; African Americans and Hispanics in the U.S. are less likely to be diagnosed than White Americans
- About 20% of infants are "high reactives" to novel stimuli and still show heightened anxiety at ages 4–5
- Unlike most disorders, anxiety disorders do not impair realistic thinking: adults usually know their anxiety is unwarranted but feel unable to control it

## Specific phobias and social anxiety (textbook) {pp. 576–578}
- **Specific phobia:** Fears of objects or situations other than those associated with agoraphobia or social anxiety disorder (animals, natural phenomena, blood and injury, flying, heights, bridges)
- 21.2% of women and 10.9% of men report at least one specific phobia; women report more situational and animal phobias, but there is no gender difference in injury phobias
- Phobias may be exaggerations of useful caution and can form through classical conditioning or by observing others (a parent screaming at a spider)
- People with spider phobias believe the spider intends to attack them; people with height phobias (acrophobia) overestimate heights and rely more on vision for balance
- **Social anxiety disorder:** The fear of being scrutinized and criticized by others, especially during public speaking and meeting new people, out of proportion to the situation; usually begins in adolescence (an exaggerated "spotlight effect")
- Social anxiety is often less common in collectivistic cultures; in the U.S. it is more common among White and Native American individuals
- **Taijin kyofusho:** A Japanese culture-bound syndrome (10% to 20% of Japanese people) involving fear of offending others with one's odor, eye contact, blushing, or appearance; Morita Therapy (Morita Masatake) treats it by accepting feelings, knowing your purpose, and doing what needs doing

## Panic disorder and agoraphobia (textbook) {pp. 578–579}
- **Panic attack:** The experience of intense fear and autonomic arousal in the absence of real threat; symptoms typically last about 10 minutes (pounding heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, fear of dying or going crazy)
- **Panic disorder:** Repeated panic attacks and fear of future attacks; affects 2.7% of the population each year (one-quarter to one-third of college students report at least one panic attack in a year)
- Women experience panic more than men; more than half of people with panic disorder also have MDD or another anxiety disorder; attacks begin in adolescence and young adulthood
- Biological clue: sodium lactate injections trigger panic attacks in people with panic disorder but not others; people with panic disorder have more orexins
- Cognitive explanation: misinterpreting body symptoms (a fast heart rate as a heart attack) creates a spiral of arousal and anxiety
- Culture shapes symptoms: dizziness in China, constipation and shortness of breath in Rwanda, gastrointestinal symptoms in Thailand
- Panic disorder is more common in children who lost or were separated from a parent and in adults after stressful life events
- **Agoraphobia:** Unrealistic fear of open spaces, being outside the home alone, or being in a crowd (from the Greek agora, "marketplace"); a common outcome of panic disorder

## Generalized anxiety disorder (textbook) {p. 580}
- **Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD):** Excessive anxiety and worry for 6 months that is not correlated with particular objects or situations
- GAD is associated with headache, stomachache, and muscle tension, and is often comorbid with other anxiety disorders, substance abuse, and MDD
- The amygdala is rich in GABA receptors; alcohol and benzodiazepine tranquilizers (e.g., Valium) reduce anxiety at GABA receptors, so people may self-medicate and become vulnerable to substance use disorders
- People with GAD show more activity in the cortex, thalamus, amygdala, and hypothalamus; the prefrontal cortex "works harder" to control danger signals from the amygdala
- Cognitive explanations: a schema that "the world is a dangerous place," and worrying used as a coping strategy
- People in lower socioeconomic classes are about twice as likely to be diagnosed with GAD; divorce, separation, death of a spouse, and job loss raise risk
- The anterior cingulate cortex is highly active during worry and catastrophizing (focusing on the worst possible outcomes)

## Treating anxiety disorders (textbook) {pp. 628–629}
- Anxiety disorders (GAD, phobias, panic disorder) are typically treated with medication, CBT, or both; medication and psychological treatment produce similar improvement
- Most anxiety medications reduce brain activity by enhancing GABA, the major inhibitory neurotransmitter (alcohol, barbiturates, and benzodiazepines all act at the GABA receptor)
- Barbiturates (developed in 1903) are still useful for seizures but are no longer desirable for anxiety because they are addictive and used in suicides
- Benzodiazepines such as Librium and Valium were the most prescribed drugs of the 1970s; alprazolam (Xanax) is used for panic disorder, GAD, and premenstrual syndrome
- Antidepressants are typically more effective than tranquilizers for anxiety, possibly because depression and anxiety share an underlying cause
- **Exposure therapy:** Gradual exposure to the feared stimulus (or watching others interact with it harmlessly) while relaxing; used for phobias
- For social anxiety disorder, exposure therapy alone kept improving after treatment ended, while gains from medication deteriorated
- Virtual reality exposure is about as effective as CBT for phobias and may be better for fear of flying

## Anxiety disorders (SAMHSA)
@ [SAMHSA: Anxiety Disorder](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/what-is-mental-health/conditions/anxiety)
- Occasional anxiety is an expected part of life; anxiety disorders involve anxiety that does not go away and can get worse over time, interfering with job performance, schoolwork, and relationships
- SAMHSA lists anxiety disorders as generalized anxiety, panic disorder, specific phobias, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and social anxiety
- **GAD (SAMHSA):** A constant feeling of anxiety or fear that may last for months or even years; caused by a complex interaction of biology and environment (genetics, brain function, personality, development, and perception of threats)
- GAD symptoms: restlessness, being on edge or wound up, difficulty concentrating, irritability, being easily fatigued, headaches and muscle aches or stomachaches, trouble sleeping, difficulty controlling worry, sweating, nausea, or diarrhea
- **Panic disorder (SAMHSA):** Frequent and unexpected panic attacks (sudden periods of intense discomfort, fear, or sense of losing control even when there is no clear danger) that can occur several times a day or a few times a year; people worry about the next attack and avoid places linked to past attacks
- Panic attack symptoms: rapid heart rate or chest pain, shortness of breath, sweating, trembling or tingling, feelings of doom, feeling out of control, chills or hot flashes, nausea
- **Specific phobia (SAMHSA):** A strong, irrational fear of something that poses little or no actual danger (e.g., heights); **agoraphobia** is a fear of public places and **claustrophobia** is a fear of closed-in places
- People with phobias know their fears are irrational but feel powerless; children may have tantrums, cling to guardians, and cry
- **Social anxiety disorder (social phobia):** An overwhelming, ongoing fear of being watched and judged by others; signs include blushing, sweating, palpitations, stiff posture, an overly soft voice, and difficulty making eye contact
- Social anxiety can begin after an incredibly embarrassing social situation (learned behavior)

## Anxiety disorders (NAMI)
@ [NAMI: Anxiety Disorders](https://www.nami.org/types-of-conditions/anxiety-disorders/)
- Anxiety disorders are the most common type of mental health concern in the United States
- More than 1 in 6 U.S. adults (19.1%) have an anxiety disorder, and 1 in 9 U.S. youth ages 3–17 (11%) have problems with anxiety; most people develop symptoms before age 21
- Everyday anxiety can be useful (it motivates us to prepare and keeps us alert), but an anxiety disorder involves fear and distress so overwhelming that they block everyday activities
- What all anxiety disorders have in common: persistent, excessive fear or worry in situations that are not threatening
- Causes of anxiety disorders: genetics (they run in families) and environment (stressful or traumatic events such as abuse, the death of a loved one, violence or prolonged illness)
- Emotional symptoms: apprehension or dread, feeling tense or jumpy, restlessness or irritability, anticipating the worst
- Physical symptoms: pounding heart and shortness of breath, sweating, tremors, headaches, fatigue, insomnia, nausea and diarrhea
- **Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD):** Chronic, exaggerated worry about everyday life that can consume hours each day
- **Social anxiety disorder:** More than shyness; intense fear of social interaction driven by irrational worries about humiliation
- **Panic disorder:** Repeated panic attacks and sudden feelings of terror that can strike without warning; a panic attack is often mistaken for a heart attack
- **Phobia:** Strong, irrational fear of certain places, events or objects; most people with specific phobias have several triggers they go to great lengths to avoid
- Other anxiety disorders: agoraphobia, selective mutism, separation anxiety disorder, and substance/medication-induced anxiety disorder
- Physical symptoms of anxiety can be confused with heart disease, thyroid problems or asthma, so a provider may first do a physical exam and lab tests to rule out medical causes
- Conditions that often occur with anxiety disorders: major depressive disorder, substance use disorders, ADHD, eating disorders and sleep disorders
- Common anxiety treatments: psychotherapy such as CBT, medications (anti-anxiety medications and antidepressants), and stress-management and relaxation strategies; therapy plus medication is often most effective
- CBT is one of the most common and well-researched treatments for anxiety, and CBT delivered by telehealth can work as well as face-to-face therapy
- **Exposure response prevention:** A therapy for phobias and social anxiety in which a person faces the feared thing so that anxiety drops over time
- **Benzodiazepines:** Anti-anxiety medications that give short-term relief but are recommended only for limited use because long-term use can lead to dependence
- Antidepressants are often a first-line treatment for anxiety disorders, even when a person is not depressed
- Lifestyle strategies for anxiety: learning your triggers, setting aside a scheduled "worry time," breathing exercises and progressive muscle relaxation, yoga and regular aerobic exercise
- Caffeine (including energy drinks) and cigarettes can worsen anxiety; drugs and alcohol may seem to help in the moment but disrupt sleep and mood
- Helping a loved one with anxiety: learn their triggers, stay calm, listen without judgment, and avoid minimizing their worries or giving immediate reassurance
